# Selective production and sampling — using partial-disclosure verification

> **What this doc is.** Informative companion to design 07 §10. The verifier mode that authenticates a subset of entries against a sealed daily Merkle root has three named consumer contexts: federal selective production under 18 USC §2703(d), SOC 2 Type II statistical sampling, and DORA Article 11-14 incident-reporting tiers. This document explains how the institution's custodian assembles a partial-disclosure bundle for each context and what the consumer does with it on the receiving end.

The mode itself is specified in design 07 §10. The exit-code contract is in spec §10.12. The audit-path shape is the RFC 6962 §2.1.1 audit path with directional bits, with the right-promote rule pinned in test-vector 016. Read those for the mechanism. This document covers the procedure each consumer expects and the byte counts each context produces.

## 1. Why a partial mode exists

Full-day disclosure is the default and the only mode that asserts chain completeness. It is also wrong for three common consumer contexts. Each context asks a narrower question than "is the day intact" and over-producing the day creates legal, evidentiary, or operational exposure. The partial mode answers the narrower question without producing the rest of the day.

The mode does one thing: it authenticates a subset of entries against a sealed daily Merkle root. An entry that verifies under partial mode was sealed in the named tenant-day by the seal-key the registry attests to. That is the entire claim. The mode declines to assert that the receiver disclosed everything responsive, that the day's chain is otherwise intact, or that no other entries exist for the day.

The consumer who needs completeness runs the full-day verifier (design 07 §3) over a complete ledger snapshot. The partial mode is the right tool when completeness is not the question. The output banner names the limit verbatim — `This report does NOT attest chain completeness for tenant_day {D}` — so a consumer cannot mistake an inclusion result for a completeness result.

A property worth stating at the top: the partial-disclosure mode is not a privacy-enhancement layer over the full-day mode. The full-day mode does not leak information any institution should be uncomfortable with — verifier output names entry counts, run counts, and sealing metadata, not entry bodies. The partial mode is a scope-narrowing layer the consumer requests when the consumer's question is narrow. The institution's privacy posture is unchanged by the choice of mode; the consumer's evidence-shape is what changes.

## 2. 18 USC §2703(d) selective production

A 2703(d) order is narrower than a Rule 41 search warrant. The order authorizes the government to compel a service provider to disclose specific records that are "relevant and material to an ongoing investigation," on a showing of specific and articulable facts. Probable cause is not required and the disclosure is bounded to what the order names. The institution receiving a 2703(d) order is required to produce only what the order authorizes — disclosing more exposes the institution to a Bivens-style claim from non-target customers and gives defense counsel a Fourth Amendment hook the government does not want to litigate.

### 2.1 What the receiver produces

The receiver's custodian assembles a 2703(d) bundle containing only the responsive entries plus inclusion proofs:

- The entries the order names. Each entry's canonical bytes per spec §5.
- One RFC 6962 §2.1.1 audit path per entry.
- The seal record for each `(tenant_id, tenant_day)` the responsive entries fall within. The seal is signed by the institution's HSM and named in the institution's published public-key registry.
- The disclosure manifest naming the 2703(d) order number, the responsive `(tenant_id, tenant_day, seq)` triples, and the SHA-256 of the entries-and-proofs payload.
- A 902(13) self-authenticating declaration certifying the records under 28 USC §1746 penalty of perjury, naming the verifier binary by SHA-256 hash, naming the public-key fingerprint, and asserting the records were generated by an electronic process per FRE 902(13).

The bodies of unrelated entries do not appear anywhere in the bundle. The audit path's siblings are leaf hashes — 32-byte SHA-256 outputs — not the entries themselves. SHA-256 preimage resistance protects the bodies. The institution can publish the audit path freely without leaking the contents of any entry the order did not name.

### 2.2 What the government does on receipt

The federal investigator opens the bundle and runs the partial-disclosure verifier with the public-key registry snapshot the regulator-side or DOJ-side trust anchor team maintains independently of the institution. The verifier produces a per-entry verdict and an aggregated summary. Each disclosed entry is independently authenticated as a member of the sealed daily set. The summary names every entry's verdict separately.

The 902(13) declaration carries the records into evidence without live custodian foundation testimony. The declaration recites the verifier binary's SHA-256, the SLSA attestation, the regular-course-of-business attestation under FRE 803(6), the integrity hash of the bundle, and the public-key fingerprint, signed under penalty of perjury. The AUSA introduces the bundle and the declaration; the records authenticate themselves under FRE 902(13).

### 2.3 Why over-production is the wrong shape

A receiver responding to a 2703(d) order with a full daily seal — the entire day's events plus the seal — has produced records the order did not authorize. Even if the institution intends only good-faith over-production for procedural simplicity, the over-produced records become discoverable in the criminal case and the institution loses the ability to assert a Stored Communications Act minimization defense if a non-target customer sues. The partial-disclosure mode lets the institution honor the order's bounds without sacrificing the cryptographic authentication path.

### 2.4 Worked byte count

A typical day for a mid-size institution might carry around 50 entries for a single customer's interaction with the AI-augmented decisioning system. A 2703(d) order naming a 6-entry responsive subset produces:

- 6 disclosed entries × 32-byte leaf hash = 192 bytes of leaf material.
- Audit paths: a 50-leaf tree has depth 6 (`ceil(log2(50)) = 6`). Each audit path is at most 6 sibling hashes × 32 bytes = 192 bytes per entry. Six entries × 192 bytes = 1152 bytes of audit-path material in the worst case.
- Seal record: roughly 800 bytes (canonical JSON form with merkle_root, signature, hkdf_inputs_digest, tenant binding, cadence, dev_mode, and the v1.0a sign_payload metadata).
- Manifest plus 902(13) declaration: roughly 2-3 KB.
- The 6 disclosed entries' canonical bytes: depends on the entry size. A typical AI-decision entry in the FFIEC corpus is on the order of 1.5-2 KB; six entries adds roughly 10-12 KB.

Total bundle size is on the order of 15 KB. The full daily seal in contrast — all 50 entries plus chain-walk metadata — runs roughly 100-120 KB. The partial-disclosure bundle is roughly an order of magnitude smaller and contains zero bytes of unrelated customer data.

The size advantage is not the point. The legal-shape advantage is the point. A 15 KB partial-disclosure bundle fits the 2703(d) authorization; a 100 KB full-day seal does not.

## 3. SOC 2 Type II sampling

SOC 2 Type II testing is sampling-driven. The auditor draws a stratified statistical sample from the population of events under audit during the engagement period, tests each sampled item against the criterion, and projects the results to the population. The AICPA Audit Sampling Guide and AT-C §205 ¶.27 govern the population definition and the sample-size determination. The audit firm's National Office independence team reviews the testing approach for adequacy.

A 12-month Type II engagement against a tenant flowing tens of millions of entries cannot test every entry. A statistical sample of 60-90 entries — sized per the population, the period, and the assessed risk — is the practitioner's instrument. Each sampled entry must be independently authenticatable. Re-running the full-day verifier against 90 different daily seals to authenticate 90 sample items is wasteful and produces working papers that document irrelevant entries; the partial-disclosure mode is the right shape.

### 3.1 What the institution produces

The auditor's sampling plan specifies the sample frame and draws the sample. The institution's custodian responds with a partial-disclosure bundle per seal-day touched by the sample:

- The sampled entries' canonical bytes — typically 1-3 entries per seal-day, sometimes more.
- One audit path per sampled entry.
- The seal record for each affected seal-day, verbatim.
- A sampling manifest naming the SOC 2 engagement, the sample identifier, the population definition, and the sample-frame parameters (period coverage, stratification dimensions, sample-size justification).

The institution does not need to produce non-sampled entries. The audit firm's population definition declared at engagement scoping — typically `(tenant_id, period_start, period_end, event_type_filter)` — is what the auditor projects results to; non-sampled entries are not in scope of the test work.

### 3.2 What the auditor does on receipt

The auditor runs the partial-disclosure verifier with the public-key registry snapshot the audit firm holds. The firm's snapshot is an independence safeguard: the auditor does not rely on the institution's claim that "this is the right seal-key"; the firm's National Office maintains a separate snapshot the engagement team uses without going through the institution's controls. Each sampled entry is authenticated as a member of its sealed daily set.

The verifier output feeds the audit firm's working-paper bundle: per-entry pass/fail, the seal-day in question, the public-key fingerprint, and the audit path's length. Working-paper retention discipline applies; the bundle is sealed in the engagement file with the standard SOC 2 evidence metadata. The auditor's testing conclusion documents that the sampled entries authenticated against the sealed roots; the sampling plan's projection produces the conclusion at the population level.

### 3.3 Why a project-shipped verifier is acceptable here

The Big-4 firms maintain their own independence position on project-shipped tools. The project-shipped verifier is the same binary the institution and the regulator use; the firm's posture is to validate the binary independently — cosign signature plus reproducible-build verification per design 07 §8 — and document the validation in the engagement file. The firm's National Office reviews the validation evidence. The firm-side snapshot of the public-key registry is the firm's independent anchor; the binary's integrity is established by validation rather than by trust-by-publisher.

A firm whose National Office requires a clean-room re-implementation can build one from the byte-level normative procedure in design 07 §10.6 and run it alongside the project-shipped verifier as a cross-check. The verifier procedure is small and the cryptographic primitives are NIST-named; clean-room implementation is a few weeks' work for a firm-internal tooling team.

### 3.4 Worked byte count

A 12-month engagement period at 100M entries per period, sampled at 90 items stratified across 12 months, draws roughly 7-8 items per month. The institution produces 90 partial-disclosure bundles spanning roughly 70-80 distinct seal-days (some seal-days carry multiple sampled items). The total bundle volume is roughly 90 entries × 2 KB plus 90 audit paths × 192 bytes plus 70 seal records × 800 bytes:

- Sampled entries: 90 × ~2 KB = ~180 KB.
- Audit paths: 90 × ~192 bytes = ~17 KB.
- Seal records: 70 × ~800 bytes = ~56 KB.
- Manifest, declaration, working-paper metadata: ~5-10 KB.

Total testing bundle: roughly 270 KB across the entire engagement. The full ledger snapshot for a 12-month period at 100M entries is hundreds of GB. The partial-disclosure approach reduces the auditor's working-paper retention burden by roughly six orders of magnitude without weakening any test-work conclusion — the cryptographic strength of an inclusion proof is the same as the cryptographic strength of the full-day chain walk for the entries the proof covers.

## 4. DORA Article 11-14 incident-reporting tiers

DORA Articles 11-14 and the related Commission Delegated Regulation 2024/1772 (RTS on incident classification) and Implementing Regulation 2024/2956 (templates) require EU financial entities to report major ICT-related incidents in three tiers:

- **Initial notification** — within 4 hours of major-incident classification. The institution names the categorisation criteria met and produces the responsive evidence at hand.
- **Intermediate notification** — within 72 hours of detection. The institution produces the intermediate-evidence picture — what is known, what is being investigated, what response is in flight.
- **Final notification** — within 1 month. The institution produces the full incident dossier.

The 4-hour clock is the binding one. An institution that misses the 4-hour clock because it could not assemble the responsive evidence in time has a supervisory finding regardless of the incident's eventual disposition. The partial-disclosure mode is the institution's instrument for the 4-hour window — the chain-derived evidence the institution can produce within the clock is a small responsive set, not the full daily seal.

### 4.1 The 4-hour initial-tier filing

At 4 hours, the institution typically knows:

- The tenant or tenants affected.
- The approximate seal-day or seal-day range the incident touches.
- A small set of chain entries directly responsive to the categorisation criteria — for example, the AI-decision entries for the customer or transaction class involved in the incident.

The institution's incident commander asks the chain custodian for a partial-disclosure bundle covering the directly responsive entries. The bundle is small, fast to produce, and sufficient for the initial notification's "categorisation criteria met" question. The custodian returns:

- The responsive entries (typically 5-20 entries at the 4-hour mark).
- One audit path per responsive entry.
- The seal records for each affected seal-day.
- The disclosure manifest naming the incident reference, the tenant, the seal-day range, and the responsive seq values.

The supervisor opens the bundle, runs the partial-disclosure verifier, and confirms the disclosed entries are authentic members of the sealed daily set. The 4-hour notification's evidence section is closed; the substantive incident analysis continues in parallel.

### 4.2 The 72-hour intermediate-tier filing

At 72 hours, the responsive set has grown. The investigation has typically identified:

- Adjacent entries — the entries before and after each directly responsive entry, which carry context the supervisor needs to assess scope.
- Entries on adjacent days — incidents rarely respect daily boundaries.
- Operational events — `master.*` events for HSM activity, sealing delays, late-binding rates during the window.

The custodian assembles a larger partial-disclosure bundle. The mode handles the larger size identically to the 4-hour bundle; nothing about the verification procedure changes. The bundle now spans more seal-days and produces more entries, but each entry independently authenticates against its sealed root.

A 72-hour bundle for a moderate incident might run 100-300 entries across 3-5 seal-days. Bundle size on the order of 500 KB to 1 MB. Verification time on commodity hardware is sub-second.

### 4.3 The 1-month final-tier filing

At 1 month, the institution may produce either a partial-disclosure bundle covering the full responsive set or a full-day verifier output for the affected seal-days, depending on the supervisor's question. If the supervisor wants completeness — "did anything else happen on those days that you have not told us about" — the full-day mode is the correct shape and the institution produces a complete ledger snapshot for the affected days. If the supervisor wants depth on the responsive set — "show me the full causal chain for the incident" — the partial-disclosure mode remains correct and the bundle carries every entry the institution's investigation identified as responsive plus the operational events that frame them.

Most final-tier filings combine both. The institution produces full-day verifier output for the affected seal-days plus a partial-disclosure bundle covering the responsive set. The supervisor sees both views: the day was intact, and here are the entries that mattered.

### 4.4 Why this works for DORA

DORA Article 18(1) requires the initial notification within 4 hours. The mode lets the institution honor the clock with cryptographic evidence rather than narrative-only prose. A 4-hour filing that says "we believe the chain is intact and we will provide evidence in the 72-hour notification" is weaker than a 4-hour filing that ships authenticated chain entries with the initial notification. The mode produces the latter shape.

A second DORA-specific property: the partial-disclosure bundle does not leak unrelated tenant data. A 4-hour filing for a single-tenant incident includes only that tenant's responsive entries; the institution's other tenants are not represented in the bundle in any form. This matters under DORA Article 28 ICT third-party arrangements and EBA/GL/2019/02 §13.2 audit-rights — the supervisor inspects what the order or notification authorizes, not the institution's broader operations.

## 5. Bundle authoring on the receiver side

The receiver's chain custodian uses the institution's verifier-side tooling to author the bundle. The flow on the institution's side is:

1. Identify the responsive `(tenant_id, tenant_day, seq)` triples — the disclosure scope.
2. For each triple, fetch the entry's canonical bytes from the ledger.
3. For each triple, compute the audit path against the day's sealed Merkle tree. Test-vector 016's right-promote rule applies whenever the day's leaf count is not a power of 2. The streaming-Merkle implementation that produced the seal can produce audit paths with no additional cryptographic work — it is the same tree.
4. Fetch the seal records for each affected `(tenant_id, tenant_day)`. The seal record is read-only; the custodian copies it verbatim into the bundle without modification.
5. Author the disclosure manifest. Name the disclosure basis verbatim — the 2703(d) order number, the SOC 2 sample identifier, or the DORA incident reference. The manifest's basis field is what step P5 (under `--strict` per design 07 §10.9 exit-3 case) checks against.
6. Compute the bundle's payload SHA-256 and include it in the manifest.
7. For 2703(d) bundles, append the 902(13) declaration signed under 28 USC §1746 penalty of perjury.

The custodian role is the same role that handles full-day examination requests under spec §10.13. The partial-disclosure path is one more output shape rather than a separate role; the institution's custodian discipline applies identically.

## 6. Limits the consumer must accept

The mode is honest about what it does not assert. A consumer relying on a partial-disclosure bundle accepts:

- The bundle covers the entries the receiver chose to disclose. It does not cover entries the receiver omitted. A 2703(d) order's investigator assesses completeness through the order's specificity and through the receiver's compliance certification, not through the cryptography. A SOC 2 auditor assesses completeness through the population definition and the sampling plan, not through any per-entry artifact. A DORA supervisor assesses completeness through the institution's incident-investigation discipline and the final-tier filing.
- The chain's full per-entry MAC walk is not recomputed for the disclosed entries. The disclosed entries' previous and next entries are not provided — the chain walk in design 07 §4.1 (steps 4-9) cannot run. The partial mode authenticates inclusion in the sealed set; the chain walk authenticates the per-entry MAC. The full-day mode runs both. The partial mode runs the inclusion check.
- The day's overall integrity is not asserted. A consumer wanting day-level integrity runs the full-day mode against the full ledger snapshot.

These limits are not weaknesses of the cryptography. They are deliberate scope-narrowing choices that match the legal, evidentiary, and operational shape of each consumer context. The full-day mode and the partial mode coexist; the consumer picks the one that matches the question being asked.

## 7. End-to-end worked example — single-day partial disclosure

A concrete walk-through, deliberately small enough to inspect by hand. The institution's tenant `tenant-acme-prod` produced 50 chain entries on 2026-04-15. A federal investigator serves a 2703(d) order naming 6 specific entries by `(run_id, seq)` triples. The institution responds.

**Hour 0 — order received.** The institution's chain custodian receives the order. The order names six triples spanning two run identifiers — three from `r_a3f29b71c` (seq 142, 187, 213) and three from `r_b8c41d92e` (seq 401, 502, 617). All six entries fall within the 2026-04-15 seal-day.

**Hour 1 — entry retrieval.** The custodian queries the ledger for the six entries' canonical bytes. Each query returns the exact bytes the SDK serialized at capture time per spec §5. Total disclosed payload at this stage: roughly 12 KB across the six entries.

**Hour 1 — audit path computation.** The custodian invokes the institution's seal-side tooling to produce one audit path per entry against the 2026-04-15 sealed Merkle root. The tree has 50 leaves, depth 6 (`ceil(log2(50)) = 6`). Each path is at most 6 sibling hashes plus directional bits. Two of the six entries (seq 213 and 617) sit near the right edge of the tree and their paths include a `"promote"` step — the right-promote rule fires for them and the audit-path encoder records `position = "promote"` at the relevant level. The other four paths are clean six-step walks.

**Hour 1 — seal record retrieval.** The custodian copies the seal record for `(tenant-acme-prod, 2026-04-15)` verbatim into the bundle. The seal record is roughly 800 bytes and is read-only — any modification breaks the HSM signature.

**Hour 1.5 — manifest authoring.** The custodian writes the disclosure manifest naming the 2703(d) order number, the six responsive triples, and the SHA-256 of the entries-and-proofs payload. The 902(13) declaration is drafted by the institution's general counsel from the regulator-pack template, signed under 28 USC §1746.

**Hour 2 — bundle dispatch.** Total bundle size: roughly 15 KB plus the declaration. The bundle is transmitted to the federal investigator through the channel the order specifies.

**Hour 2.1 — investigator-side verification.** The investigator opens the bundle on a forensic workstation. Their public-key registry snapshot was retrieved earlier from the regulator-side trust anchor. They invoke `verifier verify-partial` against the bundle. The verifier validates the seal signature once (step P4), then walks each of the six audit paths in sequence (steps P1, P2, P3, P5 per entry). Six entries pass. The output banner reads `This report does NOT attest chain completeness for tenant_day 2026-04-15`. The investigator's working paper records the verifier output, the bundle SHA-256, and the verifier binary's SHA-256.

**Trial preparation, weeks later.** The 902(13) declaration carries the records into evidence without live custodian foundation testimony. The AUSA introduces the bundle and the declaration; the records authenticate themselves under FRE 902(13). The investigator testifies as the records' producer; the institution's IT custodian is not subpoenaed because the declaration substitutes for live foundation testimony.

The investigator never sees the other 44 entries from 2026-04-15. The institution never produces them. The order's authorization bound holds.

## 8. Mode comparison — when to pick which

Three modes coexist for the v1 verifier. The right one depends on the question being asked.

| Mode | Asserts | Inputs | Output shape | Right when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full-day verify | Chain integrity end-to-end plus the day's seal binding | Full ledger snapshot, public-key registry | Per-day pass or fail; PDF + JSON | Examination of a complete period; the consumer wants completeness. |
| Witness-mode verify | Structural integrity without HMAC | Full ledger snapshot minus IKM, public-key registry | PASS-STRUCTURALLY; per-day with key-bound steps skipped | Examiner cannot receive the IKM but wants structural integrity. |
| Partial-disclosure verify | Inclusion of named entries in a sealed root | Disclosed entries plus audit paths plus seal record, public-key registry | Per-entry pass or fail; completeness NOT-ASSERTED | The consumer is asking about specific entries, not the whole day. |

A consumer who picks the wrong mode produces the wrong shape of evidence. A 2703(d) order's investigator running the full-day mode receives the entire day's bytes and now has a Stored Communications Act minimization problem. A SOC 2 auditor running the partial mode against a sample receives per-entry inclusion verdicts and has the right shape. A DORA supervisor running the full-day mode at the 4-hour clock has nothing to file because the institution cannot assemble the full ledger snapshot inside 4 hours; the partial mode is the only path that meets the clock.

Picking the right mode is a procedural choice the consumer makes before the verifier ever runs. The institution's custodian responds in the shape the consumer requests; the verifier executes the chosen mode and emits the matching output.

## 9. The disclosing-party trust posture

A partial-disclosure bundle reaches the verifier through the disclosing party. The bundle is therefore untrusted at the byte level — any byte the disclosing party authored could be wrong. The mode's security rests on three independent properties holding simultaneously:

1. **The seal record is HSM-signed.** The disclosing party cannot forge a seal record; they can only present one the institution's HSM previously signed. An attempt to ship a fabricated seal fails step P4 against the registry-anchored public key.
2. **The audit path's siblings are commitments to entries that were also in the seal.** An audit path leading to a sealed root cannot be constructed for an entry that was not in the leaf set. SHA-256 second-preimage resistance closes the forge-an-inclusion-proof attack at approximately 2^254 operations.
3. **The verifier-principal holds the public-key registry snapshot independently.** The disclosing party does not control which key the verifier validates against. An attempt to substitute a key the disclosing party controls fails because the snapshot is held outside the disclosing party's reach.

Each of the three properties is independently necessary. A consumer that skips the registry snapshot and validates the seal signature against a key the disclosing party provides is no longer running the partial-disclosure mode — they are running a useless variant where the disclosing party can swap the key. A consumer that accepts seal records the institution has not actually sealed (a fabricated seal) loses the seal-signature anchor and again cannot rely on the per-entry inclusion verdict.

The verifier's `--strict` mode in the partial path adds two further checks the consumer SHOULD enable for high-stakes contexts:

- The disclosure manifest's `basis` field MUST be non-empty. A bundle without a stated basis (the 2703(d) order number, the SOC 2 sample identifier, the DORA incident reference) is rejected at exit code 3.
- The registry snapshot's freshness MUST cover `tenant_day`. A snapshot taken before the seal-day cannot anchor the seal's signature because the signing key may have rotated between the snapshot date and the seal-day; the verifier rejects with `registry snapshot pre-dates tenant_day` at exit code 3.

The two checks are not load-bearing for cryptographic correctness; they are deployment-discipline checks the consumer activates when they want the bundle's procedural provenance documented in the output.

## 10. Frequently asked questions

**Does the partial mode require a different verifier binary?** No. The same verifier binary supports both modes through different subcommands (`verify` for the full-day mode, `verify-partial` for the partial mode). The cosign signature, the reproducible-build evidence, and the SHA-256 the consumer records in their working papers are the same artifact across both modes.

**Can the institution run the partial mode internally for self-verification?** Yes. The institution's chain custodian can produce a partial bundle and run the verifier against it as a self-check before disclosure. The institution's run validates the bundle's structure and the inclusion-proof correctness; the consumer's run later validates the same bundle against the consumer's independently held public-key registry snapshot. Two PASS results from two independent registry snapshots is stronger evidence than either one alone.

**What if an entry was sealed but the canonical bytes are no longer retrievable?** The institution's retention discipline per spec §10.13 covers this case. If the canonical bytes are unrecoverable, the institution cannot disclose the entry under the partial mode — step P1 cannot run because there are no bytes to recompute the leaf hash from. The institution documents the loss in its incident log and notifies the requesting party that the entry is unrecoverable. The audit path alone cannot substitute for the canonical bytes; the partial mode authenticates inclusion of the bytes the institution discloses, not inclusion of an entry the institution names by seq alone.

**Can a consumer re-derive the audit path themselves?** Only with the full ledger snapshot. An audit path requires knowing the leaf hashes of the disclosed entry's siblings, which means knowing those siblings' canonical bytes. A consumer who already has the full snapshot does not need the partial mode — they would run the full-day mode. The partial mode's value is precisely that the consumer does not have the full snapshot; the institution computes the audit path on the consumer's behalf and the consumer validates it against the seal.

**What if two disclosed entries are right-promoted at different levels of the same tree?** Each entry's audit path is independent. An audit path may carry a `"promote"` step at any level where the entry was right-promoted; another entry's path against the same tree may carry zero or more `"promote"` steps independently. The verifier walks each path in isolation. Test-vector 017 covers a path with no `"promote"` step (the index-3 entry); a v1.x extension of the test corpus covering the index-6 entry would exercise the `"promote"` step end-to-end.

**Does the partial mode work with the witness-verifier mode?** They are orthogonal but not commonly composed. The witness-verifier mode is for examiners running the full-day mode without the IKM — it skips the per-entry MAC checks. The partial-disclosure mode does not run per-entry MAC checks at all (the disclosed entries' previous and next entries are not provided), so the IKM is irrelevant. A consumer running the partial mode never needs the IKM. A consumer running both modes — the full-day witness mode for completeness, the partial mode for specific entries — runs them as two separate verifier invocations against the same ledger snapshot and the same registry snapshot.

**How does the partial mode interact with key rotation mid-day?** A tenant-day spanning a key rotation has entries under multiple key_versions. Each entry's leaf hash depends only on the entry's canonical bytes — the leaf hash construction is `SHA-256(0x00 || entry_canonical_bytes)`, which does not include the IKM or the session key. A rotation mid-day does not affect audit-path correctness for any entry; the seal record's signature covers the day's full Merkle root regardless of which key_version each entry was MAC'd under. The partial mode is therefore rotation-agnostic. Test-vector 010 covers the rotation case for the full-day mode; the partial mode's correctness over a rotation-touching tenant-day is implied by the leaf-hash construction's independence from the session key.

**What happens if the registry snapshot is stale?** Under `--strict`, the verifier rejects with exit code 3 and reason `registry snapshot pre-dates tenant_day`. Without `--strict`, the verifier proceeds but emits a warning and the per-entry output records `registry_anchor_freshness: "stale"`. The consumer decides whether to accept the bundle on a stale anchor; the recommended posture for high-stakes contexts is `--strict`.

## 12. Adversarial-balance section — symmetric production and the path to full disclosure

Sections 1 through 11 describe the partial-disclosure mode as an institution-side or examiner-side mechanism. The same mode produces evidence consumed by parties adverse to the institution: a class-action plaintiff, a consumer's lawyer, a national consumer-protection authority. Those consumers see the partial-disclosure bundle differently — not as a privacy-preserving narrowing of scope, but as a potentially under-inclusive production the institution has shaped to its own advantage. This section names the symmetry rules that prevent the partial mode from being used to gate access to evidence the adverse party is entitled to demand.

### 12.1 The symmetric-production rule

When the institution produces a partial-disclosure bundle to an adverse party in litigation, regulatory inquiry, or consumer dispute, the institution's bundle scope MUST be set by the adverse party's request — not by the institution's preference for narrowness. The institution responds in the shape the adverse party requested:

- The adverse party names the responsive `(tenant_id, run_id, seq)` triples (or the population from which a sample should be drawn).
- The institution produces every named or sampled entry plus the audit paths and seal records covering them.
- The institution does NOT silently substitute a narrower scope (a tighter sampling ratio, a shorter date range, fewer named class members) than the adverse party requested.
- When the institution proposes a narrower scope on proportionality grounds (per FRCP 26(b)(1) or the analogous foreign-procedural rule), the institution surfaces the proposed narrowing in writing for the adverse party to accept, contest, or escalate to the court.

The symmetric rule prevents the partial mode from becoming a one-sided shape where the institution's evidence-shaping interest dictates what the adverse party sees. The rule is itself the institution's defense — a documented policy of producing the requested shape protects the institution against later challenges that the production was strategically narrowed.

### 12.2 The consumer-side path to demand full disclosure

A consumer or a consumer's lawyer demanding full chain disclosure (the full daily seal for the affected period, not the partial-disclosure bundle) has three procedural paths:

1. **GDPR Article 15(3) "copy of personal data undergoing processing."** A data subject's first-copy request under Article 15 entitles the data subject to the full set of chain entries concerning their personal data — the captured JSON, the canonical bytes, the seal records, the public-key resolution, and the verifier-run output for the period (per `docs/customer-dispute-procedures.md` §"EU consumer-rights extensions"). This is full disclosure of the data subject's data, not a partial-disclosure bundle. The institution's first-copy response is free of charge per CJEU FT (C-307/22).

2. **FRCP 34 production with a "rule of completeness" demand.** Under FRE 106 a party that introduces a chain entry must, on request, produce "any other part… that in fairness should be considered." A plaintiff who receives a partial-disclosure bundle and identifies a context-completeness gap (e.g., the bundle includes the AI's decision but not the chained `audit.fact_verification.*` cross-check) can demand the full chain extract for the relevant runs. The institution's response is to produce the chained children — full extract for the named runs — under proportional discovery.

3. **Court-ordered full-day production.** When the adverse party can articulate a basis for full-day disclosure beyond named entries (e.g., a representative-action expert needs the full population to verify the institution's sampling policy was not biased toward favorable entries), the adverse party moves the court for full-day production. The institution's response is to assert any proportionality objection per §1.2 of this document, but does not contest the court's order on cryptographic grounds — the chain is designed to support full-day production when ordered.

The institution's CC8.1 control description names the institution's standard response posture under each of the three paths. The consumer or the consumer's lawyer presenting one of the three paths receives a documented response within the regulatory or procedural window.

### 12.3 Court-ordered full-disclosure trigger

A court ordering full-day disclosure (rather than a partial bundle) does so when:

- The adverse party has demonstrated a basis for completeness verification beyond named entries.
- The institution's proportionality objection has been overruled, narrowed, or accepted with full disclosure as a condition.
- The court has determined the adverse party's evidentiary needs cannot be met by the partial mode.

When the trigger fires, the institution responds with the full-day verifier output (per design 07 §3 and `docs/litigation-support.md` §3) covering the ordered period. The full-day response includes:

- The complete ledger snapshot for the ordered tenant-day(s).
- The verifier-run output across the full period (`Status: PASS` or the documented anomaly disposition).
- The §A FRE 902(13) declaration covering the full snapshot.
- The §D adversarial-balance addendum (per `docs/templates/fre-902-certification.md` §D) when the production is to an adverse party.

The institution does NOT use the partial mode to defeat a full-disclosure order. The partial mode is a scope-narrowing tool the consumer or examiner requests; once the court has ordered full disclosure, the partial mode no longer applies. The institution's records custodian's deposition prep includes the answer to "why did you produce only a sample?" — the answer is "the adverse party requested a sample under the partial mode; if the court orders full disclosure, we will produce it."

### 12.4 Gaming-the-system safeguards

A risk the partial mode introduces: an institution facing a narrow discovery demand might be tempted to construct a sample frame that excludes inconvenient entries. Three structural safeguards close this:

1. **The audit path's siblings are commitments to the full leaf set.** A partial-disclosure bundle's audit path proves the disclosed entries were members of the sealed root. The audit path also implicitly proves how many leaves the day contained — the path's depth equals `ceil(log2(leaf_count))`. An adverse party comparing the disclosed leaf count to the path depth can detect when the institution claims a smaller day than the path shape implies.

2. **The seal record carries the leaf count explicitly.** The seal record's canonical form names `leaf_count` (the number of entries in the day's tree). The adverse party comparing `leaf_count` against the disclosed entries' count surfaces any discrepancy. An institution that disclosed 6 entries and named `leaf_count = 50` has produced an 88% under-inclusive bundle by the institution's own seal record's testimony — discoverable on the adverse party's review of the bundle.

3. **The disclosure manifest's `basis` field is non-empty under `--strict`.** The institution names the basis (the discovery order's number, the data subject's Article 15 request reference, the representative-action court order). A bundle without a documented basis fails the verifier under `--strict` at exit code 3. The basis is itself discoverable; the adverse party's expert can verify the institution's stated basis aligns with the actual order.

The three safeguards compose. An institution gaming the partial mode by under-inclusion produces a bundle that is internally inconsistent (count mismatch), unsupported (no basis), or strikes an audit-path discrepancy. The verifier surfaces all three; the adverse party's expert reads them.
