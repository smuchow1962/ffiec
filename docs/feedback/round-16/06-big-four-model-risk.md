## Reviewer's frame of reference

I read this corpus the way I read any control description that lands on my desk: I am asked, by the bank's MRM committee or by an SR 11-7 examiner across the table, *what does this control assert, what would I have to test to confirm it asserts what it claims, and where does the control description leak responsibility back to the bank in ways that the bank's MRM program is or is not equipped to absorb*. I do not opine on whether the AI agent's decisions are good decisions — that is the institution's MRM committee's substantive work. I opine on whether the evidence layer this corpus describes is the right shape for an SR 11-7 program to consume.

I have spent the last decade watching banks try to retrofit logging integrity into AI programs that were stood up under speed-to-production pressure with vendor-provided logs as the bank's only evidence. The gap this corpus closes — the bank's evidence layer separated from the vendor's evidence layer — is the gap I have personally watched institutions struggle to articulate to the OCC. The corpus is correct that the chain provides integrity, not validity. That distinction is doing real work in the MRM-committee brief and in the customer-dispute procedures, and the institutions I work with will internalise it on first read.

What I am scoring against, in priority order: (1) does the corpus give the MRM committee the substrate to discharge its SR 11-7 oversight duty without forcing the committee into cryptographic detail it should not need, (2) does the corpus avoid leaking implementation responsibility back onto the institution in ways that exceed what an SR 11-7 program can reasonably absorb, (3) does the corpus compose with the SR 11-7 lifecycle (validation, ongoing monitoring, retirement) and with the U.S. Treasury AI RMF and EU AI Act Article 14 / Article 26 the way the institution will need to articulate to its regulators.

## Headline finding

**Pass with no blocker, no major.** The corpus is at the level of polish I expect from a final-round MRM artifact, and the load-bearing decisions for SR 11-7 alignment have been made deliberately and documented in a form the institution's MRM committee can adopt without translation work.

The two artifacts that carry the most MRM weight — `MRM-COMMITTEE-BRIEF.md` and `customer-dispute-procedures.md` — do the thing I most often find missing from AI control descriptions: they tell the committee what the chain *does not do* in the same paragraph that tells the committee what the chain *does do*. The chain produces integrity, not validity. The chain does not generate the adverse-action notice; it provides the evidence that supports the notice's reasons. The chain does not generate the reproduction; the institution's MRM team performs the reproduction, and the chain integrity-binds the inputs that the reproduction needs. Each of those scope statements is the SR 11-7 examiner's first question and the corpus answers it before the examiner asks.

The remainder of this review is observation-quality (what the institution should be aware of when adopting), not finding-quality. None of the items below would block my sign-off.

## What the corpus gets right — load-bearing for SR 11-7

**Reproducibility surface is named, not implied.** Spec §4.4 makes `gen_ai.request.model` and `gen_ai.response.model` REQUIRED on any chain entry that represents a model call. The verifier enforces this at §7 step 12a — present and non-empty under `--strict`, PASS-WITH-ANOMALY otherwise. The `gen_ai_parameters` field carries the documented reproducibility surface — decoding parameters, sampler implementation identifier, system prompt content or content-hash, retrieval context, intra-run data dependencies. These are the five inputs an SR 11-7 reproduction needs. The institution that tries to reproduce a disputed decision under these captured inputs is not asked to invent the reproduction surface — the spec named it. That is unusual in AI control descriptions and is the single highest-leverage decision in the corpus from the MRM director's chair.

**Request-side AND response-side model identifier.** Vendors silently re-route between model versions during outages. I have personally seen an institution's MRM committee chase a fair-lending disparate-impact analysis for three weeks before discovering the vendor had silently rerouted requests across two model versions during the analysis period. The corpus calls this out explicitly in §4.4 — "the response-side model identifier is the load-bearing record for SR 11-7 reproducibility" — and the MRM-committee brief asks the committee to inspect per-`gen_ai.response.model` decision-count distributions per quarter. That is the right granularity. An MRM committee reviewing only request-side model identifiers is reviewing the institution's *intent*, not the institution's *behaviour*.

**Decision-equivalence threshold is set in advance, not at dispute time.** `customer-dispute-procedures.md` §"MRM committee variation-threshold guidance" requires the committee to set the decision-equivalence threshold in advance of the institution's first dispute reproduction, tier-stratified (95% for high-stakes, 90% for medium, 80% for low). The audit-procedure P-27 confirms the threshold-reasonableness review is process-of-governance, NOT substantive model-validation — the SOC team is not asked to do the MRM committee's substantive work. This is exactly where AI control descriptions usually get the SOC team / MRM committee boundary wrong, and the corpus has it right. A threshold set ad-hoc at dispute time is the indicator of a reactive MRM program; the corpus structurally prevents that.

**Reproduction-result schema is documented and chain-bound.** The `audit.reproduction.*` schema in P-27 (and in the customer-dispute doc) is exactly the schema an MRM committee would request — `original_run_id`, `original_seq`, `iterations`, `decision_equivalent_count`, `decision_equivalent_threshold`, `outcome`, `mrm_committee_review`. The reproduction is itself a chain entry with the original decision's `(run_id, seq)` as parent linkage. That keeps the reproduction integrity-bearing in the same way the original decision is integrity-bearing. The repudiability gap that would otherwise sit between the original decision and the reproduction-derived disposition is closed.

**Treasury RMF composition is explicit.** `regulator-pack/ai-policy-alignment.md` carries the four-row Treasury RMF composition table — model inventory, model lifecycle controls, third-party model governance, ongoing monitoring — and the OCC framing under Bulletin 2011-12 §III and §IV. An MRM director defending chain adoption to the OCC has, in this single document, the framing she needs: "the chain is the technical control implementing the integrity-of-AI-decision-evidence requirement under the existing SR 11-7 / 2011-12 framework." That framing avoids over-claiming OCC has explicitly required the chain (it has not) while recognising the chain's relevance to the OCC's evolving supervisory posture. I would write that paragraph the same way in front of an OCC examiner.

**EU AI Act Article 14 (effective human oversight) is correctly scoped.** The Article 14 row in the policy-alignment table is the row most institutions get wrong. The corpus does not claim the chain *delivers* effective human oversight — it claims the chain provides the substrate (decoding parameters, audit payload, model identifier) that makes the human reviewer's second-guessing effective. That is the correct scope. An institution that tried to argue to a German BaFin examiner that the chain itself delivers Article 14 oversight would be in a longer conversation than it expected; the corpus avoids that conversation by being precise about what the substrate is.

**Article 26 (deployer obligations) and DORA Article 8 (ICT-supported business functions) are composed, not invented.** The Article 26 row maps the per-event MAC + daily seal + HSM-signed root to the integrity-bearing log requirement under Art. 26(6); the operational events catalog supports Art. 26(5) monitoring; the chain's audit-trail availability supports Art. 26(11) communications to affected persons. DORA Art. 8 is mapped to the chain's per-tenant ledger as the integrity-bearing record of which ICT systems support which business functions. The MRM director coordinating with the institution's ICT risk function uses these compositions in her cross-functional briefing; the corpus did the cross-walk so she does not have to.

**Concurrent-version detection is in the committee's question set.** Standard question 8 in the MRM-committee brief asks the committee to discriminate between A/B test, canary deployment, vendor-side silent re-routing, and multi-region drift when multiple `gen_ai.response.model` versions are observed in a single examination period. The chain captures the response-model identifier (per spec §4.4 MUST requirement) but does NOT capture the institution's *deployment intent*. The committee asks the institution for the deployment-intent record per `gen_ai.response.model` value observed. That is the correct allocation of responsibility between the chain and the institution's MRM program.

**Reproduction posture is named in three load-bearing decisions.** The customer-dispute procedures make the institution name three things in advance: (1) chain-or-not posture for reproductions, (2) reproduction-result schema, (3) SOC procedure for testing reproduction evidence (P-27). Each one prevents a specific mode of accountability collapse I have seen in real institutions. (1) prevents the reproduction from being repudiable in the same way vendor logs are repudiable. (2) prevents reproductions of the same decision-class from being recorded against incommensurable schemas across reporting periods. (3) prevents the SOC engagement from being unable to mechanically test reproduction evidence. The triple is sufficient.

## What I would highlight to the institution as observation-quality

**Customer-impact-tier taxonomy is not universal — and the corpus knows it.** P-25's stratification asks for sampling across (a) AI model-version, (b) decision-class, (c) customer-impact tier with a 3 × 5 × 3 = 45-entry minimum. The procedure has the right fallback for institutions that do not yet operate a customer-impact-tier framework: stratify on (a) and (b) only with 7 entries per stratum (3 × 7 = 21-entry minimum), and the SOC report notes the absence as a control-program-maturity observation, NOT a finding. This is the right calibration for the smaller / regional banks I work with. I read this and was relieved; I have seen procedures in this space try to impose Goldman-grade sampling on a $3B-asset community bank.

**Nondeterminism is acknowledged honestly to the customer.** The customer-side response language in the dispute procedures includes: "Significant variation across re-runs (a phenomenon called model nondeterminism) is itself information our model-risk-management committee evaluates; consistent variation in your favor would prompt us to revisit the original decision." That is the right level of honesty. Many institutions try to hide model nondeterminism from the customer, which sets up a worse conversation if the customer's counsel discovers it independently. The MRM committee that adopts this language is, I think, defending the institution better than the alternative.

**IKM access for customer-side verification is operationally segmented.** The two acceptable shapes — protective-order disclosure (litigation context) vs HSM-mediated verification (operational context) — are correctly differentiated. I have personally been asked, in an arbitration context, whether the bank's chain-of-custody could be verified by an outside expert without the bank disclosing master-key bytes. The HSM-mediated shape is the answer I would have wanted to be able to give. The corpus does not just say "the customer can independently verify" — it names the two operational shapes and asks the institution to declare which it operates and at what threshold it shifts.

**The MRM committee's posture on chained vs unchained reproduction is opinionated.** The corpus recommends chaining the reproduction (with the original decision as parent linkage) and lets the committee opine to the contrary. The recommendation is correct. An institution that does not chain the reproduction has, by construction, a reproduction-evidence layer that is repudiable in the same way vendor logs are repudiable — which is the gap the chain exists to close. The committee opining against the recommendation should expect to defend that posture in front of an examiner. The corpus is not aggressive about saying so; it allows the committee its space, but the recommendation is clear enough that an examiner reading the institution's MRM policy will notice the deviation.

**Anomaly severity for `gen_ai_model_identifier_missing` is correctly Medium.** The anomaly-documentation template assigns Medium severity to step 12a failures and is explicit that this is "control-completeness for SR 11-7 reproducibility, NOT chain-integrity; the institution's MRM program loses reproduction surface for the affected entries." That is the right framing. I have seen procedures conflate control-completeness findings with integrity findings, which makes it impossible for the SOC team to write a clean opinion. The corpus separates them cleanly.

**Audit-committee summary ≠ MRM-committee brief ≠ management summary, and that's deliberate.** The three committee-facing artifacts are written for three different audiences and do not collapse into one. The CEO-facing management summary explicitly tells the CEO that the chain does not check whether AI decisions were correct ("whether the model made a good call is a separate matter — model risk management"); the MRM-committee brief carries the same scope statement in committee-relevant terms; the audit-committee brief composes them at the oversight layer. An institution rolling out the chain to its three governance layers does not have to translate between them. That separation matters; I have seen institutions try to give the MRM committee the audit committee's brief and the audit committee the management summary, and the conversation lands wrong both times.

**The MRM-committee brief's standard question 7 is the one most committees will not ask spontaneously.** Question 7 asks whether the IR playbook covers the new failure modes — `key_fingerprint mismatch` (Scenario 7), `unknown_key_version` (Scenario 8), `audit_file.truncation_detected` (Scenario 9). MRM committees routinely fail to ask whether the IR playbook covers the chain-specific failure modes; they assume the cyber team owns it. By naming the question in the committee's standard question set, the corpus prompts the committee to test the cross-functional handoff between MRM oversight and the chain-ops team. I would expect this to surface gaps in many institutions on first adoption, which is the value of the question being there.

## Where the corpus is intentionally silent — and that's correct

**The chain does not opine on model bias, fairness, or fair-lending compliance.** The corpus is correct to be silent here. Bias and fair-lending are the MRM committee's substantive work, and the chain provides the integrity-bearing data on which the committee's analysis runs. An attempt to extend the chain into bias detection would conflate evidence integrity with substantive analysis, and the boundary the corpus draws (chain produces integrity; MRM produces validity) would erode. I would not change this posture.

**The chain does not generate the adverse-action notice.** The customer-dispute procedures are explicit: the chain provides the AI's response (which may include the model's stated reasons); the bank's compliance team translates the AI's response into ECOA-compliant adverse-action language; the translation is itself a chain entry with the AI's original response as parent. The chain does not generate the notice; it provides the evidence that supports the notice's reasons. This is the correct allocation of responsibility between the chain and the institution's compliance function.

**The chain does not standardise the institution's `audit.*` payload schema.** Spec §4.4 and the MRM-committee brief are explicit that "the chain integrity-binds whatever the institution puts in `audit.*` and `gen_ai_parameters`; the institution's `audit.*` schema is a committee-relevant decision and should be reviewed periodically against the SR 11-7 reproducibility surface." The institution owns its `audit.*` schema. P-25 tests whether the institution's schema covers the documented reproducibility surface, but the schema itself is institution-defined. This is correct: a chain that prescribed the institution's audit schema would import the chain into every regulatory framework the institution operates under (Reg B, Reg E, OCC fair-banking, state-specific consumer-protection regimes), and the substrate would no longer be jurisdiction-neutral. The institution's compliance program completes the schema; the chain integrity-binds it.

**OCC has not issued formal AI-specific guidance, and the corpus says so.** The policy-alignment doc is honest about what the OCC has and has not done. "As of v1.0-rework publication, the OCC has not issued formal new AI-specific guidance; instead, it operationalises AI model-risk supervision under the existing SR 11-7 / OCC Bulletin 2011-12 model-risk-management framework." The corpus does not over-claim OCC has required the chain. That is the right posture; an MRM director who claims to her board that OCC requires a control OCC has not formally required will find herself in an uncomfortable conversation when OCC's own examiner walks in.

## Cross-jurisdictional notes from the Latin American chair

I work primarily across U.S., EU, and Latin American institutions. The corpus reads cleanly through the U.S. (SR 11-7, Treasury RMF, OCC Bulletin 2011-12, CFPB) and EU (AI Act, EBA, DORA, NIS2) lenses. The Latin American coverage is lighter — the BCB (Brazilian Central Bank) gets one paragraph naming the audit-trail expectations and that is the right level of treatment for a v1.0 spec, but I would flag for the working group that the BCB Resolution 4,893 (cyber resilience) and BCB Resolution 4,968 (cloud and IT outsourcing) have specific logging-integrity expectations that the chain composes with cleanly. Mexico's CNBV and Colombia's SFC have nascent AI-supervisory postures; an institution operating across LatAm jurisdictions will want to do the cross-walk itself for v1.0, and the spec's `audit.*` schema flexibility makes that tractable. None of this is finding-quality for v1.0; the corpus is correctly U.S.-and-EU-first.

## The MRM director's adoption recommendation

I would sign off on this corpus as the evidence layer for an SR 11-7 AI-MRM program at a mid-to-large U.S. bank or a high-risk-AI deployer under the EU AI Act. The corpus is at the level of polish where the institution's MRM committee can adopt the artifacts (committee brief, customer-dispute procedures, anomaly template) with light institution-specific tailoring rather than rewriting from scratch. The standard questions the committee asks are well-calibrated to surface gaps that would otherwise sit unaddressed for a reporting period.

The institution should be prepared, at adoption time, to make four named decisions before its first reporting period closes:

1. The decision-equivalence threshold per customer-impact tier (`customer-dispute-procedures.md` §"MRM committee variation-threshold guidance").
2. The reproduction-result schema under `audit.reproduction.*` (P-27, customer-dispute doc).
3. The IKM-access shape for customer-side verification (operational vs protective-order; customer-dispute doc).
4. The institution's `gen_ai_parameters` schema mapping to the documented reproducibility surface (P-25, spec §4.4).

Each of these is named in the corpus with the institution's choice space defined. The institution that walks into its first chain-bearing reporting period without these four decisions documented in MRM policy will find the SOC team and the FFIEC examiner asking for them, and the institution will be writing them ad-hoc — which is exactly the failure mode P-27's threshold-reasonableness review is calibrated to detect.
