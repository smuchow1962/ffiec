### Questions

**Q1. Does the SLSA L3 claim survive contact with the institutional-consumption requirement, AND does the corpus name SLSA-aware tool-choice as a normative documentation requirement so the examiner can verify which tool the institution operates against the trust-anchor table?**

**Status:** Answered

The institutional-consumption side of the SLSA L3 claim is what separates a publication-only artifact from a control I can defend in a CSF GV.SC-04 assessment. The v1.0 corpus carries the consumer-side requirement at the right enforcement layer.

`supply-chain.md` §"Institutional consumption of the SLSA L3 attestation (MUST-tier)" carries four normative requirements:

1. The institution MUST validate `verifier.intoto.jsonl` against `slsa-verifier` (or equivalent SLSA-aware tooling) BEFORE deploying a new release version of the verifier or ledger server. The validation is a **deployment-gating control**; a binary whose provenance attestation does not validate MUST NOT be deployed. That is the right enforcement layer; an after-the-fact attestation has no enforcement layer, which is what makes a publication-only claim theatre.
2. The institution's SLSA-verification log is a control-evidence artifact retained for the binary's deployment lifetime (typically 7 years per chain-event retention).
3. The institution's `slsa-verifier` binary itself MUST be signed and trust-anchored alongside the verifier. The exact framing — "if the institution validates the chain's verifier with cosign but runs an unsigned `slsa-verifier`, the supply-chain trust path has a gap that defeats the L3 evidence claim" — names the second-order trust-anchor problem in a way I would otherwise have to write into a remediation plan myself.
4. `slsa-verifier` is named as a co-equal trust artifact alongside cosign and the GPG-signed manifest. The trust-anchor table includes the `slsa-verifier` public key and binary SHA-256 cached out-of-band.

What I came in to verify on top of the consumer-side requirement is the **tool-choice documentation** requirement. The corpus carries it as a normative requirement: §"SLSA-aware tool-choice documentation (normative for examination-grade adopters)" requires the institution's control description to name (a) which SLSA-aware tool the institution operates, (b) the tool's version, (c) the tool's trust anchor (public key, binary SHA-256, signature-validation chain), and (d) where the documentation is archived. The framing in the spec text — "the cybersecurity examiner asks: which tool, what version, what trust anchor, archived where; the institution's documentation has those four answers in one place" — is the right standard. The implicit acknowledgement that `slsa-verifier` is the canonical reference but that equivalent tools must be named with evaluation rationale is the right scoping. The OpenSSF SLSA tooling ecosystem will produce alternatives over the next three years; the spec accommodates the future without locking the institution into a single tool that may not be the right choice when the institution's CSF assessment is sampled.

The §"What examiners verify" list propagates both the consumption requirement AND the tool-choice documentation requirement to the examination surface. That is what I would write into a remediation plan: the requirement, the operational artifact, and the examination evidence in one closed loop.

What I assessed as residual: the corpus does not name a specific minimum `slsa-verifier` version. Institutions taking the deployment-gating control seriously will pin the version in their procurement discipline; the spec leaves it as institution-side detail, which I think is correct (the OpenSSF cadence is independent of this project). I would write a documentation refinement into the next iteration that names the institution-side version-pinning as a complementary control. Not a spec gap.

**Q2. Does the cold-disaster-recovery key lifecycle have a defensible operational story — rotation cadence, key-holder lifecycle, annual dry-run with attestation evidence — or is the dual-compromise fallback "documented but never exercised" theatre?**

**Status:** Answered

This is the seam I most expected to find under-specified. In the field I see vendor cold-DR-key documentation that names the dual-compromise scenario, asserts that a cold-DR key exists, and then leaves the lifecycle as a gap the institution discovers when the dual-compromise actually arrives. The §"Cold-disaster-recovery key lifecycle (project-side governance)" section in `supply-chain.md` carries the operational rigor I came in to look for.

Four lifecycle properties, each at the right cadence:

1. **Rotation cadence: every 60 months** (5 years). The justification is correctly anchored in NIST SP 800-57 guidance for cold-storage signing keys, and the explicit comparison to the standard-GPG 36-month cadence makes the choice defensible: the cold-DR key is air-gapped and used only for emergencies, so a longer rotation cadence reduces the operational risk of frequent dry-runs touching air-gap discipline without weakening the cryptographic posture.
2. **Key-holder lifecycle.** The cold-DR key is held by a third separated small group within the project's release-management role, distinct from cosign-key holders and standard-GPG-key holders. Three separated groups for three trust paths is the right separation-of-duties shape; collusion across all three groups simultaneously is the residual the threat model accepts. Key-holder rotation per departure or governance event, with emergency rotation within 30 days of any key-holder departure, is the right responsive cadence — separation-of-duties holds only as long as the holders are who you think they are.
3. **Annual dry-run cadence.** The project performs a documented annual dry-run exercise to confirm the cold-DR key's hardware is functional, the air-gap procedure works, the key holders can be reached within target timelines (24 hours), and the published recovery procedure produces a successfully-validated transition statement. The dry-run output (without the actual key material) is published as `KEY-DR-DRYRUN-{year}.asc` signed under the standard cosign or GPG key. That is the right operational discipline; an undriven recovery procedure is one I have to assume will fail when called, and the published attestation gives institution-side reviewers an evidence artifact to consume in their third-party-risk-management cadence.
4. **Health check.** The project records the cold-DR key's hardware health (HSM tamper detection state, hardware-token battery indicator) on the same annual cadence; deviations trigger out-of-band notification to the FFIEC working group. Hardware tokens decay differently from software keys; the annual battery-and-tamper check catches the decay before it becomes a recovery-time failure.

The institution-side consumption story is also right: §"Cosign-key recovery during an active examination" specifies that institution-side reviewers consume the DR-DRYRUN attestations as evidence the dual-compromise fallback is operationally exercised, not just documented. That closes the loop — the project produces the dry-run attestation; the institution's third-party-risk-management consumes it; the examiner sample-tests the institution's consumption record.

What I assessed as residual: the spec does not name what happens if the annual dry-run produces a failure (cold-DR key hardware fault, key-holder unreachable, transition statement does not validate). The spec assumes the dry-run succeeds and publishes the attestation. A future-iteration refinement would name the dry-run-failure procedure: out-of-band notification to FFIEC working group, accelerated key-holder or hardware replacement, and an interim posture statement the institution's IR playbook can reference if the dual-compromise scenario triggers during the recovery window. Not a spec gap; a documentation refinement.

**Q3. Does the mirror-registry signature handling cover the re-signing pattern with the WORM-retention-and-continuous-monitoring story I would expect to see in a mature supply-chain control, including the bridge invariant that an examiner can sample-test two years after the binary was deployed?**

**Status:** Answered

`supply-chain.md` §"Container supply chain" → "Mirror-registry signature handling" defines the two patterns and the bridge documentation requirement. The §"Mirror audit-log retention and integrity (re-signing pattern only)" carries the operational rigor; §"Mirror continuous-monitoring control (DE.CM-09)" closes the silent-failure gap.

Three properties, each at the right enforcement layer:

- **Bridge documentation.** The re-signing mirror is conformant only when the institution documents the bridge: "the mirror's re-signature is conditional on the project's cosign signature having validated at mirror-pull time, recorded in the mirror's audit log; the institution's IR playbook treats a missing mirror-pull validation log entry as a Scenario 3 finding." Re-signing without documenting the bridge is non-conformant; the institution loses the project-side cosign as a trust anchor and depends entirely on its own internal cosign key. That is the right cliff; the choice is conformant signature-preserving, conformant re-signing-with-bridge, or non-conformant re-signing-without-bridge.
- **WORM retention.** The audit log MUST be on append-only / WORM storage with 7-year retention minimum. The acceptable backends are named — AWS CloudWatch Logs with locked retention policy + S3 Object Lock, Azure Monitor with immutable storage, GCP Logging with locked retention bucket, on-prem WORM appliance. Naming the conformant backends removes the ambiguity that I would otherwise expect to surface as an examiner finding.
- **Continuous monitoring (DE.CM-09).** The reconciliation between the mirror's pulled-image set and the audit-log-validation set fires on mismatch. The pattern is explicitly framed as the same-shape control as the spec §10.1 fingerprint reconciliation, which gives the institution a familiar operational cadence to plug into. The reconciliation event is `mirror.reconciliation_completed`, institution-defined, not part of the chain spec §10.2 catalog — that is the right scoping decision. The chain spec's operational-event catalog is for events the chain construction emits; the mirror-reconciliation is institution-internal infrastructure operational evidence and should not pollute the chain's event surface.

The §"What examiners verify" list propagates the requirement to three discrete control checks an examiner can sample-test independently — audit log shows project-side validation at pull time, audit log is on WORM storage with 7-year retention, continuous-monitoring control fires on signature-validation discipline gaps. That is the right testing standard.

What I assessed as residual: the spec does not name a default reconciliation cadence. "Periodic (daily or per-pull)" leaves room for institution-specific tuning, which is appropriate, but a forward-looking refinement would name a default — daily — with an explicit institution-side option to tune per-pull. Documentation refinement; not a spec gap.

**Q4. Does the corpus carry the institution-side reception procedure for a regulator-held public-key fingerprint rotation — i.e., when the regulator publishes a new fingerprint mid-examination, does the institution have a documented, validated, archive-able reception flow, or is the procedure a hand-wave to "the institution will coordinate"?**

**Status:** Answered

This is the seam I rated as the most likely to fail under examination. In the field I see threat-model documentation that names Adversary I's residual ("the regulator-held fingerprint is the trust anchor that makes the institution-published public key trustable; rotating it is a regulator-side procedure") and then stops. The institution-side reception procedure is what makes the regulator-side procedure operationally consumable; without it, the institution either accepts a forged rotation notice or fails to update the verifier and verifies against a stale fingerprint at the next examination.

`09-threat-model.md` §2.9 (Adversary I) carries the institution-side reception-and-validation procedure as five enumerated steps:

1. **Receive the rotation notice.** Through the institution's established regulator-communication channel (encrypted messaging system or paper notice with hand-delivered envelope to the institution's compliance officer). That is the right channel discipline — the rotation notice is a high-trust artifact that should not be received through casual channels.
2. **Validate the rotation notice's authenticity.** Against the regulator's published GPG signature, with cross-check against the regulator's parallel notification on the regulator's authenticated-domain channel. Two independent validation paths is the right defense against a forged rotation notice; a single channel is a single point of compromise.
3. **Update the institution's verifier configuration.** Install the new fingerprint in the verifier's IKM-roster / trust-anchor configuration; archive the old fingerprint per the institution's standard retention.
4. **Re-validate any historical verifier reports** that depended on the old fingerprint, against the new fingerprint, to confirm the institution's verifier output remains stable across the rotation. That is the property check I would write into a remediation plan; rotation that changes verifier output retroactively is a control failure even if the new output is correct, because the institution cannot defend the historical reports without re-running them.
5. **Document the rotation in the institution's control-evidence repository** for the SOC team and the next FFIEC examination.

The framing — "Without a documented institution-side reception procedure, the institution risks (a) accepting a forged rotation notice, or (b) failing to update the verifier and verifying against the old fingerprint when the new fingerprint is in force" — names both failure modes correctly. The institution's IR playbook references this procedure when a fingerprint-rotation notice arrives, which is the right cross-reference.

The boundary statement is also right: "Examiner-laptop hygiene is a regulator-side control, NOT an institution-side control." The chain's defense composition (cosign + GPG + regulator-held fingerprint + `bundleverify` + offline binary + reproducible build) is what the institution can defend; examiner-laptop hygiene is what the regulator's own program defends. Calling out that the residual is **out of scope for the institution's CC6 evaluation** under SOC 2 closes the seam I most often see misread by institution-side reviewers — SOC engagement partners who try to write examiner-laptop hygiene into the institution's CC6 evaluation produce findings the institution cannot remediate. The corpus carries the boundary cleanly.

What I assessed as residual: the regulator-side fingerprint rotation procedure itself is referenced as a v1.1 candidate stub (`regulator-pack/regulator-procedures.md`). The institution-side reception procedure is the right scoping for what the institution can control; the regulator-side rotation procedure is what the regulator owns. Until the regulator-side procedure is published, the trust-anchor rotation event is named as an unbounded residual the institution cannot control institution-side. That is the honest framing — the institution's procedure is documented and complete; the regulator's procedure is acknowledged as out-of-scope-for-this-corpus and scheduled for v1.1.

**Q5. Does the 36-hour FFIEC cyber-incident triage matrix name (a) the federal-regulator-routing-per-institution-charter detail and (b) the multi-alert / multi-tenant / CIRCIA-only edge cases I would expect to see written into the IR Commander's checklist, or does it leave the determination point as a free-form judgment call?**

**Status:** Answered

The 36-hour clock is the hardest examination surface I work, and counsel-side defensibility hinges on the institution's documented determination point. A triage matrix that names per-scenario clock-start triggers but leaves the routing and the edge cases to in-the-moment judgment produces inconsistent determinations across incidents — the kind of inconsistency a FFIEC examiner samples and writes up.

`incident-response-playbook.md` §"36-hour cyber-incident notification triage matrix" carries the per-scenario clock-start trigger as a matrix the IR Commander can reference. Ten scenarios, each with a default disposition and a named clock-start trigger. The matrix carries the institutional-guidance framing — "counsel decides per incident. The chain produces precise alerts; the institution's IR program produces the determination and the clock-start" — which is the right boundary; the spec produces the alerts; counsel's call is what starts the clock.

The §"Edge cases the matrix names explicitly" subsection carries four edge-case framings, each at the right scoping:

1. **Concurrent multi-scenario alerts (rollup rule).** The IR Commander treats a constellation of Scenario 1 + Scenario 7 + Scenario 8 alerts in the same 60-minute window as one incident and starts the 36-hour clock at the earliest qualifying determination across the constellation, not the union of per-scenario clocks. The reasoning — "treating each scenario's clock independently risks both (a) double-counting incidents in the regulator notification and (b) under-counting where the constellation is the signal even though no individual scenario crossed its threshold" — is correct. Constellation-as-single-incident is the right call; the IR Commander documents the rollup decision and the chosen clock-start in the incident-management system, which is what counsel needs to defend the determination.
2. **Cross-tenant scope discovery during investigation (vendor-hosted topology).** The 36-hour clock applies per institution; in a vendor-hosted deployment one investigation may produce N institution-side determinations on different timelines. That is the right framing — the vendor's contractual notification SLA (typically 4–12 hours from vendor's determination) is a SEPARATE clock from the institution's 36-hour FFIEC clock, both apply, both are tracked. The vendor-vs-institution responsibility split is correctly named.
3. **Federal-regulator routing per institution charter.** This is what I came in to verify. The matrix names six chartering structures and the corresponding primary federal regulator (OCC, FRB, FDIC, NCUA), with explicit handling of state regulators as the secondary path for state-chartered entities and explicit handling of multi-charter holding companies. The framing — "The IR Commander confirms the routing per institution charter at the time of the incident; the institution's standing IR documentation pre-records the primary regulator and the secondary state-side counterpart where applicable" — is correctly scoped. Pre-recorded routing means the IR Commander does not lose the first hour of the 36-hour clock figuring out who to call. Examiners working a sample-test of the IR documentation will look for the pre-recorded routing; institutions whose IR documentation does not have it produce a finding I would write up.
4. **CIRCIA-only triggers without FFIEC trigger.** The matrix names five scenarios and each one's FFIEC vs CIRCIA trigger profile. Counsel works the matrix per incident; the institution's IR program SHOULD have CIRCIA notification on its standard checklist for chain-detected events even when the FFIEC clock has not started. That is the right operational discipline — CIRCIA's "substantial cyber incident" definition is broader than the FFIEC rule's "safety/soundness" framing, and an institution that runs only the FFIEC checklist will under-notify CIRCIA-only events.

The full notification-paths table at the end of the playbook (§"CIRCIA and additional notification frameworks") names FFIEC, CIRCIA, state data-breach notification, CFPB UDAAP, and sealing-delay notification as separate paths the institution's IR program tracks. Five paths, each with their own trigger and timing and recipient. That is the right comprehensiveness; an IR Commander who works only one path produces a multi-path-failure I have seen in two engagements in the last three years.

What I assessed as residual: the federal-regulator-routing table does not name what happens when a multi-charter holding company has both a chartered subsidiary that triggers FFIEC and a non-chartered subsidiary (insurance, broker-dealer, fintech) that does not. The matrix names the chartered routing; the holding-company-with-mixed-subsidiaries case is left to counsel. A future-iteration refinement would name the holding-company-mixed-subsidiary routing pattern (the chartered subsidiary's primary regulator gets the FFIEC notification; the non-chartered subsidiary's regulator — SEC for broker-dealer, state insurance commissioner for insurance — gets its own notification through its own framework). That is a documentation refinement; not a spec gap.
