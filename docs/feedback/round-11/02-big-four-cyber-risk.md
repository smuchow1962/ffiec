## Persona — Eitan Schreiber

**Background.** Nineteen years across Israeli and US bank cybersecurity advisory; current title is Senior Manager in the Cyber Risk practice of a Big Four firm with a portfolio of top-50 US bank clients. Last four years have been almost entirely supply-chain: SLSA framework adoption programs at three G-SIBs, mirror-registry build-outs for two regional banks coming off a vendor-imposed-image incident, and engagement-quality reviewer on a SOC 2 + SOC for Cybersecurity for two AI-platform vendors with bank end-customers. Reviewer for the Linux Foundation's OpenSSF working group on AI/ML supply chain since 2024. **Reading angle.** I look at substance, not posture. A SLSA L3 claim that the institution does not consume is theatre; a cosign trust path that has no documented recovery procedure is a single point of failure waiting for an operator to discover; a mirror registry that re-signs without WORM-backed audit log is an open back door dressed in compliance language. I pay particular attention to the seams: where the spec hands off to operational discipline, where a documented procedure terminates in "the institution will," where the trust path crosses an organizational boundary that the document does not control. The 36-hour FFIEC clock is a hard examination surface for me — if the institution cannot produce a defensible determination point and the document's triage matrix does not name the multi-alert and multi-tenant edge cases, that is a finding I will write into the audit-committee deck. Adversary I (examination-time tooling subversion) is the hardest part of the threat model to scope honestly because it crosses into the regulator's own program; I want to see whether the document carries that line cleanly or whether it tries to push regulator-side hygiene back onto institution-side controls.

### Questions

**Q1. Does the SLSA L3 claim survive contact with the institutional-consumption requirement — i.e., does the institution actually validate the published `verifier.intoto.jsonl` at deployment time, with `slsa-verifier` operated as a co-equal trust artifact alongside cosign and the GPG-signed manifest, and is the validation a deployment-gating control rather than an after-the-fact attestation?**

**Status:** Answered

This is the gap I came in expecting to find. Most SLSA L3 claims I see in bank-vendor documentation are publication-only: the vendor publishes the provenance attestation and the institution files it. An examiner working a CSF GV.SC-04 assessment will challenge that posture. The v1.0 corpus closes the gap explicitly.

`supply-chain.md` §"SLSA level claim" → "Institutional consumption of the SLSA L3 attestation (MUST-tier)" carries four normative requirements that match what I would have asked for in a remediation plan:

1. The institution MUST validate `verifier.intoto.jsonl` against `slsa-verifier` BEFORE deploying a new release version. The validation is a **deployment-gating control**; a binary whose provenance attestation does not validate MUST NOT be deployed. That is the right enforcement layer — a publication-only claim has no enforcement layer, which is precisely what makes it theatre.
2. The institution's SLSA-verification log is a control-evidence artifact retained for the binary's deployment lifetime (typically 7 years per chain-event retention).
3. The institution's `slsa-verifier` binary itself MUST be signed and trust-anchored alongside the verifier — "if the institution validates the chain's verifier with cosign but runs an unsigned `slsa-verifier`, the supply-chain trust path has a gap that defeats the L3 evidence claim." That is the second-order trust-anchor problem and I am pleased to see it called out in the spec text rather than left as an institution-side discovery.
4. `slsa-verifier` is named as a co-equal trust artifact alongside cosign and the GPG-signed manifest. The trust-anchor table needs to include the `slsa-verifier` public key and binary SHA-256 cached out-of-band. Same enforcement standard as cosign and GPG.

The §"What examiners verify" list propagates the requirement to the examination surface: "The institution validates the SLSA provenance attestation against `slsa-verifier` at deployment time (deployment-gating control); the SLSA-verifier output log is archived for the binary's deployment lifetime (typically 7 years)" and "the institution has cached the project's cosign public key AND the project's GPG public key (for fallback) AND the project's SLSA-verifier public key." That mirror between requirement and examination evidence is what gives the control a defensible posture in a CSF assessment.

The framing — "An institution that publishes the L3 claim without operating `slsa-verifier` consumption has a gap a GV.SC-04 examiner will challenge" — is correctly attributed to GV.SC-04 (cybersecurity supply chain — third-party assessments) and ID.RA-09 (authenticity of hardware and software). Both are the right CSF 2.0 references. Examiners working those subcategories will read the SLSA-verification log; the institution that produces it passes; the institution that does not, fails.

What I assessed as residual: the spec does not name a specific `slsa-verifier` version or pin its toolchain in the way that the verifier's own toolchain is pinned in §"Toolchain pinning." Institutions that take the deployment-gating control seriously will pin `slsa-verifier` as a versioned dependency in their deployment pipeline; the spec text leaves that as institution-side procurement detail, which I think is the right call (the SLSA-verifier upstream cadence is governed by OpenSSF and not by this project) but a forward-looking refinement would name `slsa-verifier`'s version pinning as an institution-side complementary control. That is a documentation refinement, not a spec gap.

**Q2. Does the mirror-registry re-signing pattern have the WORM-and-retention-and-continuous-monitoring story I would expect to see in a mature supply-chain control — i.e., is the bridge invariant ("mirror's re-signature is conditional on the project's cosign signature having validated at mirror-pull time") actually examinable two years after the binary was deployed, and is there a continuous-monitoring control that catches a silent failure of the bridge?**

**Status:** Answered

`supply-chain.md` §"Container supply chain" → "Mirror-registry signature handling" defines the two patterns and the bridge documentation requirement; §"Mirror audit-log retention and integrity (re-signing pattern only)" carries the operational rigor I came in to look for, and §"Mirror continuous-monitoring control (DE.CM-09)" closes the silent-failure gap.

**Retention and integrity (the audit-log invariant):**

- **Retention duration: 7 years minimum** (or longer if chain-event retention exceeds 7 years). The justification in the spec text matches the operational reality I see in the field: "The bridge invariant is examined long after the binary's deployment; an FFIEC examiner sampling the bridge on a binary deployed two years ago needs the original validation log entry available." Standard cloud-logging defaults (CloudWatch Logs, Azure Monitor, GCP Cloud Logging) retain for shorter periods; the spec correctly forces an explicit institutional decision to extend.
- **WORM storage.** The audit log MUST be on append-only / WORM storage, NOT standard log rotation that the operator can delete. The acceptable backends are named: "AWS CloudWatch Logs with locked retention policy + S3 Object Lock; Azure Monitor with immutable storage; GCP Logging with locked retention bucket; on-prem WORM appliance." That is the right enforcement layer — operator-deletable storage breaks the bridge invariant in a way the operator can do unintentionally and the institution cannot then recover from at examination time. Naming the conformant backends removes the ambiguity that I would otherwise expect to surface as an examiner finding.

**Continuous monitoring (the silent-failure invariant):**

§"Mirror continuous-monitoring control (DE.CM-09)" addresses what I rate as the highest-likelihood failure in a re-signing mirror deployment: "A re-signing mirror that silently stops validating project signatures (operator misconfig at the mirror, registry-side bug, vendor regression) would produce mirror-side re-signed images with no project-side validation evidence — and the institution's deployment pipeline, validating only the institution's signature, would not detect this." The operational answer is a periodic (daily or per-pull) reconciliation between the mirror's pulled-image set and the audit-log-validation set; mismatch fires an operational alert. The control is correctly attributed to DE.CM-09 (continuous monitoring — software environment and personnel activity), and the pattern is explicitly framed as the same-shape control as the spec §10.1 fingerprint reconciliation, which gives the institution a familiar operational cadence to plug into.

The reconciliation event is named — `mirror.reconciliation_completed`, institution-defined, not part of the chain spec §10.2 catalog — which is the right scoping decision. The chain spec's operational-event catalog is for events the chain construction emits; the mirror-reconciliation is institution-internal infrastructure operational evidence and should not pollute the chain's event surface. That separation is clean.

**Examination surface:**

The §"What examiners verify" list propagates the requirement: "For institutions using the re-signing mirror pattern: the mirror's audit log shows the project-side cosign signature validated at pull time, AND the audit log is on WORM storage with 7-year retention, AND the mirror's continuous-monitoring control fires on signature-validation discipline gaps (the `mirror.reconciliation_completed` operational event)." That is the right testing standard — three discrete control checks that an examiner can sample-test independently.

What I assessed as residual: the spec does not name a default reconciliation cadence. "Periodic (daily or per-pull)" leaves room for institution-specific tuning, which is appropriate, but I would write into a remediation plan a default — daily — with an explicit institution-side option to tune per-pull. The institution's standard cadence-tuning posture aligns with the §10.1 fingerprint reconciliation cadence (weekly default, daily/hourly/continuous as institution-elected). Documentation refinement; not a spec gap.

**Q3. Does the GPG-key recovery procedure cover the dual-compromise edge case (cosign AND GPG simultaneously compromised) with a defensible operational continuity path that is not just "the project will issue a new release"?**

**Status:** Answered

This is the seam I most expected to find under-specified. Most key-recovery procedures I see in vendor documentation cover the single-key compromise case and silently assume the dual-compromise case is "outside the threat model." The dual-compromise case is precisely where Adversary I lives in a sustained-attacker scenario, and an honest threat model has to address it.

`supply-chain.md` §"GPG-key recovery procedure" carries the symmetric structure I would expect for the single-compromise case: 24-hour project response (revocation notice signed by cosign, since GPG is the compromised artifact), 48-hour institution notification through the same channels as cosign-key recovery, 7-day new-key authentication chain (new GPG key fingerprint published in spec text and as KEY-INTRODUCTION notice signed by the project's cosign identity), institution-side action to remove the revoked GPG key from trust anchors. Standard symmetric structure to the cosign recovery procedure. No issue.

The §"Dual-compromise edge case" subsection is the part that matters for my reading. Four operational components:

1. **Remaining trust anchor named explicitly.** "The project's only remaining trust anchor is the regulator-held public-key fingerprint (which is the cosign public key as recorded with the regulator at tenant onboarding). Per Adversary I, three independent compromises must align for forged data to silently pass." This carries the trust-anchor depth correctly: even with cosign and GPG compromised simultaneously, the regulator-held fingerprint (recorded at tenant onboarding) is the third leg. The spec text correctly attributes this to Adversary I in the threat model.
2. **Cold-disaster-recovery key.** "The project escalates: published recovery notice signed by an out-of-band emergency GPG key the release-management role pre-provisioned for this scenario (the 'cold-disaster-recovery key' maintained by yet another separated small group, never used until a dual-compromise event)." That is the right pre-provisioning posture — a cold key that exists only for the dual-compromise scenario, held by a separated small group, never exercised in normal operations. Most projects do not have one and discover the gap during their first dual-compromise drill; the spec text correctly forces the pre-provisioning.
3. **Institution-side operational continuity.** "The institution's emergency response: stop deploying new releases of the verifier and ledger server until the project completes the new-key authentication chain. Continue using the historical (pre-compromise) verifier binary against historical seals; the historical binary's signatures remain validatable against the pre-compromise cached cosign public key." That is the right operational continuity story — the historical binary continues to work because the historical signatures were valid at deployment time and the institution's reproducible-build log preserves the deployment-time evidence. The institution is not blocked on the project's recovery timeline for ongoing examination work.
4. **Regulator coordination.** "The institution coordinates directly with its primary regulator on examination posture during the multi-week dual-compromise recovery window." That carries the right framing: dual-compromise is a multi-week recovery, not a multi-hour one, and the institution-regulator communication is part of the operational response, not an afterthought. The spec text does not paper over the timeline.

The cross-references are correctly placed: dual-compromise is a project-side governance event documented in `GOVERNANCE.md` §"Crypto Key Disaster Recovery"; the institution-side response is documented in IR Scenario 3 (signature verification failed) under the "binary-signature variant" with a pointer to this section. That is the right separation — project-side governance is one document, institution-side IR is another, the two cross-reference rather than duplicating.

What I assessed as residual: the spec text says "the cold-disaster-recovery key maintained by yet another separated small group, never used until a dual-compromise event." Operationally, "never used" is risky — keys that are never exercised tend to discover their own failures at the moment of use. A defensive refinement would have the cold key exercised on a documented cadence (annually, signing a no-op test artifact) so its operational health is known when it is needed. That is an institution-side / project-side operational refinement; not a gap in the spec text but a worth-flagging operational practice. The §"GPG key rotation cadence" subsection covers main-key and subkey rotation cadences but does not name a cold-key health-check cadence; a v1.1 refinement could add it.

**Q4. Does the cosign-key recovery procedure during an active examination window carry a defensible operational posture — i.e., can the institution continue an in-progress examination through the recovery window without producing inconsistent evidence between pre-recovery, during-recovery, and post-recovery verification runs?**

**Status:** Answered

`supply-chain.md` §"Cosign-key recovery during an active examination" addresses what I would expect to find as the most operationally awkward scenario in the supply-chain section — the recovery notice arrives mid-examination, the EIC is on-site, and the bank has to produce defensible verification results without re-running the entire examination on the recovered binary.

Four operational components:

1. **Historical binary remains usable against historical seals.** "The historical binary's signature was valid at deployment time, recorded in the bank's reproducible-build log. Past verifications using the historical verifier remain authoritative." That is correct — the deployment-time signature validation is what mattered, and the institution's reproducible-build log is the evidence that the validation occurred at the right point. Past verification results do not need to be re-litigated by the recovery.
2. **New-version deployment paused by the deployment-gating control.** "The bank pauses new-version verifier deployment until the new cosign key is available and validated. The bank's deployment-gating control (per the SLSA L3 institutional consumption section above) refuses to deploy the next release until the new cosign key is on the institution's trust-anchor list." That ties the deployment-gating control I asked about in Q1 to the operational continuity here — the same gate that enforces the SLSA L3 consumption requirement also enforces the cosign-recovery operational continuity. Single gate, two compliance properties.
3. **Mid-engagement EIC communication and GPG-fallback interim posture.** "If the examination is mid-engagement, the bank communicates the supply-chain incident to the EIC immediately, offers the GPG-fallback validation as the interim posture, and continues the examination using the GPG-validated binary. The bank's `bundleverify` script (per `07-verifier-design.md` §8.5) supports the GPG-only validation mode for this case; the EIC re-runs the bank's verifier outputs through `bundleverify --gpg-only` to confirm the GPG fallback validates." That is the right operational posture — the GPG path was always the documented fallback for Sigstore compromise, and a cosign-key compromise is operationally similar from the institution's perspective (the cosign trust path is unavailable until recovery completes). The EIC is not asked to trust the institution's word; the EIC re-runs the validation through `bundleverify --gpg-only` independently.
4. **Post-recovery re-validation for evidence stability.** "Post-recovery, when the new cosign key is published and the bank installs it, the bank re-runs the affected verifier outputs through the new-cosign-key-validated binary to confirm the verification results are stable across the trust-anchor change. The examination's working papers record both the GPG-fallback and post-cosign-recovery validation results for evidence completeness." This is the part most operational documents skip and that I most wanted to see. The examination's working papers carry both the during-recovery and post-recovery results, side-by-side, so the examiner can confirm that the trust-anchor change did not change the verification outcome. That is the right evidence-completeness standard for a regulated examination.

The cross-reference to IR Scenario 3 (signature verification failed) under the "binary-signature variant" places the procedure correctly in the institution's IR documentation rather than only in the supply-chain doc.

What I assessed as residual: the procedure assumes `bundleverify` supports a `--gpg-only` mode. The cross-reference points to `07-verifier-design.md` §8.5. I did not read §8.5 directly in this round, so I am taking the cross-reference on faith; an implementation-level review would confirm `bundleverify --gpg-only` actually exists in the released wrapper script. That is implementation verification, not a spec-text gap.

**Q5. Does the 36-hour cyber-incident notification triage matrix handle the realistic edge cases — concurrent multi-scenario alerts (rollup), vendor-hosted determination split, and CIRCIA-only triggers without FFIEC trigger — with operational guidance that does not collapse into "counsel decides per incident"?**

**Status:** Answered

`incident-response-playbook.md` §"36-hour cyber-incident notification triage matrix" lays out the per-scenario clock-start triggers in a row-per-scenario form, and the §"Edge cases the matrix names explicitly" subsection addresses the three multi-event cases I came in to look for.

**Concurrent multi-scenario alerts (rollup rule).** "If the institution receives a Scenario 1 (chain hash mismatch) alert AND a Scenario 7 (key_fingerprint mismatch) alert AND a Scenario 8 (unknown_key_version) alert in the same 60-minute window, the institution may be looking at a single root-cause event that triggered three detection paths. The institution's IR program SHOULD treat the constellation as one incident and start the 36-hour clock at the earliest qualifying determination across the constellation, NOT at the union of per-scenario clocks." That is the right operational guidance. Most IR documents I review treat each scenario clock independently and produce double-counted incidents; the rollup rule correctly names the failure mode ("(a) double-counting incidents in the regulator notification and (b) under-counting where the constellation is the signal even though no individual scenario crossed its threshold") and gives the IR Commander an explicit decision to make and document. The 60-minute window is correctly bounded — narrow enough to identify constellation events, wide enough to absorb realistic alert-pipeline delays.

**Cross-tenant scope discovery during investigation (vendor-hosted topology).** "Scenarios 4 and 7 may discover during investigation that the suspected compromise spans more than one tenant in a multi-tenant deployment (vendor-hosted topology, shared-cloud-HSM at community-bank tier per `00-overview.md` §6.5). The 36-hour clock applies per institution; in a vendor-hosted deployment one investigation may produce N institution-side determinations on different timelines." That carries the per-institution scope correctly — the FFIEC clock is per regulated entity, and one cross-tenant compromise does not collapse into one regulator notification.

**Vendor-vs-institution responsibility split.** "The vendor's IR team produces the alert and conducts the cross-tenant investigation. Each institution's IR program receives the alert through the vendor's notification channel (typically a dedicated security-event feed) and produces its own determination based on the vendor's investigation findings AND any institution-specific evidence the institution gathers. The institution's clock starts when the institution determines, not when the vendor first alerted. The vendor's contractual notification SLA (typically 4-12 hours from vendor's determination) is a SEPARATE clock from the institution's 36-hour FFIEC clock — both apply, both are tracked." That is the right framing — the vendor-contract clock and the FFIEC clock are different clocks, both applicable, both tracked. I have written this exact finding into a remediation plan more than once in my career; the spec text gets it right on the first cut.

**CIRCIA-only triggers without FFIEC trigger.** The matrix in §"CIRCIA-only triggers without FFIEC trigger" enumerates the asymmetric cases per scenario (Scenario 5 sealing-delay associated with broader cyber incident: maybe FFIEC, likely CIRCIA; Scenario 9 truncation-pattern across hosts: maybe FFIEC, likely CIRCIA at scale; Scenario 7 confirmed unauthorized substitution: yes both; Scenario 4 confirmed master compromise: yes both; Scenario 6 software-key fallback without unauthorized signing: no neither). The closing guidance — "The institution's IR program SHOULD have CIRCIA notification on its standard checklist for chain-detected events, even when the FFIEC clock has not started — CIRCIA's 'substantial cyber incident' definition is broader than the FFIEC rule's 'safety/soundness' framing" — is the operationally important framing. Counsel decides per incident; the playbook's role is to make sure counsel has the matrix in front of them.

The matrix at row level handles the per-scenario determination triggers clearly:

- Scenario 1: clock starts when investigation rules out SDK defect AND confirms in-flight modification.
- Scenario 2: clock starts when investigation rules out storage/recovery causes AND confirms ledger-content alteration between capture and verification.
- Scenario 3: clock starts at alert receipt (the determination is the alert, given HSM-key compromise implication). That is the right framing — Scenario 3 is the one where the alert IS the determination because the implication is severe enough that further investigation is the post-clock-start work.
- Scenario 4: clock starts at the moment the institution determines the suspect signal is credible (not at first alert; intel-driven scenarios may have a longer evaluation period).
- Scenario 5: does not start the clock unless the delay is associated with a suspected security incident; then the security incident's clock starts independently.
- Scenario 6: does not start the clock unless the fallback's presence is associated with confirmed unauthorized signing.
- Scenario 7: clock starts when the institution determines the mismatch is not attributable to documented operations (rotation-in-flight, restored-backup, tenant-row-restored) AND treats as suspected unauthorized key substitution. The triage tree in Scenario 7 itself enumerates the operational triage cases 1-3 (P2, no clock) versus case 4 (P1, clock starts).
- Scenario 8: clock starts when investigation indicates the `key_version` field was altered after capture (tampering signal) rather than missing-from-registry (operational signal).
- Scenario 9: does not start the clock unless the truncation pattern recurs in a way suggesting deliberate corruption.
- Scenario 10: clock starts when the institution determines the gap is unfillable AND the affected days remain unverifiable.

What I assessed as residual: the matrix does not address what I would call the "delayed determination" edge case — a scenario whose default disposition is operational (no clock) but whose investigation extends across a period during which new evidence arrives that re-classifies the disposition. Example: a Scenario 7 fingerprint mismatch that triages as case 1 (rotation-in-flight, no clock) on day 1, but on day 5 the institution discovers the rotation was unauthorized and the case re-classifies to case 4. The institution's clock starts on day 5 at the re-classification, not retroactively to day 1, but the spec text does not name this case explicitly. A v1.1 refinement could add a "re-classification rule" — clock starts at the moment of re-classification, with the institution documenting the original triage disposition and the re-classification trigger as part of the determination evidence. Documentation refinement; not a spec-text gap.

**Q6. Does the threat model carry the Adversary I residual scope correctly — specifically, does it frame examiner-laptop hygiene as a regulator-side control NOT an institution-side control, in a way that does not let an institution-side reviewer (SOC engagement partner, internal audit team) misread it as an institution-owned control gap?**

**Status:** Answered

This is the part of the threat model that I see most often misread in my engagements. An SOC engagement partner reading a threat model that names "examiner-laptop compromise" as a residual risk will sometimes interpret the residual as something the institution should be controlling, and write a finding into the SOC report that the institution cannot remediate because the institution does not operate the examiner's laptop. The spec text has to carry the line cleanly.

`09-threat-model.md` §2.9 (Adversary I — Examiner-side tooling subversion) carries the framing I would expect. Three operational components:

1. **Defense composition correctly identified.** "Three independent compromises (cosign, GPG-signed manifest, regulator-held public-key fingerprint) would have to align for the verifier to silently pass forged data. The examiner runs `bundleverify` (or the equivalent shell wrapper) before every verifier invocation; the wrapper validates cosign + reproducible-build manifest + GPG fallback and exits 0 only if all three pass. The verifier itself makes no network calls (build constraint bans the network stack from the verification path)." That is the institution-defendable layer of the defense — the verifier supply-chain discipline, the binary signature validation, the reproducible-build evidence, the no-network construction. Those are the controls the institution can defend.
2. **Residual risk correctly attributed.** The residual paragraph names the simultaneous compromise of cosign + GPG + regulator-held fingerprint as a project-side governance and supply-chain compromise (not a chain-construction defect), and names the regulator-held fingerprint rotation as a regulator-side procedure (referenced in `regulator-pack/regulator-procedures.md` — v1.1 candidate stub). That is the right attribution — the institution does not own the regulator-held fingerprint and cannot rotate it.
3. **Examiner-laptop hygiene framing — the load-bearing paragraph.** "Examiner-laptop hygiene is a regulator-side control, NOT an institution-side control. A compromised examiner laptop can produce a false report regardless of the chain's integrity properties. Mitigation is the FFIEC's own examiner-IT program: separation of duties between examination work and personal work, dedicated audit machines for high-sensitivity engagements, examiner-IT lifecycle management, and the FFIEC's standard endpoint-security posture. **This residual is out of scope for the institution's CC6 evaluation under SOC 2 because the institution does not operate examiner-IT; institution-side reviewers (SOC engagement partners) should NOT misread this residual as an institution-owned control gap.** The chain's defense composition (cosign + GPG + regulator-held fingerprint + `bundleverify` + offline binary + reproducible build) is what the institution can defend; examiner-laptop hygiene is what the regulator's own program defends."

That is the language I came in to look for, written almost verbatim to the framing I would put into a remediation plan. The bolded paragraph closes the failure mode I have seen most often in the field: the SOC engagement partner reads the residual, interprets it as a control gap the institution owns, writes a finding, and the institution has no remediation path because the control does not belong to them. The spec text cuts that off at the first reading.

The cross-reference to §2.4 in `00-overview.md` (examination-time tooling subversion) is correctly placed for first-class catalog completeness — Adversary I appears in two places (the four-attack-approaches catalog in `00-overview.md` and the threat-model adversaries catalog in `09-threat-model.md`) so that a reader of either document finds it.

What I assessed as residual: the spec text references `regulator-pack/regulator-procedures.md` as a v1.1 candidate stub for the regulator-held fingerprint rotation procedure. Until that procedure is published, the trust-anchor rotation event is "recognized as an unbounded residual the institution cannot control institution-side." That is honest framing — the spec text does not paper over the gap, it names it as unbounded-until-v1.1 and attributes it to the regulator-side procedure. An institution-side reviewer reading the residual cannot misread it as institution-owned because the spec text explicitly says it is not. This is the model I want for residual-risk attribution.

The §6 residual-risk register includes an additional row — R8 ("Session key leakage to logs") — that touches the `key_fingerprint` offline-grindability concern: "The public per-entry `key_fingerprint` is NOT offline-grindable due to spec §10.6 IKM minimum (32 bytes per RFC 4868) — an attacker observing chain entries cannot brute-force the IKM from the fingerprint at acceptable cost." That is the right framing for the public-fingerprint exposure surface; the 32-byte IKM minimum makes the fingerprint computationally non-recoverable. Closing this concern at the §10.6 layer rather than at the verifier layer is the right architectural choice.

## Summary verdict

**0 gaps. 0 partials.** The supply-chain depth I came in to test is present at the right granularity:

- SLSA L3 institutional consumption (the deployment-gating control with `slsa-verifier` as a co-equal trust artifact) is normatively required, not aspirational. The trust-anchor table includes the SLSA-verifier public key alongside cosign and GPG. The examination surface propagates the requirement.
- Mirror-registry WORM with 7-year retention and continuous-monitoring (DE.CM-09) closes the silent-failure gap I came in expecting to find. The acceptable WORM backends are named explicitly. The continuous-monitoring control is the same-shape pattern as the spec §10.1 fingerprint reconciliation.
- GPG-key recovery procedure with the dual-compromise edge case is comprehensive. The cold-disaster-recovery key is pre-provisioned. The institution-side operational continuity (use the historical binary against historical seals, pause new deployments) is the right multi-week-recovery posture.
- Cosign-key recovery during active examination ties cleanly to the deployment-gating control from the SLSA L3 section. The GPG-fallback interim posture with `bundleverify --gpg-only` and post-recovery re-validation for evidence stability is the right examination-continuity story. The examination working papers carry both during-recovery and post-recovery results for completeness.
- The 36-hour triage matrix handles the concurrent-multi-scenario rollup rule, the vendor-hosted per-institution determination split, and the CIRCIA-only-without-FFIEC asymmetric cases. Per-scenario clock-start triggers are explicit.
- Adversary I framing carries examiner-laptop hygiene as a regulator-side control with explicit "out of scope for the institution's CC6 evaluation" language that prevents institution-side reviewers from misreading the residual as an institution-owned control gap.

Documentation refinements I would carry into a v1.1 remediation plan:

- Name a default mirror-reconciliation cadence (daily) with explicit institution-side option to tune per-pull.
- Name a cold-disaster-recovery key health-check cadence (annually, signing a no-op test artifact) so the cold key's operational health is known before it is needed.
- Add a "delayed determination" / re-classification rule to the 36-hour triage matrix for scenarios whose disposition changes mid-investigation.
- Pin a `slsa-verifier` version posture as an institution-side complementary control in the trust-anchor table.

None of the four refinements above is a gap or a partial in the v1.0 spec text. They are operational refinements an institution-side procurement team or a v1.1 spec author would address.
