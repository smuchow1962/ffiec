## What stands out from a cyber-risk lens

This is the first chain-of-custody corpus I have read where the supply-chain trust path is documented to a depth I would actually accept in a SOC 2 Type II vendor file. The combination of (a) signed spec PDF + signed conformance corpus + signed binary, (b) explicit cosign-key recovery procedure with named timelines, (c) named GPG rotation cadence with the NIST SP 800-57 anchor, (d) SLSA L3 claim mapped to in-toto attestation with a path to slsa-verifier, and (e) explicit two-pattern handling for mirror-registry signature preservation — these together close the four supply-chain questions a Big Four advisory would otherwise have to write up as findings. The fact that the cosign and GPG signing roles are split across separate small groups, with the project's SOC 2 report on the release pipeline available for vendor-file consumption, is the level of governance maturity I expect from a paid commercial vendor, not from an Apache-2.0 project.

The IR playbook with Scenarios 7–10 plus the 36-hour triage matrix is the second standout. Most chain-of-custody products I have evaluated treat "verifier failed" as a single alert class. Splitting the alerts by spec section step number (step 7 → unknown_key_version, step 8 → key_fingerprint mismatch, step 9 → MAC mismatch, step 10 → Merkle mismatch, step 11 → signature mismatch) and giving each one a named scenario with explicit clock-start triggers is the operational maturity that actually reduces cyber-risk posture. The triage matrix with the per-scenario default disposition is what the bank's IR program will paste into its runbook; the explicit "does not start the 36-hour clock unless …" qualifier on the operational-failure scenarios prevents the over-reporting failure mode I see frequently.

The threat-model expansion to R9–R13 plus Adversary I closes the residual-risk register against my standard FFIEC checklist. R9 (cross-tenant HKDF input collision) is the kind of risk that historically lives in a footnote and surprises the institution at examination; here it is explicitly closed by spec property + per-entry fingerprint + verifier step 8. R13 (algorithm-confusion at the seal layer) is forward-looking against the post-quantum transition that is going to land somewhere in v1.x — closing it now is cheaper than closing it during the transition. Adversary I (examiner-side tooling subversion) is named first-class, which matches the §2.4 attack approach in the overview and prevents the catalog gap I look for.

## Strengths from the cyber-risk lens

- **Four-primitive composition with explicit "without the key / with the key" decomposition** (overview §2.3). This is the framing a CISO can defend to a board: HMAC catches the attacker who does not have the key; HSM-rooted Merkle catches the attacker who does. Two failure modes, two different defenses, named explicitly.
- **BYOC IAM matrix in §6.1 of the ledger design and §32 of the BYOC guide.** The matrix names every resource and every actor — bank chain-ops, vendor support, vendor's image running in the bank account — and answers yes/no for each cell. This is the artifact a vendor-management lead can hand to procurement; nothing else I have seen for this product class is at that level of specificity.
- **Mirror-registry signature handling with both signature-preserving and re-signing patterns explicitly addressed** (supply-chain §132). The re-signing pattern with the documented bridging requirement ("conditional on project's cosign signature having validated at mirror-pull time, recorded in the mirror's audit log") is exactly the right framing — institutions that re-sign typically lose the upstream trust anchor without realizing it; this puts the bridging requirement on the institution and gives the examiner the audit-log artifact to verify.
- **Cosign-key recovery procedure with named timelines** (supply-chain §77). 24-hour project response, 48-hour institution notification, 7-day new-key authentication chain. The procedure also addresses the historical-binary case ("binaries signed under the revoked key remain verifiable for historical audit purposes") which is the question I would otherwise raise in a Big Four review.
- **GPG rotation cadence anchored to NIST SP 800-57** (supply-chain §69). 36-month default for the master key, 12-month subkey rotation, 7-day emergency rotation, 30-day governance-event rotation. The anchor to NIST SP 800-57 is what makes this defensible to a federal examiner — "we picked 36 months because NIST says so for offline keys" is a stronger position than "we picked 36 months."
- **SLSA L3 claim mapped to GV.SC-04 and ID.RA-09** (supply-chain §238). The cybersecurity examiner working a CSF assessment can use the SLSA L3 claim as cited evidence for the named CSF outcomes; this is the integration I would otherwise write up as a gap.
- **Spec text and conformance corpus integrity binding** (supply-chain §259). The cosign-signed spec PDF + cosign-signed corpus tarball + GPG-signed content-hash manifest closes the corpus-subversion attack class explicitly; the recommendation to rebuild the corpus from spec text rather than rely on the published tarball is the defense-in-depth posture a security-mature institution will appreciate.
- **HSM unavailability framing with the dual notification path** (spec §4.3.1, IR Scenario 5). 72-hour SHOULD-notify for sealing delay is the operational SLO; the cyber-incident 36-hour clock starts independently when the unavailability is associated with a security incident. The two notification paths are explicitly distinct, which means the institution does not over-report routine HSM failover and does not under-report a security incident hidden under a sealing delay.
- **Triage-tree branching in IR Scenario 7** (key_fingerprint mismatch). Four triage cases — rotation-in-flight, restored-backup, registry-restored, unauthorized-substitution — with explicit clock-start guidance per branch. Most fingerprint-mismatch alerts I have seen handled in production turn out to be operational misconfigs; treating them all as 36-hour incidents creates IR fatigue and erodes the bank's ability to recognize the real signal. This branching framework is the right cyber-risk posture.
- **Long-dwell adversary section in the IR playbook** (§352). Explicit acknowledgment that the chain detects modification at action time, not observation time, with the recommendation to compose with UEBA, anomalous-access detection, and intel-feed correlation. This is the framing that prevents the "we have a chain, so we don't need EDR on the master-key custodian" failure mode.

## Questions

### 1. Mirror-registry re-signing pattern: how does the institution prove the bridge invariant held historically?

Supply-chain §132's re-signing pattern requires the institution to record the project-side cosign signature having validated at mirror-pull time. The institution's IR playbook treats a missing log entry as a Scenario 3 finding. My question is forward-looking: when the FFIEC examiner samples the bridge in three years against an image deployed two years ago, where does the validation log live, and how is its integrity bound? Mirror registries (Harbor, ECR, ACR, GAR) emit audit logs to standard cloud-logging targets; those logs are not chain-bound and are subject to log-rotation, retention, and (in some configurations) operator deletion. The bridge invariant is asserted but the evidence trail is the institution's standard log retention. Two refinements would help: (a) name the minimum log-retention duration (matching the binary's deployment lifetime plus regulatory retention; suggest 7 years to match chain retention), and (b) recommend the institution's mirror-log retention be on append-only / WORM storage, not standard log rotation, since the bridge invariant rides on it.

### 2. SLSA L3 provenance verification at the institution: what does the institution do with `verifier.intoto.jsonl`?

Supply-chain §238 lists the SLSA L3 properties and says institutions MAY verify the published provenance attestation against `slsa-verifier`. From a cyber-risk lens this should be MUST-tier for institutions claiming the SLSA L3 evidence at examination — otherwise the institution is consuming the SLSA L3 claim without exercising it, which a GV.SC-04 examiner will challenge. Recommendation: the supply-chain doc should specify (a) the institution MUST verify the in-toto attestation against `slsa-verifier` before deploying a new release version, (b) the institution's SLSA-verification log is a control-evidence artifact retained for the same period as the binary's deployment, (c) the institution's `slsa-verifier` binary itself is signed and trust-anchored alongside the verifier (if the institution validates the chain's verifier with cosign but runs an unsigned `slsa-verifier`, the supply-chain trust path has a gap). Naming `slsa-verifier` and treating it as a co-equal trust artifact would close this.

### 3. GPG-signed manifest: what is the master-key-holder's posture for the GPG offline key, and how is it validated by the institution?

Supply-chain §66 says the GPG key is offline-held by a separate small group from the cosign-key holders. Cosign-key recovery procedure §77 is well-specified; the GPG-key recovery procedure is not separately spelled out, only the rotation cadence (§69). My question: if the GPG key is compromised (or merely suspected), what is the project response procedure? The cosign-key recovery procedure has 24/48/7-day timelines; an analogous procedure for GPG-key recovery is implied but not documented. Two specific risks: (a) if the GPG holder departs (the §69 30-day governance-event rotation triggers), what does the institution do with the previous 36 months of GPG-signed manifests during the rotation window? (b) if the GPG key is suspected compromised, the project's only fallback is the regulator-held public-key fingerprint — but the regulator-held fingerprint is the cosign public key, not the GPG public key (§104 trust-anchor table). The institution's fallback path collapses to "trust the project's announcement of the new GPG key" if the cosign key is also compromised — the two-trust-anchor independence claim relies on the cosign and GPG holders not being subverted simultaneously, which is reasonable but the institution-side procedure for the dual-compromise edge case is not documented.

### 4. Adversary I residual: what does "examiner laptop hygiene" look like as a control description?

Threat-model §189 names Adversary I (examiner-side tooling subversion) and recites the defense — three independent compromises (cosign, GPG, regulator-held fingerprint) plus `bundleverify` validation, plus the verifier's no-network-call build constraint. The residual is "examiner laptop hygiene (separation of duties, dedicated audit machines) is a residual risk the chain itself does not defend against; mitigation is examiner-IT." From a cyber-risk lens this is the right scope — chain-of-custody is not endpoint security — but the FFIEC examiner reading this corpus is going to ask the bank "what is your examiner-laptop hygiene control?" The answer is the FFIEC's own program, not the bank's. Recommendation: note explicitly that the residual is regulator-side and reference (or cross-link to) the FFIEC's examiner-laptop standard if one is published, or note that this is a regulator-side control out of scope for the institution's CC6 evaluation. Otherwise the institution's SOC 2 reviewer may misread the residual as institution-owned.

### 5. Cosign-key recovery during an active examination: what is the bank's continuity posture?

Supply-chain §77 cosign-key recovery procedure is project-side. The bank's posture during the recovery window (24 hours response, 48 hours notification, 7 days new key) is implied but not documented: what does the bank do if the cosign-key revocation notice arrives mid-examination, when the FFIEC EIC is on-site validating verifier signatures? The IR playbook's Scenario 3 references the supply-chain procedure for binary-signature failures, which is the right pointer, but the bank-continuity-during-recovery procedure is the gap. Recommendation: a paragraph in the IR playbook or the supply-chain doc covering: (a) the bank's verifier remains usable against historical seals during the cosign-key recovery window (because the historical binary's signature was valid at deployment time, recorded in the bank's reproducible-build log), (b) the bank pauses new-version verifier deployment until the new cosign key is available and validated, (c) if the examination is mid-engagement, the bank communicates the supply-chain incident to the EIC and offers the GPG-fallback validation as the interim posture. The procedure exists implicitly across the corpus; pulling it into one paragraph would close the operational ambiguity.

## Bottom line for the cyber-risk lens

The corpus, as I read it, is examinable as a cyber-risk control today against an FFIEC IT examination, with the five open questions above representing operational refinements rather than structural gaps. The supply-chain trust path is the strongest I have evaluated for this product class. The IR playbook and 36-hour triage matrix is the operational artifact a CISO can hand to the IR program lead and have it work. The threat-model residual register, with R9–R13 plus Adversary I, closes the catalog gaps I check for.

If a top-25 US bank's vendor-management lead asked me to write the third-party risk assessment for adopting this chain spec, I would write a green-rated assessment with the five open questions logged as enhancement requests for v1.1, not as adoption blockers. The supply-chain artifact set in particular — signed spec, signed corpus, signed binary, signed provenance, named recovery procedures, named rotation cadence — is the level of artifact maturity that closes the SOC 2 vendor file in a single review cycle rather than the typical three-cycle back-and-forth.

The five questions are honest enhancement asks. None of them changes my assessment that this is examinable, defensible, and operationally mature for first-engagement use at any institution operating an FFIEC-supervised AI program.
