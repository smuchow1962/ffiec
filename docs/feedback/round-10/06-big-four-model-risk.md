### Questions

**Q1. Is the `gen_ai_parameters` attribute, as expanded in §4.4, sufficient for SR 11-7 reproducibility — i.e. can a validator pull a chain entry two years later and re-run the agent's LLM call closely enough to evaluate the decision under effective challenge?**

**Status:** Answered

Spec §4.4 defines `ffiec.chain.gen_ai_parameters` as JCS-canonical JSON of model sampling and reproducibility parameters, with a substantive RECOMMENDED contents enumeration that materially closes the SR 11-7 reproducibility surface. The enumeration covers six categories that together constitute what a validator needs to re-run an LLM call inside an agent flow:

- decoding parameters (temperature, top_p, top_k, seed, max_tokens, stop_sequences, presence_penalty, frequency_penalty, repetition_penalty);
- sampler implementation identifier (vendor + library version);
- model identifier and version, request-side AND response-side, citing the OTel `gen_ai.request.model` / `gen_ai.response.model` semconv with the explicit operational note that vendors silently re-route between model versions during outages;
- system-prompt content or content-hash with reference to a prompt-version registry;
- retrieval context (RAG document IDs and content-hashes);
- intra-run data dependencies expressed as `(run_id, seq)` of earlier chain entries whose outputs fed this LLM call.

Each of those six is the right item, named at the right level of normative force ("RECOMMENDED contents for chain entries representing a model call" — strong enough to drive institutional schema decisions, deferential enough that the institution determines the schema). The closing sentence — *"For chain entries that represent a model call, `gen_ai.request.model` and `gen_ai.response.model` SHOULD be present (their integrity is bound through the OTel envelope per §5)"* — picks up the model-routing concern that an OCC examiner asking "which model actually answered this call on the day in question" would otherwise leave hanging. The integrity binding through §5 is what makes the substrate auditable rather than narrative.

The MRM-committee brief picks this up appropriately in its updated "What the MRM Committee should ask" item 3, which now includes the parenthetical *"For SR 11-7 reproducibility, the chain integrity-binds whatever the institution puts in `audit.*` and `gen_ai_parameters`; the institution's `audit.*` schema is a committee-relevant decision and should be reviewed periodically against the SR 11-7 reproducibility surface."* That layering — the chain integrity-binds whatever is logged; the institution's schema decision is what is logged — is the load-bearing framing for the MRM committee. The brief gets it right.

The customer-dispute procedures doc (§"Reproducibility and the customer's 'would the AI have said the same thing on a different day' question") translates the same substrate to the customer-protection audience with the right institution-side answer template. That doc's sample language — *"The decision's full input set is recorded in our integrity-bearing audit trail, including the AI model's version, the decoding parameters, the system prompt, and any retrieved context. We can re-run the decision under the same inputs to evaluate whether the AI would produce the same output. Significant variation across re-runs (a phenomenon called model nondeterminism) is itself information our model-risk-management committee evaluates"* — names model nondeterminism explicitly. That naming matters: the MRM committee's reproducibility posture has to acknowledge that re-runs may not produce byte-identical outputs even with the same inputs, and the chain's role is to bound the input-side reproducibility, not to manufacture output-side determinism the underlying model does not provide.

What I would still note for an institution adopting v1.0 is that the field's contents are recommended, not required. An institution that captures only the named decoding parameters and skips the system-prompt content-hash and retrieval-context fields will pass conformance but will not satisfy SR 11-7 reproducibility for RAG-flavoured agent decisions. The audit-procedures doc could pick this up as a P-22 or similar — sample chain entries representing model calls, confirm the institution's documented `gen_ai_parameters` schema covers the SR 11-7 reproducibility surface, and confirm the schema is what the captured entries actually populate. That is an audit-procedure gap rather than a spec gap; the spec correctly leaves the schema to the institution and requires the integrity binding regardless.

**Q2. Do the linkage-field changes (parent_run_id, parent_seq, dag_parents now INCLUDED in canonical bytes per §5; handoff event normative schema in §8.1.2; verifier step 13 DAG topology resolution) close the cross-process and DAG integrity gaps for SR 11-7 effective challenge of multi-step agent decisions?**

**Status:** Answered

Three coordinated changes, taken together, close the gaps the rework left for cross-process integrity. I read each in turn.

**§5 inclusion of linkage fields under the MAC.** The canonical-form section now lists *"The cross-run linkage fields: `parent_run_id`, `parent_seq`, `dag_parents`. These describe the event's relationship to other events in other runs and are substantive evidence about the agent's decision graph; they are not chain-stamp fields. Including them in the canonical bytes binds the cross-run linkage under the per-event MAC, so an attacker who can write to the ledger after capture but before seal cannot rewrite the parent linkage without breaking the MAC."* Design doc §2.3 mirrors this with the same rationale. That is the right change at the right place. The cost is zero — the verifier already computes the MAC; including three optional attributes in the canonical bytes does not increase verification cost — and the property gain is that the parent-edge graph is now integrity-bound under the same per-event MAC the rest of the substantive payload enjoys. A server-side compromise that rewrote linkage attributes between capture and seal would now break the per-event MAC, which the verifier catches at §7 step 9. This is the property a SR 11-7 validator needs when reconstructing a multi-step decision two years downstream.

**§8.1.2 normative handoff event schema.** Design doc §8.1.2 now defines the handoff event as the first event in the receiving process's half of the run with required fields `audit.handoff.from_run_id`, `audit.handoff.from_seq`, `audit.handoff.from_payload_hash`, `audit.handoff.handoff_at`, and an optional `audit.handoff.from_host`. The schema is named under the `audit.*` namespace, which means it lands inside the canonical bytes (§5) and inherits the per-event MAC. The verifier behavior on missing or malformed handoff event is also normatively addressed: a first event without the `audit.handoff.*` fields is structurally indistinguishable from a regular event AS LONG AS its `prev_hash` matches the previous event's `payload_hash`, and a handoff event whose `audit.handoff.from_payload_hash` does not equal the entry's `prev_hash` is rejected at spec §7 step 6 (`chain link broken at seq N`). That correctly resolves the ambiguity case I would have flagged: the handoff is the moment a model-risk validator is most exposed, and the spec now binds the handoff fields under the same integrity property as the rest of the chain, with the verifier's existing structural check catching mismatch.

**Verifier §4.3.1 step 13 DAG topology resolution.** The verifier design now adds an optional step 13 (under default; FAIL under `--strict`) that walks each event with `dag_parents`, parses the comma-separated `(run_id, seq)` pairs, and looks up each parent in the same tenant's ledger. A missing parent (typo, SDK persistence failure, network drop, parent in another tenant) produces an anomaly under default mode and a FAIL with reason `dag_parents resolution failed` under `--strict`. The check is appropriately placed at the day level after step 10 because parent events may be in earlier days within the verification range. The PDF report's anomaly section (§5.1) lists `dag_parents resolution failures` as a reportable anomaly type, which means the institution's anomaly-evaluation process picks it up through the existing template (`docs/anomaly-documentation-template.md`).

The anomaly template carries the new failure modes correctly: the type list at line 28 of the template includes `key_fingerprint mismatch`, `unknown_key_version`, `format_version mismatch`, `hkdf_inputs_digest mismatch`, `master_key_rotation_observed`, and `audit_file_truncation_detected`, and the severity table at lines 100-106 assigns Critical/High/Medium/Low ratings consistent with the IR playbook scenarios. The template also adds the higher-bar evidence requirements for `key_fingerprint mismatch` (four required evidence items including IKM-roster row identification, change-management approval, re-run of the affected period, and reconciliation cross-check) and for `hkdf_inputs_digest mismatch` and `audit_file_truncation_detected`. That is the right operational discipline: a fingerprint mismatch is not "explainable as a network blip" and the template's stricter bar prevents the institution from waving it through.

The combination — linkage fields under the MAC, normative handoff schema, DAG topology resolution — gives an MRM validator the structural primitive AND the integrity binding AND the verifier-side detection for the cross-process and DAG-shaped decision graphs that production agent platforms increasingly produce. I would have expected this to land in v1.1; that it is in v1.0 is material for the AI-MRM position paper my firm publishes.

**Q3. Do the MRM-committee briefing materials (`docs/MRM-COMMITTEE-BRIEF.md`) — specifically the new "Three additional defensive properties from the v1.0-rework" section and the updated questions — give the committee chair what they need to oversee the v1.0 control at the right level?**

**Status:** Answered

The brief's new §"Three additional defensive properties from the v1.0-rework" lands what a chair without a cryptographic background can use. Three properties, each with the right framing:

- **Per-tenant cryptographic binding.** The text describes the binding as *"structurally prevented by the construction, not just operationally avoided"* and connects the failure mode to the named verifier output (`key_fingerprint mismatch`, "wrong key" not "data tampering") and to the operational evidence trail (the institution's weekly key-fingerprint reconciliation report, audit-procedures P-6). That is the right level: the chair understands that the integrity property is structural, the failure is precisely named, and the operational evidence is locatable.

- **Compile-time prevention of dev-key material in production.** The text explicitly names the two enforcement layers (build-time exclusion plus verifier strict-mode refusal) and characterises them as *"either alone would be a single point of failure"* — the defence-in-depth framing the chair uses when oversight conversations turn to "what stops the obvious failure mode." This is the property that closes the "misconfigured environment flag in production" question that examiners have started asking after recent industry incidents.

- **Constant-time comparison discipline.** Short, accurate, and at the right level: *"Side-channel attacks against the verifier process are closed at the cryptographic-discipline layer; no exposure during verification."* The chair does not need to know what constant-time comparison is to understand that the residual risk is closed.

The updated §"What the MRM Committee should ask" carries the operational consequences. Item 4 now reads *"How are master-key (IKM) rotation and key-version transitions managed? Specifically: walk us through the most recent key-fingerprint reconciliation report (the `master.reconciliation_completed` operational event), including any fingerprint mismatches during the period and how they were resolved (audit-procedures P-6)."* Item 6 now adds *"For any `key_fingerprint mismatch` finding: was the IKM-roster row identified, the change-management approval recorded, and the verifier re-run against the corrected roster?"* — the four-item evidence bar from the anomaly template, expressed as a question the chair can ask. Item 7 now points at the new IR playbook scenarios for `key_fingerprint mismatch` (Scenario 7), `unknown_key_version` (Scenario 8), and `audit_file.truncation_detected` (Scenario 9). Item 3 adds the parenthetical on `audit.*` schema review, which is the load-bearing decision for SR 11-7 reproducibility (per Q1).

What the brief still does not surface is *where* each primitive lives. The four-primitive list mentions placement for primitive 2 (Merkle seal at the ledger server, on the bank's perimeter) but does not for the others. The closing paragraph after the three additional properties names the placements for the chair, which addresses my prior concern, but the primitive-by-primitive table does not. This is a minor formatting polish: a "where it lives" column on the four-primitive table would let the chair scan all four placements at once. I would not block on it.

The brief is now what the chair needs to oversee the control. The v1.0 reading is materially more useful to an MRM committee than what was previously on the table.

**Q4. Do the customer-dispute procedures (`docs/customer-dispute-procedures.md`) — specifically ECOA chaining of the translation step (§"Adverse-action notices") and IKM access for customer-side verification (§"IKM access for customer-side verification") — handle the chain evidence appropriately given v1.0?**

**Status:** Answered

Two procedural items, both substantively closed.

**ECOA chaining of the translation step.** The procedures doc now states *"The translation is itself a chain entry, with the AI's original response as the parent (per spec §4.4 `parent_run_id` / `parent_seq` linkage). This is the institution's choice but it is the recommended posture: a translation logged outside the chain is repudiable in the same way a vendor's logs are repudiable, which is exactly the gap the chain exists to close. The MRM committee opines on whether the institution chains the translation step or logs it outside the chain; the recommended decision is to chain."* That is the right framing on three counts. First, it names the linkage primitive (`parent_run_id` / `parent_seq`) the institution uses to chain the translation, which makes the recommendation actionable. Second, it cites the model-risk concern (a translation logged outside the chain is repudiable) in the language of the rest of the corpus. Third, it correctly identifies the decision as the MRM committee's, not the chain operations team's — the chain provides the substrate; the committee opines on the institution's posture. Combined with the linkage-fields-under-the-MAC change (§5; per Q2), the chained translation now inherits the per-event MAC integrity binding. The translation step is no longer a hole in the institution's customer-dispute response.

**IKM access for customer-side verification.** The new §"IKM access for customer-side verification" addresses the verifier-side failure mode I would have expected to surface in the first contested customer dispute. It names the two acceptable shapes — protective-order disclosure (litigation context) and HSM-mediated verification (operational context) — with the right thresholds for each:

> The institution's customer-dispute procedure MUST name which shape it operates and the threshold at which it shifts (typically: operational shape for CFPB inquiries and regulator complaints; protective-order shape only when the dispute escalates to litigation). Without naming the shape, the institution risks the customer's expert encountering `unknown_key_version` at step 7 mid-verification, which leaves the verification incomplete and the dispute unresolved.

That is precisely the failure mode — the verifier reports `structurally consistent, key-bound verification skipped` per §7 fail-closed semantics, which is technically a successful structural verification but does not give the customer's expert what they came for. The MUST-name-the-shape requirement, combined with the institutional-decision framing (the MRM committee opines on the chosen shape per its overall risk tolerance for IKM disclosure), correctly places the operational decision before the dispute starts rather than during it. The annual-review framing (the institution documents the decision in its control description and reviews it annually) keeps the decision alive in the institution's governance cycle.

The §"Reproducibility and the customer's 'would the AI have said the same thing on a different day' question" subsection (covered in Q1) closes the third procedural concern an MRM director would have raised — the customer's reproducibility question, distinct from the integrity question. The institution's response template ("we can re-run the decision under the same inputs to evaluate whether the AI would produce the same output. Significant variation across re-runs is itself information our model-risk-management committee evaluates") is the right model-risk framing for a customer-protection audience.

The procedures doc is now what an MRM director would want their customer-dispute desk to operate from.

**Q5. Does the AI-policy-alignment doc (`docs/regulator-pack/ai-policy-alignment.md`) — Treasury RMF expansion, EU AI Act Article 14 + Article 26, DORA Articles 6 + 8, and OCC supervisory posture — reflect current policy at the level a senior MRM director would defend in front of cross-jurisdictional examiners?**

**Status:** Answered

Four expansions, each landing the alignment story correctly.

**Treasury AI RMF expansion.** The §"U.S. Treasury AI Risk Management Framework (Feb 2026)" subsection now carries a four-row composition table (model inventory, model lifecycle controls, third-party model governance, ongoing monitoring) with chain-side composition for each. The model-inventory row makes the right operational point — *"every model that produced a decision is enumerated by the `gen_ai.request.model` field on the integrity-bound canonical bytes"* — which an OCC examiner asking "show me your AI model inventory" can be walked through directly. The ongoing-monitoring row picks up the integrity property's contribution to drift analysis (*"removes a class of 'did the vendor's logging system drop or alter events' doubts"*), which is the framing my firm uses in its annual position paper. The closing sentence — *"the Treasury framework's expectations are not invented in v1.0 but are recently consolidated, and the chain's evidence base directly supports them"* — is the right defensive posture for an MRM director who is not over-claiming Treasury endorsement.

**EU AI Act Article 14 and Article 26.** The Article 14 row now reads *"The Act's Article 14 specifically requires effective human oversight: the chain's `gen_ai_parameters` (decoding parameters), `audit.*` (institutional payload including system prompt and retrieval context), and `gen_ai.request/response.model` (model identifier) are the substrate that makes Article 14 oversight effective. The institution configures `audit.*` schema to ensure the human reviewer has the input set the AI actually used."* That is the right substantive expansion. Effective human oversight is exactly where Article 14's enforcement focus has been landing (the EU AI Act guidelines on human oversight are explicit that oversight without sufficient information is non-compliance), and the chain's contribution is precisely the substrate, not the oversight itself.

The Article 26 row is now present and correctly cites the three load-bearing sub-articles (Art. 26(5) monitoring, Art. 26(6) logging with the 6-month retention floor, Art. 26(11) informing affected persons) with the chain composition for each. The 6-month retention floor citation is important because institutions adopting the chain often default to FFIEC-style 7-year retention without realising EU AI Act Art. 26(6) sets a separate floor that may apply for cross-jurisdictional deployments. Naming it in the table prevents that operational gap.

**DORA Articles 6 and 8.** The DORA section now includes Art. 6 (ICT risk-management framework) and Art. 8 (identification of ICT-supported business functions) with substantive composition for each. The Art. 6 framing — *"The chain is one component of that framework; the institution's ICT risk function references the chain's threat model, the IR playbook, and the operational events catalog as the chain-specific contributions to the institution's documented framework. The chain is not the framework itself; it composes within it."* — is the correct disclaim. The Art. 8 framing — *"AI agents in production are ICT-supported business functions; the chain's tenant-and-deployment topology (per-tenant ledger, BYOC and vendor-hosted variants) is the integrity-bearing record of which ICT systems support which business functions. The institution's Art. 8 register references the chain's per-tenant scope."* — connects the chain's per-tenant scope to DORA's identification register, which is the operational integration point my firm's DORA practice asks about first. The closing paragraph naming the Art. 6 + Art. 8 + Art. 17 + Art. 28 composition is the framing the MRM director uses with the institution's ICT risk function.

**OCC supervisory posture.** The new §"OCC emerging supervisory posture on AI" is honestly bounded. It says explicitly that the OCC has not issued formal new AI-specific guidance and that AI model-risk supervision currently operates under SR 11-7 / OCC Bulletin 2011-12. The three sub-bullets cite specific OCC Bulletin sections (§III Model Validation, §IV Governance) plus the trend in supervisory letters since 2024, with chain composition for each. The closing-paragraph framing for an MRM director defending chain adoption — *"The chain is the technical control implementing the integrity-of-AI-decision-evidence requirement under the existing SR 11-7 / 2011-12 framework. We adopted the chain to position the institution for current and emerging OCC expectations, including expectations the OCC has signalled through supervisory letters to peer institutions."* — is exactly the framing I would want in front of an OCC examiner. It does not over-claim ("the OCC has required this") and does not under-claim ("the OCC has not addressed this"); it accurately characterises where the OCC's posture sits in May 2026.

The "v1.x candidate trigger" sentence at the end (formal new OCC AI-supervisory issuance triggers a spec working-group review) is the right governance posture: the doc is calibrated to current policy and commits to a refresh trigger when policy moves, without speculating about what that policy will say.

The doc as a whole is what a senior MRM director defends in front of a cross-jurisdictional examination. The expansions in v1.0 close the gaps that an MRM director's reading of the prior version would have flagged, and the framing throughout is appropriately bounded — composing rather than over-claiming, naming load-bearing institution-side decisions rather than absorbing them into the chain's claims.

## Cross-cutting observations

Three observations cut across the questions and matter for the MRM committee chair, the customer-dispute desk, and the cross-jurisdictional defence stance my firm advises on.

**The chain is now substantively load-bearing for SR 11-7 reproducibility, not just for logging integrity.** The v1.0 reading is that `gen_ai_parameters` with the expanded RECOMMENDED contents, plus `audit.*` with the institution's documented schema, plus the linkage fields now under the MAC, plus the OTel `gen_ai.request/response.model` integrity binding, together capture the SR 11-7 reproducibility surface for an LLM call inside an agent flow. The institution's adjacent decision (the `audit.*` schema) is still load-bearing — the chain integrity-binds whatever is logged; what is logged is the institution's choice — but the chain primitive set is now sufficient for the institution to make that decision well. That is a step change from "the chain is a logging-integrity control adjacent to SR 11-7" to "the chain is a load-bearing input to SR 11-7 reproducibility."

**The cross-process integrity story is now complete.** Production agent platforms increasingly fan out across processes (orchestrator + worker, pre-process + LLM + post-process, sandboxed tool execution) and increasingly use DAG-shaped flows. The v1.0 changes — linkage fields under the MAC, normative handoff event schema, verifier step 13 DAG topology resolution — give the MRM validator the structural primitive AND the integrity binding AND the detection mechanism for the multi-process and DAG cases. There is no gap that requires institution-side compensating ETL or out-of-band reconciliation to close.

**The committee-facing materials, the customer-dispute desk, and the cross-jurisdictional regulator-pack are now in alignment with the substantive control.** In a prior reading the committee brief understated the defensive primitives, the customer-dispute desk did not address ECOA chaining or IKM access for customer-side verification, and the policy-alignment doc undersurfaced Treasury RMF and DORA. v1.0 closes all three. An MRM director can now hand the brief to the committee chair, the procedures doc to the customer-dispute desk, and the policy-alignment doc to the regulator-relations team without needing to brief over the gaps. That alignment is what makes the chain operationally usable in an institution's AI-MRM program rather than just technically conformant.

The headline assessment for the firm's annual position paper: the v1.0 chain corpus is sufficient for an institution to adopt as a load-bearing input to its AI model-risk framework, with the institution's adjacent decisions (the `audit.*` schema and the IKM-disclosure shape for customer disputes) appropriately surfaced as institution-side choices the MRM committee opines on at adoption and at annual review.
