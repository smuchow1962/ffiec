## 3. Threat model — adversary catalog defensibility

The threat-model doc (`09-threat-model.md`) covers nine adversaries (A through I) plus thirteen residual risks (R1-R13). I read the catalog three times against the FFIEC IT Examination Handbook's adversary taxonomy and against the recent CIRT (Cyber Incident Response Team) adversary vocabularies. Three observations.

**Adversaries A through G map cleanly to standard categories.** External attacker on the wire (A), insider with database access (B), insider with seal-job credentials (C), HSM physical attack (D), vendor supply-chain compromise (E), application process compromise (F), master-key exfiltration (G). These are the standard adversary categories an FFIEC cyber examiner expects to see. Each has explicit capability, goal, defense, and residual-risk statements. Adversary C in particular — the seal-job-operator collusion case — is the case most institutions under-document; the doc gives it first-class treatment with separation-of-duties as the primary defense and independent audit + regulator verification as the long-cycle defense. That matches how I would write up the same adversary in a cyber-specialist examination.

**Adversary H (cryptographic break) and the post-quantum readiness roadmap (lines 96-117).** The four-row trigger-and-response table is the kind of multi-year migration framework cyber-specialist examiners ask about. The 30-day emergency spec-patch SLA at lines 110-111 is a project-side governance commitment, not an institution-side operational commitment, but it bounds the institution's exposure window — when NIST publishes a deprecation timeline or a practical attack on Ed25519 is demonstrated, the institution's response is bounded by the project's response window. The dual-algorithm transitional-period framing at lines 113-117 closes the algorithm-confusion attack class (R13 in §6) at the spec layer rather than as a runtime check. This is the right place to close it.

**Adversary I (examiner-side tooling subversion) at lines 189-211 and the institution-side reception procedure.** This adversary is the one I would scope hardest in cyber fieldwork. The reception procedure for regulator-fingerprint rotation at lines 199-207 (five steps, three operational events, an institution-side validation tooling stake) is the load-bearing operational discipline. Without the documented reception procedure the institution risks accepting a forged rotation notice, failing to update the verifier, or under-instrumenting the trust-anchor lifecycle. The IR Scenario 11 sub-variant for "trust-anchor reception failure" (incident-response-playbook.md lines 283-297) is the operational tie-in. The 36-hour-clock-start trigger for case 1 (forged notice confirmed) is on the matrix at incident-response-playbook.md line 352 and matches the cyber examiner's expectation that a forged trust-anchor rotation attempt is a CIRCIA-relevant substantial cyber incident.

**The line at 209 on examiner-laptop hygiene.** "Examiner-laptop hygiene is a regulator-side control, NOT an institution-side control." This is exactly right. I would not write this finding into an institution exam; the FFIEC's own examiner-IT program owns examiner-laptop posture. The doc names this explicitly so an over-eager institution-side reviewer (the SOC engagement partner) does not mis-scope it as an institution-owned gap. From the cyber-specialist perspective this is correctly framed.

**Residual-risk register R1-R13 at lines 238-252.** Thirteen risks with likelihood, impact, and mitigation status. R8 (session-key leakage to logs) is the one I would probe in fieldwork — the institution-side controls (no key logging, key handles only) are operational discipline that is hard to evidence at scale. The note at R8 about the public per-entry `key_fingerprint` not being offline-grindable due to spec §10.6 (32-byte IKM minimum per RFC 4868) is the right defensive framing — it closes the attacker's "brute-force IKM from observed fingerprints" path at the spec layer rather than relying on the institution's log hygiene.

## 4. IR playbook — cyber-specialist usability

The IR playbook (`incident-response-playbook.md`) is the document an institution's IR program operationalizes during an incident. From the cyber-examiner perspective I evaluate two things: (a) does the playbook handle the full chain-detection signal set, and (b) does the 36-hour-clock-start determination logic match the FFIEC computer-security incident notification rule.

**Chain-detection signal coverage.** Twelve scenarios (1 through 12, with 11 and 12 added in this rework). Each scenario names trigger, investigation, containment, remediation, notification. The scenario-to-clock-start mapping at lines 340-353 is the matrix the IR Commander uses during incident response.

**Scenarios I would probe specifically.**

- **Scenario 7 (key_fingerprint mismatch) at lines 201-225.** The triage tree (rotation-in-flight, restored-backup, registry-restore, suspected unauthorized substitution) is operationally realistic. Three of four root causes are operational misconfigurations, not security incidents; only the fourth (suspected unauthorized substitution) starts the 36-hour clock. The doc gets this right — most fingerprint-mismatch alerts in production will be triage cases 1-3, and an IR program that auto-starts the 36-hour clock on every mismatch alert wastes regulator notification and dilutes the institution's signal-to-noise. The clock-start trigger at line 224 is the load-bearing determination point.

- **Scenario 11 sub-variant (forged rotation notice) at lines 283-297.** The three-cause triage (forged notice, regulator-side operational error, institution-side tooling defect) maps to the operational reality of trust-anchor lifecycle. Case 1 (forged notice confirmed) starts the 36-hour clock and is a CIRCIA-relevant substantial cyber incident per the matrix at line 398. Cases 2 and 3 do not start the clock. The out-of-band cross-check posture at lines 295-296 (contact the regulator's primary IT examination liaison via a separate authenticated channel) is the right operational posture. As an FRB Atlanta examiner I would want to be on the institution's pre-documented out-of-band contact list before any incident — that pre-coordination is what the institution's IR program SHOULD pre-document, and an institution that has not pre-coordinated will burn hours during an actual incident hunting for the right liaison.

- **Scenario 12 (co-signed seal failure case (e)) at lines 299-313.** The three-branch triage tree (published algorithm break, per-algorithm signing-key compromise, under investigation) is the dispatcher for the 36-hour clock during the dual-algorithm transitional period. Branch (i) — published algorithm break — does not start the 36-hour clock because the deprecation event is regulator-coordinated and the un-broken algorithm's signature still provides integrity assurance. Branch (ii) — per-algorithm signing-key compromise — starts the clock at credibility determination. Branch (iii) — under investigation — starts the clock at investigation conclusion within a bounded 48-72h window. The bounded window prevents indefinite clock-deferral, which is the operational pattern an FRB cyber examiner watches for in dual-algorithm-transitional-period institutions. Without the bounded window an institution could defer "we're still investigating" indefinitely; the 48-72h commitment closes that.

**The 36-hour-clock-start matrix at lines 340-353 is correctly framed for the FFIEC computer-security incident notification rule.** The rule's 36-hour clock starts at the institution's *determination* that a computer-security incident affecting safety/soundness has occurred, not at the alert. The matrix names what triggers the determination per scenario. From the cyber-specialist perspective this is the right reading of the rule. Two examples worth calling out:

- Scenario 5 (sealing delay > 72h) does NOT start the 36-hour clock by default; it is operational. The doc's posture at line 346 is right — a sealing delay in isolation is a SHOULD-notify operational event under spec §4.3.1 (72-hour SHOULD), not a 36-hour computer-security incident.
- Scenario 6 (software-key fallback in production) does NOT start the 36-hour clock by default; it is a control failure. Per spec §10.7 the production build is supposed to exclude the software-key adapter at compile time; this scenario fires when the exclusion is bypassed. The clock-start trigger at line 347 — "UNLESS the fallback's presence is associated with confirmed unauthorized signing" — is the right escalation path. The doc handles this scenario the way an FRB cyber examiner would: a configuration drift, not a cyber incident, until evidence of unauthorized signing surfaces.

**The CIRCIA matrix at lines 388-403.** CIRCIA's "substantial cyber incident" threshold is broader than the FFIEC rule's "safety/soundness" threshold. Some events trigger CIRCIA without triggering FFIEC; some trigger both; some trigger neither. The matrix names the disposition per scenario. The "Counsel works the matrix per incident" note at line 403 is the right framing — the matrix is institutional guidance, not a substitute for counsel's judgment. From the cyber-specialist perspective the matrix is usable as a pre-determined institutional checklist that counsel finalizes per incident.

**Edge cases at lines 359-401 — the rollup rule, cross-tenant scope discovery, vendor-vs-institution responsibility split, federal-regulator routing per institution charter, dual-algorithm verifier-version timing-overlap.** These five edge cases are the operational realism that distinguishes a competent IR playbook from a checkbox playbook. Two specifically:

- The federal-regulator routing table at lines 367-374 with charter-type-to-CFR-citation mapping. The "primary federal regulator" the FFIEC 36-hour rule notifies depends on charter type. National banks notify OCC at 12 CFR Part 53. State member banks (FRB-supervised, my own portfolio) notify FRB at 12 CFR Part 225 App. F. State non-member banks notify FDIC at 12 CFR Part 304 Subpart C. State counterparts notify the state banking regulator per state-specific timing. The institution's IR program SHOULD pre-document the routing — and the doc names this as a SHOULD at line 376. The pre-documented routing is what the IR Commander confirms at incident time.

- The dual-algorithm verifier-version timing-overlap at lines 378-384. When the institution upgrades to a verifier supporting algorithm Y but the regulator's verifier still supports only algorithm X, posture-disagreement scenarios result. The load-bearing case is the third row (institution: FAIL case (e); regulator: PASS under X-only verifier) — the regulator's X-only verifier is structurally blind to the Y-algorithm validation, and the institution must proactively notify the regulator and supply the case-(e) verifier output as supplementary evidence. This is operational reality during the dual-algorithm transitional period that an FRB cyber examiner would not anticipate without the doc's framing. The institution-side coordination posture at line 386 ("pre-coordinate verifier-version compatibility BEFORE the multi-year transitional period begins") is the right operational discipline.

## 5. Supply-chain — trust-path posture

The supply-chain doc (`supply-chain.md`) is the document that handles GV.SC (cybersecurity supply chain) evidence. I read it for trust-anchor independence, signing-role separation, dual-compromise edge case, and SLSA L3 institutional consumption.

**Two-trust-anchor independence at lines 58-95 (cosign + GPG).** Cosign-key holders are a separate small group from GPG-key holders within the project's release-management role. The separation prevents a single compromise from collapsing both trust paths. Both trust anchors have rotation cadences (cosign on its own cadence, GPG default 36 months per NIST SP 800-57 guidance for offline signing keys, GPG subkey 12 months, GPG emergency 7 days on compromise). The institution caches both public keys out-of-band as fallback. Recovery procedures are symmetric: cosign-key recovery is signed by GPG; GPG-key recovery is signed by cosign. This is the standard two-trust-anchor pattern.

**Dual-compromise edge case at lines 97-103.** Cosign AND GPG simultaneously compromised. The project's cold-disaster-recovery key is the third independent trust anchor, held by yet another separated small group, never used until a dual-compromise event. The institution's emergency response — stop deploying new releases, continue using historical verifier against historical seals, coordinate directly with primary regulator on examination posture during the multi-week recovery — is the right operational posture. The historical binary's signatures remain validatable against the pre-compromise cached cosign public key, so the institution's existing examination evidence does not collapse during the recovery window. From the cyber-specialist perspective this is a defensible operational continuity posture.

**Cold-DR key lifecycle at lines 104-119.** 60-month rotation cadence (5 years), held by a third separated small group, annual dry-run cadence with documented `KEY-DR-DRYRUN-{year}.asc` attestation. The institution-side consumption procedure at lines 111-119 (download attestation, validate signature against cached public key, archive in control-evidence repository, fail-to-obtain-within-30-days triggers IR Scenario 11 branch (c)) is the load-bearing institution-side discipline. As an FRB cyber examiner I would specifically sample-test the institution's `KEY-DR-DRYRUN-{year}.asc consumption log` against the institution's archived attestations per the binding at CSF-2.0.md line 91. The doc gets this right: the dual-compromise fallback must be operationally exercised, not just documented; without the institution-side consumption procedure, a GV.SC-04 examiner would challenge un-tested control evidence.

**SLSA L3 institutional consumption at lines 296-325.** Publishing the SLSA provenance attestation is necessary but not sufficient — the institution MUST consume it. Four operational requirements at lines 318-321: validate `verifier.intoto.jsonl` against `slsa-verifier` BEFORE deploying a new release version (deployment-gating control); SLSA-verification log retained for binary's deployment lifetime (typically 7 years); `slsa-verifier` itself signed and trust-anchored alongside the verifier (a co-equal trust artifact); `slsa-verifier` public key cached in the institution's trust-anchor table out-of-band. The "which tool, what version, what trust anchor, archived where" framing at line 322 is exactly the documentation question I would ask in fieldwork.

**Mirror-registry signature handling at lines 178-202.** Two patterns (signature-preserving mirror, re-signing mirror with documented bridging). The re-signing pattern's bridge invariant — "the mirror's re-signature is conditional on the project's cosign signature having validated at mirror-pull time, recorded in the mirror's audit log" — depends on the audit-log retention and integrity properties at lines 197-200 (7-year minimum retention, WORM storage). The continuous-monitoring control at line 202 (`mirror.reconciliation_completed` operational event firing when the mirror silently stops validating project signatures) is the same control pattern as spec §10.1 fingerprint reconciliation, applied to the mirror's signature-validation discipline. This is the right operational discipline for an institution running the re-signing pattern. The doc's posture at lines 195-196 (re-signing without documented bridging is non-conformant) is correctly framed — examiners verify which pattern the institution operates and confirm the bridge documentation if re-signing is in use.

**Cosign-key recovery during an active examination at lines 123-132.** Operational continuity posture during the cosign recovery window. The bank's verifier remains usable against historical seals (historical signatures validatable against pre-compromise cached public key); the bank pauses new-version verifier deployment until new cosign key is available; the GPG fallback (`bundleverify --gpg-only`) is the interim posture; post-recovery re-validation against new cosign key documents evidence stability across the trust-anchor change. This is the operational posture I would expect of a competent institution mid-engagement.

## 7. HSM custody and FIPS conformance

The cloud-HSM-guide (`cloud-hsm-guide.md`) provides the per-cloud provisioning discipline. From the cyber-specialist perspective the load-bearing content is the conformance matrix at lines 16-27 (which products are FIPS 140-2 L3 conformant and which are not), the per-cloud provisioning steps, and the pitfalls section.

**Conformance matrix at lines 16-27.** AWS CloudHSM Classic, AWS KMS Custom Key Stores backed by CloudHSM, Azure Managed HSM (Premium tier of Key Vault), Azure Dedicated HSM, Google Cloud HSM, on-prem options (Thales Luna, Entrust nShield, Utimaco, YubiHSM 2) — all conformant. AWS KMS default tier (L2 only), Azure Key Vault Standard (L1), Azure Key Vault Premium legacy SKU (partial; L2 default with L3 available), Google Cloud KMS software keys — all non-conformant. The matrix is reproducible by an examiner against the FIPS validation certificate registry. This is the right level of detail for a cyber-specialist examination — I would not need to validate the matrix from scratch; I would confirm the institution operates one of the conformant options and pull the FIPS validation certificate number from the institution's control description (per the validation discipline at lines 162-167).

**Pitfalls at lines 178-183.** Mixing HSM tiers, sharing keys across tenants, backup keys without replication, PIN management drift. These are the four operational mistakes I have seen institutions make. The doc names them explicitly and the institution's control description SHOULD address each. Pitfall #1 (mixing tiers — a tenant with two HSM keys, one at L3 and one at L2, has the L2 as the weakest link) is the one I would specifically probe in fieldwork.

**HSM custody from spec §10.5 at lines 388-393.** "The signing key MUST be held in a FIPS 140-2 Level 3 (or higher) HSM, with the private key non-extractable. Acceptable cloud HSM products at v1.0 publication include AWS CloudHSM Classic, Azure Managed HSM (and Azure Dedicated HSM), and Google Cloud HSM. AWS KMS at default tier and Azure Key Vault Standard are not conformant." The seal-job operator role MUST grant `sign` only; `extract`, `delete`, and `import` MUST require separate authorization. Separation of duties between seal-job operator and HSM administrator is REQUIRED at institutions where role separation is operationally feasible; small institutions MAY use documented dual-control as a compensating control.

The dual-control compensating-control posture for small institutions at line 392 is the operational realism that distinguishes a usable spec from a tier-1-only spec. Community banks frequently cannot operate a separate seal-job operator and HSM administrator role; documented dual-control is the right compensating control. From the cyber-specialist perspective this is the kind of proportionality I would expect to see in an institution's control description with size-appropriate framing.

## 8. Edge and federated AI deployment patterns

The edge-and-federated-AI doc (`edge-and-federated-ai.md`) covers four deployment patterns: edge AI, federated learning, multi-agent systems with autonomous coordination, on-device inference. From the cyber-specialist perspective the load-bearing content is the edge master-key custody patterns (Pattern A vs Pattern B), the multi-agent DAG-semantics handling, and the residual-risk handling for edge-device physical compromise (R12).

**Pattern A (edge-derived session keys) vs Pattern B (bulk session-key issuance) at lines 30-37.** Pattern A is more secure (no batch leak); Pattern B is simpler operationally for fleets without secure-enclave capability. Institutions choose based on the device fleet's hardware. The institution documents the pattern in its control description per the operational guidance at line 141.

**R12 (edge-device physical compromise) at threat-model.md line 251.** "Mitigation deferred to v1.1: edge / federated patterns work in v1.0 but assume secure-enclave-attestation-or-equivalent at the edge node. Until v1.1 ships explicit physical-attacker handling, edge deployments document the residual risk and operate compensating controls (Pattern A: per-device IKM in TPM/secure-enclave; reconciliation cadence baseline tuned per fleet)." The deferred-to-v1.1 posture with documented compensating controls is operationally honest — the spec does not over-promise on edge-device physical-compromise handling, and the institution's compensating controls (Pattern A's secure-enclave assumption + per-fleet reconciliation cadence) are the institution-side discipline that bounds the residual.

**Multi-agent DAG semantics at lines 81-100.** Each agent is a chain participant with its own session key; agent-to-agent communication is captured as cross-run references (parent-child or DAG); the verifier reconstructs the topology at examination time. The pattern accommodates arbitrary coordination topology. From the cyber-specialist perspective the substantive evidence is the cross-run linkage fields (`parent_run_id`, `parent_seq`, `dag_parents`) being included in the canonical bytes per spec §5 — meaning an attacker who can write to the ledger after capture but before seal cannot rewrite the parent linkage without breaking the MAC. The substantive integrity property holds across multi-agent topologies.

**Edge fleet reconciliation considerations at line 27.** "Session-key-id reconciliation on edge devices may show 'unmatched' entries during disconnection windows; the institution baselines the unmatched count for the edge fleet separately from cloud." This is the operational reality of edge fleets — disconnection windows produce reconciliation noise that the institution baselines per fleet. An institution that operates edge AI without a per-fleet reconciliation baseline will produce noisy reconciliation evidence that obscures genuine `key_fingerprint` mismatches.

## 9. Operational requirements (spec §10) — the institution-side operational floor

Spec §10 is the operational floor that an institution-adopting-the-spec commits to. From the cyber-specialist perspective, eight subsections matter: §10.1 reconciliation cadence, §10.2 operational events, §10.3 append-only enforcement, §10.4 time synchronization, §10.5 HSM custody, §10.6 IKM minimum length (32 bytes), §10.7 software-key adapter compile-time exclusion, §10.8 constant-time comparison.

**§10.1 reconciliation cadence at lines 351-357.** "Institutions SHOULD operate key-fingerprint reconciliation at no more than weekly cadence." The reconciliation bounds the master-compromise detection window to at most one week, plus the time between observation and remediation. Institutions with elevated risk profile MAY operate reconciliation at higher frequency (daily, hourly, or continuous). The reconciliation evidence is emitted as `master.reconciliation_completed` with `unmatched_count`. The audit-procedures cross-check (P-6) is the institution's substantive evidence trail.

**§10.2 operational events at lines 359-373.** Twelve event categories (ledger lifecycle, seal job lifecycle, chain integrity, audit file integrity, HSM operations, configuration, master-key events, reconciliation, regulator-held fingerprint lifecycle). Operational events MUST be retained at least as long as the chain events they relate to. From the cyber-specialist perspective these events are the substantive evidence trail for CSF DE.CM-09 (continuous monitoring) and DE.AE-02 / DE.AE-03 (adverse event analysis and correlation).

**§10.3 append-only enforcement at lines 375-382.** Two layers: application level (codebase contains no UPDATE or DELETE statements) and database role level (writer role granted INSERT and SELECT only; UPDATE, DELETE, TRUNCATE revoked). The Merkle seal catches deletion regardless of which layer it occurs at; role-level enforcement is defense in depth. From the cyber-specialist perspective the role-level enforcement is what I would specifically probe in fieldwork — a database role with TRUNCATE granted to the writer is a finding, regardless of application-level invariants.

**§10.6 IKM minimum length (32 bytes) at lines 394-400.** RFC 4868 §2 requires HMAC-SHA-256 keys to match the hash output size (32 bytes) for full security; shorter IKMs degrade HMAC strength. The public 16-byte `key_fingerprint` is offline-grindable against a low-entropy IKM — an attacker observing chain entries could brute-force the IKM from the fingerprint if the IKM had less than ~64 bits of entropy. The 32-byte minimum makes this attack computationally infeasible. Institutions SHOULD enforce at IKM-provisioning time (refuse to register an under-length IKM) and MUST enforce at SDK-configure time. This is the right operational floor; the public-fingerprint-not-grindable property at threat-model.md R8 depends on §10.6 holding.

**§10.7 software-key adapter compile-time exclusion at lines 402-410.** "The software adapter MUST be excluded from production release builds at compile time. Run-time environment-variable gating is NOT sufficient — a misconfigured deployment that flips the flag must NOT be able to bring the software adapter online in production." The adapter stamps every chain entry with `kms_handle_uri = "plaintext-dev"` (or another agreed prefix beginning with `"plaintext-"`); the verifier refuses the produced chain under `--strict` mode. The double-protection is a regulator-visible line: development-only key material cannot accidentally ship to production via configuration drift. From the cyber-specialist perspective the compile-time exclusion is the load-bearing control — a misconfigured deployment that bypasses the run-time flag would be caught at the verifier, but the compile-time exclusion prevents the misconfigured deployment from existing in the first place.

## 10. Spec §7 step 11 dual-algorithm dispatch and §7 step 12a GenAI completeness check

Two specific spec items deserve cyber-specialist callouts.

**§7 step 11 cases (a)-(e) at lines 322-332.** The dual-algorithm transitional-period dispatch. Cases (a) both signatures valid (PASS), (b) single-algorithm signature during dual-algorithm posture (PASS-WITH-ANOMALY, control-completeness), (c) algorithm not on declared posture list (FAIL under strict; PASS-WITH-ANOMALY under non-strict), (d) single-algorithm posture default (PASS / FAIL), (e) co-signed seal failure with one valid + one invalid (FAIL under strict; PASS-WITH-ANOMALY under non-strict).

The load-bearing case is (e) at line 330. The severity is Severe regardless of bracket. The PASS-WITH-ANOMALY disposition under non-strict reflects the spec's posture that the un-broken algorithm's signature still provides integrity assurance for downstream consumers, NOT that the failure is itself low-severity. Examiners writing up case (e) findings cite `regulator-pack/finding-language.md` row "11 (dual-algo) co-signed seal failure" (Severe MRA) regardless of which bracket the verifier reported. This is exactly the right framing for an FRB cyber examiner — the verifier disposition is not the severity, the case identification is the severity. Case (e) indicates EITHER (i) one of the algorithms has been broken OR (ii) one of the seals is forged under a compromised algorithm-specific signing key. The verifier does NOT attempt to interpret which case applies; the institution's IR program does the interpretation per IR Scenario 12. The working paper records BOTH the valid-algorithm validation and the invalid-algorithm failure so the regulator has the full picture.

The examiner working-paper convention at line 332 — "the verifier output MUST record both algorithms' validation results (PASS / FAIL / NOT-PRESENT per algorithm) when the seal record carries `signatures`. The examiner's working paper carries both rows; the examination report cites both algorithm validations" — is the load-bearing examiner discipline. As a cyber examiner I would cite both algorithm validations regardless of which bracket the verifier reported; the working paper preserves the full picture for the regulator's record.

**§7 step 12a GenAI model-identifier completeness check at line 335.** For each chain entry that carries any attribute under the OTel `gen_ai.*` namespace prefix, assert that BOTH `gen_ai.request.model` and `gen_ai.response.model` are present and non-empty. Missing either reports `gen_ai_model_identifier_missing at seq N`. Under `--strict`: FAIL. Under non-strict: PASS-WITH-ANOMALY (control-completeness for SR 11-7 reproducibility, NOT chain-integrity). The check fires only on chain entries representing model calls; entries with no `gen_ai.*` attribute (tool calls, audit-only events) are unaffected.

The cyber-specialist relevance is the SR 11-7 reproducibility framing — the response-side model identifier is the load-bearing record because vendors silently re-route between model versions during outages, and without `gen_ai.response.model` the institution's MRM director cannot reproduce a specific decision under the model that actually answered. From the FRB cyber-specialist perspective this is operational reality I have observed in vendor-API deployments — the institution requested model X, the vendor's API answered with model Y due to capacity routing, the institution's audit trail recorded only model X. The response-side identifier closes that gap.

The inline-execution requirement at line 335 — "implementations MUST NOT defer it to a second pass; deferral makes the check observable-at-scale (memory-overhead and latency for the deferred queue) and is non-conformant" — is the right implementation discipline. A second-pass check would be observable in the institution's resource utilization at scale, which an examiner could detect indirectly through deployment-resource sampling. The inline check avoids that observation surface entirely.
