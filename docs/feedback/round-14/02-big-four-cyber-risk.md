## What I checked, and what I found

### 1. The construction against the adversary set

`00-overview.md` §2 names four attack approaches and ties each to the primitive that defends it. The mapping is the right shape for a regulator brief: an examiner who reads §2 alone has the threat model.

What I read for, specifically, was whether the four approaches cover the adversary set a Dutch G-SIB would name. They do. **2.1 host-side tampering** is the SDK-and-wire attacker, defended by the per-event HMAC. **2.2 cross-tenant or cross-version key confusion** is the multi-tenant misconfiguration class — the danger is operational, not adversarial, and the per-tenant HKDF binding plus per-entry `key_fingerprint` close it before any MAC compute (`spec §10.1` reconciliation bounds residual to one week). **2.3 server-side or privileged-insider history rewrite** is the DBA-with-IKM attacker, defended by HSM-signed daily Merkle seal — the load-bearing distinction "HMAC defends without the key, Merkle plus HSM defends with the key" is articulated explicitly in §2.3 and is the right framing for a Big Four review. **2.4 examination-time tooling subversion** is the supply-chain attacker against the verifier itself, defended by cosign + GPG + regulator-held fingerprint + offline binary + reproducible build, with three independent compromises required for silent forgery. The §2.5 "what is NOT in the four" list is honest about residuals (application-host IKM exfiltration collapses to 2.3; examiner-laptop hygiene is not the institution's control).

The threat model in `09-threat-model.md` §2 expands the four approaches into nine adversaries (A through I). The adversary catalog is complete; Adversary I (examiner-side tooling subversion) mirrors §2.4 and is named here for first-class catalog completeness — that is the right editorial choice. The institution-side reception-and-validation procedure for regulator-fingerprint rotation in §2.9 is the piece I would normally flag as missing in a v1.0 corpus, and it is here in concrete form: three operational events (`regulator_fingerprint.rotation_received`, `regulator_fingerprint.rotation_validated`, `regulator_fingerprint.installed`) with field-level schema and a reception-failure sub-variant that hands off to IR Scenario 11.

I have nothing to add to the construction-against-adversaries layer. It defends the set I would test against.

### 2. HSM custody, dual-algorithm posture, and cloud-HSM tiering

`04-hsm-custody.md` §3.2 is the pivotal section for a Big Four review. The institution is signing under per-tenant Ed25519 today and is committing to a dual-algorithm posture (Ed25519 + Dilithium or SLH-DSA) for the post-quantum transition. What I checked:

- **Per-tenant per-algorithm key isolation.** §3.2 specifies "one signing keypair per algorithm per tenant." Cross-tenant signing is structurally prevented; cross-algorithm replay is closed by the `algorithm` field bound into `sign_payload` per spec §4.3 (R13 in the §6 residual register). Good.
- **Algorithm-posture transition framing.** Provisioning the second algorithm's keypair, publishing the public key, updating declared algorithm posture (the configuration the verifier consults at spec §7 step 11) — this is a multi-step institution-side operation, and §3.2 frames it as such. The single → dual transition and the dual → single retirement are both named, with retirement gated on broken-algorithm timeline and regulator coordination.
- **Cloud-HSM tier conformance.** `cloud-hsm-guide.md` plus §3.2.1 disqualify AWS KMS default tier and Azure Key Vault Standard. Bank IT teams routinely confuse Azure Key Vault Standard with the Premium and Managed HSM tiers; the spec's L3-or-higher requirement disqualifies Standard and the conformance table makes the disqualification explicit. AWS KMS Custom Key Stores backed by CloudHSM are conformant because the FIPS validation flows through the underlying CloudHSM. This is the right level of cloud-tier specificity for the institution's third-party-risk file — a procurement officer reading the conformance table can refuse a cheaper tier without the security team having to re-litigate.
- **IKM rotation window.** §3.2.2 frames the rotation window correctly: both `key_version` values are valid during the window, the verifier resolves per-event via stamped `key_version`, and §10.10 names late-binding entries crossing the seal boundary as PASS-with-anomaly behavior. The seal record's `key_versions` list field handles the two-version day correctly. This is the operational reality at a tier-1 bank — application processes don't restart simultaneously, and the rotation window is bounded by the longest-lived process. Closed.
- **Cross-region key replication.** AWS CloudHSM, Azure Managed HSM, Google Cloud HSM all support cross-region replication with per-tenant key labels preserved. Per-tenant signing key remains isolated across regions; cross-tenant signing remains structurally prevented. Good.

This is a section I would lift into a board-level cyber-risk-committee brief without modification.

### 3. Supply chain — SLSA L3 institutional consumption and cold-DR-key governance

`supply-chain.md` is the section a DORA Article 28 reviewer reads first, and the section a SLSA-aware cybersecurity examiner reads first. What is here is unusually thorough for a v1.0 corpus.

- **SLSA L3 deployment-gating control (MUST-tier).** "Publishing the SLSA provenance attestation is necessary but not sufficient — the institution MUST consume it." The institution validates `verifier.intoto.jsonl` against `slsa-verifier` BEFORE deploying a new release; the validation is a deployment-gating control; the SLSA-verification log is retained for the binary's deployment lifetime; `slsa-verifier` is a co-equal trust artifact alongside cosign and the GPG manifest with its public key and binary SHA-256 cached out-of-band; the institution's control description names the SLSA-aware tool, version, trust anchor, and archive location in one place. This is the framing that turns SLSA from a publish-only claim into an operational control. A GV.SC-04 cybersecurity examiner sampling the institution's deployment pipeline has four answers in one place — exactly what a CSF-2.0 sampling protocol asks for.
- **Cold-DR-key lifecycle (project-side governance).** The dual-compromise edge case (cosign AND GPG simultaneously compromised) is named, and the cold-disaster-recovery key has its own documented lifecycle: 60-month rotation, third separated small group of holders, annual dry-run with `KEY-DR-DRYRUN-{year}.asc` published under standard cosign/GPG, and an annual hardware health check with out-of-band notification on deviations. This is the level of project-side governance I look for when evaluating an open-source standard for inclusion in a tier-1 bank's third-party-risk file. The pieces I would normally find missing — what happens when both primary trust anchors are compromised, who holds the recovery key, how do we know the recovery procedure works — are all present.
- **Institution-side consumption of the cold-DR-key dry-run attestation.** The `KEY-DR-DRYRUN-{year}.asc consumption log` is the operational evidence for CSF GV.SC-04. The institution downloads, validates against cached cosign or GPG, archives in control-evidence repository for the year, and treats a missed 30-day window as IR Scenario 11 branch (c). SOC and examination teams sample-test the consumption log against the institution's archived attestations. This is what I would normally have to draft as a control in the institution's own playbook; it is shipped in the standard.
- **Mirror-registry handling for re-signing pattern.** Two patterns named, both conformant if documented. The signature-preserving mirror is preferred (no additional trust anchor; trust path: project → mirror preserves bytes → deployment validates project signature). The re-signing mirror is acceptable but the institution MUST document the trust-path bridge and the bridge's audit-log retention (7 years minimum) on WORM storage, with a continuous-monitoring control (`mirror.reconciliation_completed` operational event) firing when the mirror silently stops validating project signatures. This is the level of detail a Big Four mirror-discipline review produces — and it is in the standard rather than in the institution's own controls binder.

The supply-chain document is the strongest section in the corpus for a DORA reviewer. What is shipped here is what the regulation expects from a critical ICT third party.

### 4. BYOC topology and the IAM permission matrix

`byoc-deployment.md` is the topology a Dutch tier-2 bank with multi-cloud strategy and a "no vendor data, no vendor keys" procurement posture uses. The IAM matrix is the document I would attach to the institution's vendor-management file.

The matrix's load-bearing assertion: "the vendor must not be able to reach the bank's HSM, master key, or event-payload data." The matrix encodes that with eleven resources × three roles (bank chain-ops, vendor support, vendor's image running in bank account). Vendor's support role has access only to redacted telemetry; vendor's image runs under bank-controlled IAM the bank can revoke unilaterally. Bank-controlled mirror registry pulls vendor images, validates cosign before storing; deployment pipeline pulls from the bank's mirror, never from the vendor's registry. Bank-controlled OTel Collector is the only egress for vendor support telemetry; redaction is enforced at the collector, not at the vendor's endpoint.

This is the right level of separation for a DORA Article 30 contractual-arrangement requirement. The four common pitfalls in the doc's "Common pitfalls" section (vendor pull credentials compromised, vendor image over-permissioned, HSM PIN leaks via vendor support telemetry, vendor support role drifts to broader access) are the four I would test on engagement; defenses are in place for each.

The exit posture in "When the vendor relationship ends" is also right — bank's data and keys remain bank-controlled; only the runtime image changes. The chain's open-source standard plus the bank-controlled keys make vendor switching tractable. This is what a procurement officer needs to hear: the bank's exit cost is bounded.

### 5. Incident response — Scenarios 11 and 12, the 36-hour matrix, the CIRCIA matrix, federal-regulator routing, and the dual-algorithm verifier-version-overlap row

This is the section I read most carefully because it is where most v1.0 corpora fail. They handle the obvious scenarios (chain mismatch, signature failure, master-key compromise) and leave the operational edges (project-side governance events, dual-algorithm transitions, multi-charter holding companies, CIRCIA-only triggers) implicit. This corpus does not.

- **Scenario 11 (project-side trust-anchor degradation, cold-DR fallback).** Three triggers named: failed dry-run, dual-compromise activation, missed dry-run window. Investigation, containment, remediation, and notification are all branched per trigger. Notification: does NOT start the institution's 36-hour clock by itself (this is project-side governance, not an institution-side cyber-security incident). Counsel decides on examination-posture communication if the institution is mid-engagement. Right disposition.

- **Scenario 11 sub-variant (forged or invalid rotation notice).** The reception-procedure failure path. Three plausible root causes (forged notice, regulator operational error, institution-side validation tooling defect). Containment: do NOT install the new fingerprint until validation succeeds. Out-of-band cross-check via separate authenticated channel. **Clock-start: STARTS the 36-hour clock at the institution's determination of forgery for case 1; does NOT start for cases 2 and 3.** This is the right triage — a forged rotation notice is an attempted trust-anchor substitution and deserves the cyber-incident framing. A regulator operational error is not.

- **Scenario 12 (co-signed seal failure, case (e)).** Three branches. Branch (i) — published algorithm break — no 36-hour clock (this is a known cryptographic-deprecation event the regulator is also aware of). Branch (ii) — per-algorithm signing-key compromise — clock starts at credibility determination per Scenario 4. Branch (iii) — under investigation — clock starts at investigation-conclusion within a bounded 48-72 hour window. The bounded window for branch (iii) is the right discipline; without it, an institution could indefinitely defer the clock-start by leaving the investigation "open." The institution's working paper records both the valid-algorithm validation result and the invalid-algorithm failure so the regulator has the full picture.

- **36-hour cyber-incident notification triage matrix.** Twelve scenarios with default disposition and clock-start trigger. The matrix is the institution's IR program's reference card. The discipline is "the determination point — not the alert point — starts the clock," which matches the FFIEC rule's intent. The matrix names what counts as the determination per scenario. Counsel decides per incident, but the matrix gives counsel a defensible starting point.

- **Concurrent multi-scenario alerts (rollup rule).** A Scenario 1 + Scenario 7 + Scenario 8 alert in the same 60-minute window may be a single root-cause event triggering three detection paths. The IR Commander treats the constellation as one incident and starts the clock at the earliest qualifying determination. This avoids both double-counting in regulator notification and under-counting where the constellation is the signal. The IR Commander documents the rollup decision. This is the operational reality at a tier-1 bank — multi-scenario constellations are common, and the right discipline is rollup, not parallel clocks.

- **Cross-tenant scope discovery during investigation (vendor-hosted topology).** In a multi-tenant deployment one investigation may produce N institution-side determinations on different timelines. Vendor's IR team produces the alert; each institution's IR program produces its own determination. Vendor's contractual notification SLA is a SEPARATE clock from the institution's 36-hour FFIEC clock — both apply, both are tracked. This is the right framing for a vendor-hosted topology; institutions who outsource to a multi-tenant vendor often misunderstand which clock controls.

- **Federal-regulator routing per institution charter.** The "primary federal regulator" depends on the institution's charter type. Six charter types, primary federal regulator, CFR citation, state-side cadence. National bank → OCC (12 CFR Part 53), federal-only. State member bank → FRB (12 CFR Part 225 App. F) plus state banking regulator (24-72 hours, state-specific). State non-member bank → FDIC (12 CFR Part 304 Subpart C) plus state banking regulator. Federal credit union → NCUA (12 CFR Part 748 App. B), federal-only. State credit union → NCUA plus state credit-union regulator. Insured depository subsidiary of FBO → OCC, FRB, or FDIC depending on chartering structure. **Multi-charter holding companies notify all applicable primary regulators at the affected entity's chartering structure.** This is the routing matrix the institution's IR program needs pre-documented BEFORE the incident, not constructed under time pressure during the incident. CFR citations are correct; state cadence is realistic at 24-72 hours with state-specific variation noted.

- **Dual-algorithm verifier-version-overlap row in the 36-hour matrix.** The institution has upgraded to a verifier supporting algorithm Y (post-quantum); the regulator's verifier still supports only algorithm X (Ed25519). A co-signed seal arrives at examination time. Three posture-disagreement scenarios. The load-bearing case is **Institution: FAIL case (e) (Y-algorithm validates, X-algorithm does not — X-key compromised or X-algorithm broken). Regulator: PASS under its X-only verifier (the regulator's verifier doesn't process the `signatures` list and only sees the X-algorithm signature).** The regulator's X-only verifier is structurally blind to the Y-algorithm validation. **The institution MUST proactively notify the regulator of the case-(e) failure and supply the institution's case-(e) verifier output as supplementary evidence.** This is the load-bearing reason the institution coordinates verifier-version compatibility BEFORE the transition begins. The regulator's IT examination program SHOULD upgrade to a Y-algorithm-aware verifier within 6 months of the institution's adoption. This is the regulator-side blind spot most v1.0 corpora handwave; this corpus names it as a load-bearing case and ships the institution-side mitigation (proactive notification + supplementary evidence + pre-transition verifier-version coordination). Right.

- **CIRCIA-only triggers without FFIEC trigger.** Some chain-detected events rise to CIRCIA's "substantial cyber incident" threshold (72-hour CISA path) without rising to FFIEC's "computer-security incident affecting safety/soundness" threshold. Eleven scenarios in the matrix (Scenarios 1-12 plus sub-variants) with FFIEC trigger and CIRCIA trigger noted independently. The Scenario 11 sub-variant (forged rotation notice confirmed) is "Yes / Yes" — a substantial cyber incident because an attacker attempted trust-anchor substitution, and a 36-hour FFIEC notifiable because it is suspected unauthorized substitution. Scenario 12 branch (i) published algorithm break is "No / No" typically — a cryptographic-deprecation event the regulator is also aware of; multi-institution scope might make it CIRCIA-relevant. **Counsel works the matrix per incident.** The institution's IR program SHOULD have CIRCIA notification on its standard checklist for chain-detected events even when the FFIEC clock has not started. This is the right disposition; CIRCIA's "substantial cyber incident" definition is broader than the FFIEC rule's "safety/soundness" framing.

The IR playbook is the strongest IR document I have read in a v1.0 cyber-risk corpus. The pieces I would normally have to write — federal-regulator routing matrix, dual-algorithm verifier-version-overlap row, the load-bearing case-(e) interaction with the regulator's X-only verifier, the rollup rule for concurrent alerts, the CIRCIA-vs-FFIEC matrix — are all here.

## Pieces I want to call out as load-bearing

For my own working papers and for the institution's third-party-risk file, the pieces I would highlight as load-bearing:

1. **The "no clock by default" framing for project-side governance events.** Scenarios 5, 6, 9, 10, 11, 12 branch (i) all default to "no 36-hour clock" with explicit conditions for when the clock starts. This avoids the failure mode where institutions over-notify on operational events and the regulator's signal-to-noise ratio degrades. The framing is the right discipline.

2. **The bounded 48-72 hour window for "under investigation" determinations (Scenario 12 branch (iii)).** Without the bounded window, an institution could indefinitely defer the clock-start. The bound is cited as an institutional commitment, not a regulatory requirement, but it is the right discipline.

3. **The reception-failure sub-variant for forged regulator-fingerprint notices.** This closes the institution-side trust-anchor-substitution attack vector. The institution's verifier configuration retains the previous fingerprint as the active trust anchor until validation succeeds; out-of-band cross-check via separate authenticated channel; case 1 (forged) starts the 36-hour clock at determination. Concrete, examinable, defensible.

4. **The structural blindness of the regulator's X-only verifier to the Y-algorithm validation in dual-algorithm transitions.** This is the case where the institution's verifier reports FAIL case (e) and the regulator's verifier reports PASS — and the regulator's PASS is structurally incomplete. The institution's proactive notification + supplementary evidence + pre-transition verifier-version coordination is the right institution-side mitigation.

5. **`slsa-verifier` as a co-equal trust anchor alongside cosign and the GPG manifest.** Most v1.0 corpora treat SLSA as a publish-only claim. This corpus operates `slsa-verifier` consumption as a deployment-gating control with its own trust-anchor discipline. For a GV.SC-04 cybersecurity examination, this turns the L3 claim from a checkbox into operational evidence.
