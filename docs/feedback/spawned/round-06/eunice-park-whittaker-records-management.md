**Reviewer:** Dr. Eunice Park-Whittaker, CRM, IGP
**Background:** 18 years at NARA, most recently Senior Records Officer for federal financial regulators. Now consults to Federal Reserve Banks, OCC-supervised banks, and state-chartered institutions on records-retention and disposition programs. Teaches a graduate seminar on ISO 15489-1:2016 and ISO 30300:2020.

## Framing — what the spec is, from a records-management vantage

I want to set out plainly what I think the spec is doing well before I name what is missing, because the framing matters for the gaps.

The chain-of-custody v1.0a specification is, in records-management terms, a very strong **integrity and authenticity substrate** for AI-decision records. It addresses two of the four ISO 15489-1:2016 characteristics — authenticity (the record is what it purports to be) and integrity (the record has not been altered) — at a level of cryptographic rigor I do not normally see in a banking-records specification. The HMAC-SHA-256 chain at capture, the daily Merkle seal, the FIPS 140-2 Level 3 HSM-rooted root signature, and the explicit failure-reason discipline at §7 step 1 through step 12a give an examiner exactly what an examiner needs to say "this record was not altered between capture and verification." The §10.6.1 IKM-generation requirements, the §10.5 HSM custody rules, and the §10.7 software-key adapter exclusion all close the kinds of gaps a records officer learns to look for after a few decades of bench experience.

The retention-justification document at `regulator-pack/retention-justification.md` is unusually careful for a v1.0 release. The "longer of" rule, the proportionality test against GDPR Article 5(1)(e), the documented quarterly retention audit, the legal-hold integration with the IKM-rotation freeze, and the post-deletion verification at 30 days — these are records-program disciplines, not just engineering discipline. The institution that wrote this knows what a records-management program looks like.

Where v1.0a falls short — and this is the thread that runs through most of my findings — is in the records-management vocabulary and the formal records-lifecycle accountability. The spec speaks fluent cryptography and fluent compliance citation, but it does not speak fluent records management. The words "records officer," "records schedule," "disposition authority," "certificate of destruction," "Federal Records Act," "GRS," "NARA," "ISO 15489," "ISO 16363," and "OAIS" do not appear in the spec, in any design document, in the operator guide, in the audit-procedures, or in the regulator pack. The retention-justification document gets to the right number (7 years) for the right reasons, but it does not close the program loop — there is no named records officer, no records schedule, no disposition certification, and no migration plan that survives across the 7- to 10-year retention horizon a federally-supervised bank actually operates under.

Below I name the eight findings. Each carries a tag, a one-line title, the substantive paragraph with the standards I am citing, and what would close it.

---

## Finding 1 — Gap — No named records officer, no records-program accountability

The Federal Records Act (44 U.S.C. § 3105) requires every federal agency to designate a records officer responsible for the agency's records-management program; for federally-supervised banks operating under 12 CFR Part 30 Appendix B, the analogous discipline is the institution's records-management program with named accountability. The chain-of-custody specification reaches all the way to FIPS 140-2 Level 3 HSM custody, IKM generation provenance, and seal-job operator role separation — and yet there is no named records officer in the operating model. The chain-operations team, the privacy-data-protection team, the SOC team, the legal team, and the HSM administrator are all named in the operator guide; the records officer is not. This matters because retention scheduling, disposition certification, hold release, and migration across format generations are records-program functions that do not naturally fall to chain operations, privacy, or legal. ISO 15489-1:2016 §6.3 and §7.2 describe the records-management responsibilities; ISO 30300:2020 §5.3 describes the records-management leadership accountability. **What would close it:** add a "Records officer" role to the operator-guide operating model, name the role in `retention-justification.md` §5 (the deletion procedure currently routes through the records-management team without naming an accountable individual), and add a records-officer attestation row to the §10 control-attestation matrix. The role does not have to be a new headcount at smaller institutions; it can be a designation on top of an existing privacy or compliance role, but the designation has to be explicit because Federal Records Act-style accountability is named accountability.

## Finding 2 — Gap — No records schedule, no disposition authority

A records schedule is the formal document that names each record series, names the retention period for that series, names the legal authority for the retention period, and names the disposition action at the end of the period. NARA General Records Schedule 4.2 (Information Access and Protection Records) and GRS 5.6 (Security Records) are the federal models. The retention-justification document in `regulator-pack/retention-justification.md` cites the right statutes (12 CFR 1002.12(b)(1) for ECOA, 31 U.S.C. § 3731(b) for the False Claims Act, FFIEC supervisory floor) and reaches the right composite period (7 years), but it is a justification for a retention period — not a records schedule. A records schedule has a different shape: it lists the record series (chain entries, seal records, IKM history, verifier output, operational events, key-fingerprint reconciliation evidence, deletion logs, hold documents) with each series's retention trigger, retention period, disposition action (destruction, transfer to NARA, transfer to long-term archive), and disposition authority. Under SEC Rule 17a-4 (broker-dealer records, 17 CFR 240.17a-4), CFTC Rule 1.31 (commodity records, 17 CFR 1.31), and FINRA Rule 4511 (general books and records), a records schedule is the document the regulator asks for first. **What would close it:** ship a records schedule alongside `retention-justification.md` — one row per record series, columns for retention trigger, retention period, disposition action, disposition authority. The records schedule references the retention-justification for the legal grounding; the retention-justification stops being a hybrid document and reverts to its proper role as a legal-grounding analysis.

## Finding 3 — Gap — No disposition certification, no certificate of destruction

When records reach end of retention and disposition is destruction, the institution's records officer issues a certificate of destruction. The certificate names the record series destroyed, the volume destroyed, the destruction method (cryptographic erasure for the privacy-store mapping, in this case), the destruction date, the records officer's signature, and a witness signature. The §5 deletion procedure in `retention-justification.md` describes step-by-step what happens at the 7-year boundary — the records-management team identifies the batch, the legal team confirms no hold, the privacy-data-protection team deletes the mapping rows, a deletion log is recorded, a 30-day verification confirms permanent deletion. This is excellent operational hygiene, but it is not a certificate of destruction. The deletion log captures the operator-side fact ("operator X deleted N rows on date Y"); a certificate of destruction is the records-officer-signed attestation that disposition was authorized, lawful, and complete. ISO 15489-1:2016 §9.10 names the disposition documentation requirement; NARA's standard practice for federal-records destruction requires the certificate in writing. **What would close it:** add a step 7 to §5 of `retention-justification.md`: "the records officer issues a written certificate of destruction naming the batch, the volume, the destruction method, the date, and the records officer's signature; the certificate is retained for the longer of 7 years or the institution's records-schedule retention for disposition documentation." A template certificate of destruction in `docs/templates/` (alongside `fre-902-certification.md`) would let the institution operationalize this without designing it from scratch.

## Finding 4 — Partial — Legal-hold operations are documented, but hold release is informal

The litigation-hold operations described in `operator-guide.md` lines 498-520 and §6 of `retention-justification.md` are nearly complete. The hold opens with a written document carrying hold identifier, scope, source, estimated duration, review date, and issuing counsel's signature. The hold suspends end-of-retention deletion for the held subset and freezes IKM rotation for the affected period. The hold's effect on the chain integrity story is well thought out — append-only enforcement plus IKM-rotation freeze means the held records remain verifiable in the state they were held in. What is partial is the hold-release leg. `retention-justification.md` §6 says "when the hold is released, the records-management team resumes the standard quarterly retention audit." That is operationally right but procedurally thin. A hold release is a records-program event analogous to a hold opening: counsel signs that the hold is released, the records officer acknowledges, and the records-program log records the release with the same fields as the hold opening (release identifier, effective date, scope released, source closing the hold, releasing counsel's signature). Without a parallel structure on release, an opposing party in later litigation can argue the hold was implicitly released without authorization, or that records were destroyed before authorization to release. ISO 15489-1:2016 §9.9 and FRCP 37(e) practice both expect symmetric hold-release documentation. **What would close it:** extend `retention-justification.md` §6 with a "Hold release" subsection mirroring the hold-opening fields, and add a `hold.released` operational event to §10.2 of the spec alongside the implicit hold-opening events the chain-operations team already emits.

## Finding 5 — Gap — Migration plan across the 20-year-plus practical retention horizon

The retention-justification document names a 7-year retention period and the §6 legal-hold extension can push that out further on a per-record basis. In practice, federally-supervised banks routinely operate records under hold for 10, 12, even 15 years (False Claims Act actions, multi-year regulator inquiries, complex consumer-litigation cycles). And the chain ledger itself, even when its privacy-store mapping is deleted at 7 years, continues to exist as an integrity-bound record for as long as the institution chooses to retain it. The cryptographic primitives the chain depends on — Ed25519, HMAC-SHA-256, SHA-256, JCS canonicalization — have known and unknown obsolescence horizons. SHA-256 has a documented post-quantum collision concern; Ed25519 has a documented Shor's-algorithm exposure; FIPS 204 (Dilithium) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) are named in the spec as future migration targets but the spec does not set out a migration plan. The dual-algorithm transitional period in §7 step 11 is well thought out as a *verification-time* construct, but it does not address how the institution will actually re-seal a 12-year-old chain when SHA-256 is deprecated by NIST. ISO 14721:2012 (OAIS reference model) §5.1 and ISO 16363:2012 (trustworthy digital repository) §5.4 both require a documented preservation plan that names format-migration triggers, format-migration procedures, and format-migration evidence preservation. The spec has none of this — it names format_version "v1" and gestures at a v2 dispatch, but does not say what happens to a 2026 chain when v3 is released in 2034 and SHA-256 is deprecated in 2036. **What would close it:** add a `docs/preservation-plan.md` or extend the operator guide with a preservation-plan section naming (a) the cryptographic-primitive obsolescence-monitoring procedure (which body's deprecation announcements the institution monitors — NIST, BSI, CESG); (b) the migration trigger (typically NIST sunset announcement plus institution-specific buffer); (c) the migration procedure (re-seal under the new primitives while preserving the original seal as an integrity-bound parallel record); (d) the evidence retention for the migration itself (the migration certificate is itself a record series). Without this, the spec is a strong v1.0 substrate but the institution cannot answer the records-officer question "will this still verify in 2046?"

## Finding 6 — Partial — The four ISO 15489 characteristics are 3 of 4 covered, usability is implicit

ISO 15489-1:2016 names four characteristics a record must have: authenticity, reliability, integrity, and usability. The spec covers three of these explicitly. Authenticity comes from the per-tenant IKM, the HKDF-bound session key, and the per-event HMAC — the chain entry verifies as the entry the institution captured under that tenant's key. Reliability comes from the §10.4 NTP synchronization, the §4.2.2 receive-timestamp authoritativeness, and the deterministic verifier output format. Integrity comes from the HMAC chain plus daily Merkle plus HSM signature. The fourth characteristic — usability — is the one that says a record must be "located, retrieved, presented, and interpreted" by a competent reviewer over the retention horizon. The spec has the cryptographic side of usability (the verifier reproduces the integrity property) but the records-officer side of usability is implicit. There is no documented *retrieval* procedure for a single chain entry by customer ID, by tenant_id, by date range — the litigation-hold and FRCP 34 production posture in `operator-guide.md` describes producing a date-range package but does not define an indexed retrieval over the lifetime of the chain. There is no documented *presentation* posture — the chain entry's `gen_ai.completion.text` is captured as a hash by default, with the full text optionally retained per CC8.1 declaration; without the full text, the record may be integrity-bound but not usable as evidence in a consumer dispute or examiner inquiry. Indexing, retrieval, and presentation are records-program concerns, not chain-cryptography concerns. **What would close it:** add a "Retrieval and presentation" subsection to the operator guide naming the institution's index over the chain (typically a privacy-store-side index by customer_id and date), the retrieval SLA (records-program standard is "produce within 14 days of request" matching FRCP 34), and the presentation format (NDJSON for examiner consumption, PDF working-paper for litigation). The cryptographic substrate is sound; the records-program wrapper around it needs to be named.

## Finding 7 — Nit — FOIA/Privacy Act exemption posture is unstated

When a federally-supervised bank produces records to a regulator, the records frequently fall under FOIA exemption (b)(8) for bank examination records, exemption (b)(4) for confidential commercial information, and the Privacy Act protections for individually identifiable information. When a regulator subsequently faces a FOIA request for the bank's records, the regulator's FOIA officer has to determine which exemptions apply and consult with the originating institution. The spec does not name the FOIA-exemption posture for chain entries — the entries clearly carry confidential commercial information (model identifiers, prompt content, pricing, vendor information) and frequently personally identifiable information (customer identifier hashes, demographic-attribute hashes, decision rationale). The institution should have a documented posture that names which FOIA exemptions the institution will claim if its chain records are sought under FOIA, so the regulator's FOIA officer can act consistently. This is a nit at v1.0 because the institution can backfill it; it becomes a gap if the institution is operating in a regulator who routinely receives FOIA requests against its examination records. **What would close it:** a paragraph in `regulator-pack/finding-language.md` or `regulator-pack/examination-response-workflow.md` naming the institution's FOIA posture: chain entries are confidential commercial information per (b)(4); chain entries containing customer identifiers are protected by Privacy Act / GLBA Title V; chain entries that are part of an examiner's working papers are exempt under (b)(8). The institution names its FOIA contact and the procedure for the regulator to consult.

## Finding 8 — Confirmation — The retention-justification document is exemplary, with one structural note

I want to close with a confirmation rather than another gap, because I think `regulator-pack/retention-justification.md` is the strongest single document in the regulator pack from a records-management vantage. The "longer of" rule in §4, the proportionality test in §3, the explicit purpose-by-purpose retention analysis (adverse-action defense, examination evidence, regulatory inquiry response, civil-fraud defense), the post-deletion verification at 30 days, the annual review with named triggers in §8 — all of these are records-program disciplines I see done badly far more often than well. The composite 7-year period reached by the "smallest window that simultaneously satisfies the longest applicable supervisory record-retention floor and covers the common-case window of the federal civil-fraud statute of limitations" is the right answer for the right reasons. The structural note is that the document conflates two different documents — the legal-grounding analysis (which it does excellently) and the records schedule (which it gestures at but does not deliver). Splitting the document into a `retention-justification.md` (legal grounding) plus a `records-schedule.md` (record series, retention triggers, dispositions, authorities) lets each document do its job cleanly, and lets the records officer maintain the records schedule without re-litigating the legal grounding every annual review. This is Finding 2's "what would close it" expressed as a confirmation of what is already nearly there.

## A closing note on the cryptographic substrate, from a records-program vantage

I want to be clear about something that distinguishes my review from a cryptographic review or a SOC-attestation review. The cryptographic substrate of v1.0a is, on the evidence in front of me, very strong — strong enough that an examiner can stand in court and testify that a chain entry was not altered between capture and verification. That is rare in banking-records practice and the institution has earned it. The findings above are not findings against the cryptographic substrate. They are findings about the records-program scaffolding that wraps around the cryptographic substrate over the 7- to 20-year horizon a federally-supervised bank actually operates under. A cryptographically sound record that has no named records officer, no records schedule, no certificate of destruction, no migration plan, and no documented usability posture is an integrity-bound record without a records program around it. The institution is closer to a complete records program than it appears at first glance — the disciplines are mostly there, they are just not named in records-management vocabulary or organized into the records-program documents a records officer would expect to see. Closing the eight findings above would move v1.0a from "cryptographic substrate with retention discipline" to "records program with cryptographic substrate."

## Records-program posture — would I accept v1.0a as a records-management substrate?

Yes — with conditions. I would accept v1.0a as the records-management substrate inside a federally-supervised bank's recordkeeping program subject to four conditions, all of which the institution can satisfy in the v1.x window without changing the spec's normative content.

**Condition 1 — Designate a records officer.** The institution names a records officer in writing, ties the role into the operating model in `operator-guide.md`, and gives the records officer authorization to release legal holds, sign certificates of destruction, and approve format migrations. Without a records officer, the records-program accountability is implicit and a regulator inquiry will land on whichever team happens to be in front of the inquiry. With a records officer, the records-program accountability is explicit and the institution can answer the records-program questions on the first pass.

**Condition 2 — Ship a records schedule alongside the retention justification.** The institution publishes a records schedule listing each record series the chain produces, the retention trigger for each series, the retention period for each series, the disposition action for each series, and the disposition authority for each series. The records schedule is the document the records officer maintains and the regulator asks for first. The retention-justification document remains the legal-grounding companion to the records schedule.

**Condition 3 — Operationalize the certificate of destruction and the symmetric hold-release documentation.** The institution adds a written certificate of destruction to the §5 deletion procedure and a written hold-release document to the §6 hold procedure. Both are records-officer-signed. Both are retained for the longer of 7 years or the institution's records-schedule retention for disposition documentation.

**Condition 4 — Publish a preservation plan covering the cryptographic-primitive migration horizon.** The institution names which body's deprecation announcements it monitors (NIST), the migration trigger (NIST sunset plus institution-specific buffer), the migration procedure (re-seal under new primitives, preserve original seal as parallel record), and the migration evidence retention (migration certificate is itself a record series). The preservation plan is the institution's answer to the records-officer question "will this still verify in 2046?" The plan does not have to choose the post-quantum primitives now; it has to name the procedure that will choose them later.

Under those four conditions, v1.0a is a records-management substrate I would accept inside an OCC-supervised bank, a Federal Reserve member bank, a state-chartered institution under FDIC supervision, or a CFPB-supervised non-bank. The cryptographic substrate is doing the heavy lifting on authenticity and integrity; the records-program scaffolding above closes reliability and usability over the retention horizon. The institution should treat the four conditions as v1.x records-program work rather than v2 spec work — the spec's normative content does not need to change, only the records-program documents that organize the operating model around it.

I have signed many records-program acceptances over 18 years at NARA and a decade of consulting since. I would sign this one with the four conditions named in writing. The institution's records officer and I would together name the responsible parties, the document deliverables, and the dates. I would expect the four conditions to close inside 90 days of designation; the spec is close enough to a records program that the close-out is documentation work, not engineering work.
