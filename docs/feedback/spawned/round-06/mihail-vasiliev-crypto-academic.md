# First-look cryptographic review — FFIEC chain-of-custody v1.0a

**Reviewer.** Dr. Mihail Vasiliev, ETH Zürich (Cryptography), IETF CFRG.
**Reading angle.** Cryptographic engineering and long-horizon archival durability.
**Artifacts read.** `spec/chain-of-custody-v1.md` (§1-§7, §10), `docs/design/01-primitives-spec.md`, `docs/design/02-chain-construction.md`, `docs/design/03-merkle-seal.md`, `docs/design/07-verifier-design.md`, `docs/design/09-threat-model.md`, `spec/test-vectors/008-jcs-edge-cases/`, `spec/test-vectors/015-dual-algorithm-cosigned-seal/`.
**Persona note.** I have no prior exposure to earlier iterations. Everything below is observable from the shipped spec, design notes, and test-vector corpus.

---

## Overall posture before the findings

This is a competent FIPS-aligned construction. The primitive choices — HMAC-SHA-256 (FIPS 198-1), HKDF-SHA-256 (RFC 5869), RFC 6962 binary Merkle, Ed25519 (FIPS 186-5, RFC 8032), JCS (RFC 8785) — are the choices a CFRG reviewer would make in 2026 if they were starting from scratch. Each is mature, each has stdlib implementations in every target language, each has a mechanical conformance test. The `0x00` / `0x01` domain separators on Merkle leaves and internal nodes are straight RFC 6962 §2.1. Feeding `expected_prev_hash` rather than `entry.prev_hash` into the MAC recompute (spec §4.1 inviolate property #8) is the kind of engineering that survives ten years of maintainer churn. The JCS edge-case fixture (case 008) is unusually thorough — float canonicalization, NFC vs NFD, surrogate pairs, NaN rejection, UTF-16 code-unit key ordering all have byte-level expectations. Most specs that reach for RFC 8785 ship the integers-and-ASCII subset and discover float disagreement during the first cross-vendor sample.

The findings below are not about the primitives. They are about the substrate's readiness for the 20-50 year retention horizon the spec implicitly targets.

## Findings

### Finding 1 — `[Confirmation]` Ed25519 is the right signature primitive for v1.0

Ed25519 is the correct call. Three reasons. (i) FIPS 186-5 (February 2023) admits Ed25519, putting it in the same compliance bucket as ECDSA-P256 and RSA-PSS for federally-supervised systems. (ii) Deterministic signatures (RFC 8032 §5.1.6 — the nonce is `SHA-512(prefix || message)` rather than fresh random) eliminate the catastrophic-nonce-reuse failure mode that has cost ECDSA deployments private keys repeatedly; the Sony PS3 and Bitcoin random-k incidents are canonical. The seal job's idempotency story (design 03 §4.4) leans on this — retry produces the same signature byte-for-byte, which makes recovery trivial. ECDSA retry would either produce a different valid signature or require persisting nonce state across crashes. (iii) Ed25519 has 128-bit classical security at 32-byte public keys and 64-byte signatures, conservative against any pre-quantum attack model. The §4.3 requirement that the verifier enforce strict canonicalization per RFC 8032 §8.4 closes the malleability hole older libraries (libsodium pre-1.0.16, several Java implementations) historically exposed — most FIPS-validated HSMs already enforce this on the signing side, but mandating verifier-side strictness is the right belt-and-braces. **Closure status: nothing to close.**

### Finding 2 — `[Confirmation]` Domain separation, JCS, and the Merkle construction are textbook-correct

Three things land cleanly and deserve to be named. First, the RFC 6962 leaf and internal-node prefixes (`0x00` for leaves, `0x01` for internal) are present in design 03 §2 and explicitly stated as second-preimage defenses in spec §4.2. Without those prefixes, an attacker who controls one node's `payload_hash` value could in principle present an internal-node hash as if it were a leaf at the next level, producing a different ordered set with the same root — RFC 6962 §2.1 closes this and the spec inherits the closure verbatim. Second, the empty-day root `SHA-256(b"") = e3b0c44298fc...` is consistent with RFC 6962 §2.1 (MTH of an empty list); the streaming-Merkle empty-input contract in design 03 §3.1 is explicit that a `nil` return is non-conformant, which catches the most common library mis-port. Third, the JCS test vector (008) exercises ECMAScript Number-to-String conversion, NFC vs NFD divergence, UTF-16 code-unit key ordering, NaN/Infinity rejection, and the `\u00XX` lowercase-hex convention. Hash-input determinism across Python, Go, Rust, .NET, and JavaScript is the property cross-implementation byte-equivalence rests on, and 008 is the gate that catches the silent disagreements I have seen break interop in three previous deployments — Java's `Double.toString`, Go's `strconv.FormatFloat`, and Python `repr` all need post-processing to match the JCS shortest-round-trip. **Closure status: nothing to close.**

### Finding 3 — `[Gap]` Hash-function agility is missing — SHA-256 is hard-baked across five call sites and the migration path is verbal, not structural

The signature `algorithm` field is a real upgrade vector — §4.3.2 names the seal record's `algorithm` line, the verifier dispatches on it, and the §4.2 `signatures` list (Variant B per-algorithm `sign_payload`) admits dual-algorithm coexistence at the wire level. That is well done. The HASH function, however, is not parameterized at all. SHA-256 is the hard assumption in five distinct call sites — HKDF-SHA-256 (session-key derivation), HMAC-SHA-256 (per-event MAC), `key_fingerprint = SHA-256(utf8(tenant_id) || ikm)[:16]`, `hkdf_inputs_digest = SHA-256(HKDF_SALT || info_for_tenant || length_LE32)`, and the RFC 6962 Merkle leaf and internal-node hashes. Spec §4.1 names a "Per-entry algorithm agility — forward note" admitting `ffiec.chain.algorithm` as an OPTIONAL forensic-only attribute that a future v1.1 MAY define as a dispatch identifier, but the note is informative; the seal-record `algorithm` field dispatches signatures only, not hashes. If a practical SHA-256 collision attack lands during the v1.0 deployment horizon — unlikely but the spec admits the possibility in §4.3.2's 30-day patch SLA — the migration is a wire-format change for every chain entry, not a parameter flip. The Merkle seal happens to depend on second-preimage resistance rather than collision resistance per spec §4.2 (and the best published second-preimage attack on SHA-256 has complexity exceeding 2^254 per Khovratovich-Rechberger 2010 type results), so the seal alone is robust against the realistic 2030-2045 cryptanalytic horizon, but the per-event MAC's HMAC-SHA-256 collision robustness sits at 2^128 birthday-bound — fine today, watch-list at 25 years.

**What would close this.** Promote the `ffiec.chain.algorithm` attribute and a parallel seal-record `hash_algorithm` field to v1.1 normative (default `"sha-256"`, future values `"sha-256/256"` truncation-tolerant SHA-512/256, `"sha-3-256"`, `"shake-128"`). Bind both into the per-event canonical bytes and into `sign_payload`. The plumbing is mechanical — the verifier dispatches per-entry on the hash identifier the way it dispatches per-seal on the signature identifier. Until then the spec acknowledges the gap honestly in §4.1, but a forward note is not a structural defense and a CFRG reviewer reading the v1.0 substrate today knows the day SHA-256 weakens, every chain entry produced under v1.0 needs re-MAC-ing or the institution accepts that the historical chain's repudiation-resistance moves with the algorithm.

### Finding 4 — `[Partial]` Hybrid signatures are structurally admitted but no shipped fixture exists

Spec §4.2 carries the `signatures` list, §4.3.2 names the dual-algorithm transitional posture, and §4.3 binds `algorithm` and `sign_payload_version` into the per-algorithm `sign_payload` (Variant B). The verifier's §7 step 11 dispatch table covers cases (a) through (e) including AND-security under both signatures, single-algorithm fallback, and "one valid + one invalid" with a clear severity stance. The dispatch table is the right shape — AND-security is the only defensible posture during the transition because OR-security would let an attacker who breaks one algorithm forge while the un-broken algorithm still validates legitimate seals. NIST IR 8413 and SP 800-208 both lean toward AND-style hybrid for archival, and the spec lands there.

What is missing is a shipped fixture. Test vector 015 (`dual-algorithm-cosigned-seal/`) is explicitly a stub — `description.md` carries the recipe but no `inputs/` or `expected/` directory, no actual Dilithium3 (FIPS 204 ML-DSA) or SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) keypair, no signed payload bytes. The spec admits this honestly ("Byte-level fixture deferred to the v1.x ship that introduces the second algorithm"), but for an institution planning a 30-year retention horizon, the FIPS 204 ML-DSA-65 (192-bit classical-security level under quantum, ~50 KB public key, ~3.3 KB signature) or FIPS 205 SLH-DSA-SHA2-192f (~50 byte public key, ~35 KB signature) integration is the load-bearing question. Two seal records co-signed under Ed25519 and ML-DSA-65 would carry a ~3.4 KB signature payload per day per tenant — 1.2 MB per tenant per year, easy to absorb. SLH-DSA's hash-based stateless construction inherits SHA-256's security margin, which under Grover would degrade to roughly 2^96 against preimage attacks — still comfortable for a 50-year horizon, and SLH-DSA has no structured-lattice concerns (unlike Dilithium, which depends on Module-LWE hardness assumptions still under active cryptanalysis).

**What would close this.** A shipped 015 fixture with TEST-USE-ONLY ML-DSA-65 (FIPS 204) and SLH-DSA-SHA2-192f (FIPS 205) keypairs, real signature bytes, and a v1.x-final-amendment commitment that lifts case 015 from stub to normative. The structural plumbing is already in place — what's missing is the byte-level conformance gate that proves a verifier built today against the spec text actually accepts dual-algorithm seals when the bytes arrive. The spec is ready; the corpus is not.

### Finding 5 — `[Gap]` No key-transparency mechanism — the verifier trusts whatever public key it is handed

Spec §4.3 says "the corresponding public key is published via the institution's tenant key registry (out of scope of this specification, but implementations MUST provide a documented retrieval path)." Verifier design 07 §2 names `--root-key` as a flat-file PEM the examiner provides. The seal record carries `public_key_id` as a string identifier, and §7 step 11 verifies the signature against the resolved public key. This is the trust root, and the spec is silent on how the verifier knows the trust root is correct.

The threat model in design 09 §2.10 (Adversary I) acknowledges this as "examiner-side tooling subversion" and names a multi-channel defense — cosign signature on the verifier binary, GPG-signed manifest, regulator-held public-key fingerprint cross-check — but the regulator-held-fingerprint procedure is itself "a v1.x roadmap candidate" referenced in `regulator-pack/regulator-procedures.md` (currently un-published). Until that procedure ships, the trust anchor for the entire chain — the public key the verifier validates Ed25519 signatures against — is whatever bytes the examiner's flat-file contains. An attacker who substitutes both the public key file and the seal records under a forged signing key produces a chain that PASSes verification.

This is the standard PKI trust-root problem and there are mature answers. Certificate Transparency (RFC 6962, ironically the same RFC the Merkle construction is taken from) builds an append-only log of certificates with cryptographic gossip between independent witnesses. Sigstore's Rekor log is a more recent shape. CONIKS does key transparency for end-user-controlled keys. Any of these would let a verifier walking a seal cross-check that the public key it verified against was the public key the institution registered as of `seal_date`, with cryptographic evidence rather than out-of-band trust. The spec is consciously narrower — the verifier is offline, makes no network calls (design 07 §1), and the regulator holds the trust anchor out-of-band. That is a defensible v1.0 posture for federally-supervised submissions where the examiner-regulator channel substitutes for transparency, but it does not extend to non-supervised consumers (a journalist, an academic, an external auditor without regulator credentials).

**What would close this.** A v1.x amendment naming a key-transparency scheme. Two paths the spec could take. (i) **Regulator-operated CT log.** The FFIEC (or each prudential regulator) operates an append-only Merkle log of `(institution_id, tenant_id, public_key_id, valid_from, valid_until, public_key_bytes)` records signed by the regulator's master key. Witness statements gossip between regulators. The verifier walking a seal includes the log inclusion proof in its report. (ii) **Sigstore-style ephemeral identity.** Each signing key is bound to a workload identity attested by an OIDC IdP at registration, the binding logged in Rekor, and the verifier checks the log inclusion proof. Path (i) is the better fit for the spec's regulator-anchored trust topology; path (ii) is the better fit if the spec wants to extend trust to non-supervised consumers. Either path is a multi-year commitment but the spec's algorithm-agility plumbing makes it straightforward — the seal-record schema can carry an optional `key_log_inclusion_proof` field without breaking existing chains.

### Finding 6 — `[Nit]` `public_key_id` is not bound into `sign_payload`; the verifier-side `algorithm/key-type mismatch` defense is operational rather than cryptographic

I read §4.3 carefully and the v1.0a `sign_payload` carries 10 lines: magic, `sign_payload_version`, `algorithm`, `format_version`, `tenant_id`, `seal_date`, `hex(merkle_root)`, `hex(hkdf_inputs_digest)`, `cadence`, `dev_mode`. The `public_key_id` field is on the seal record but NOT inside `sign_payload`. Spec §7 step 11 covers the case where the resolved public key's algorithm disagrees with the seal's claimed algorithm (`algorithm/key-type mismatch at signature verification`), which is the right response to a corrupted resolution path, but it does not cover the case where the attacker resolves a different valid key for the same algorithm — say, an attacker controls `public_key_id="tenant-A-key-v2"` and substitutes a different valid Ed25519 public key under that label. The seal's signature was produced under a different private key, the substituted public key is a real Ed25519 key, the algorithm matches, and only the registry's integrity stops the substitution. Under Adversary I's three-channel defense, this is closed — but it is closed at the regulator-held-fingerprint layer, not at the cryptographic layer.

Binding `public_key_id` into `sign_payload` would close it cryptographically. A signature produced under key X cannot be presented as a signature under key Y because the bytes the signer covered name X explicitly. The cost is one additional line in `sign_payload` (call it line 11, `public_key_id`); the benefit is that the algorithm-confusion defense closes one more attack surface. Since v1.0a is the current `sign_payload` form and any extension produces a v1.0b discriminator, this is a clean v1.x amendment.

**What would close this.** Promote a v1.0b `sign_payload` form that adds `public_key_id` as line 11. Pre-amendment chains continue to verify under v1.0a per the existing dispatch logic in §4.3. The schema change is mechanical; the verifier cost is one additional `entry.public_key_id == header.public_key_id` check. A nit because Adversary I's defense composition does close the practical attack — but the cryptographic cost of binding the key identifier into the payload is one line, and a CFRG reviewer's instinct is "if it's signed-over content, sign over it explicitly."

### Finding 7 — `[Partial]` Quantum threat model is articulated but the migration window may be tighter than 30+180 days under harvest-now-decrypt-later

The §4.3.2 quantum-readiness commitment names two response windows: a 30-day spec patch on credible Ed25519 break and 180-day institution migration; 30-day patch + 90-day migration on HMAC/SHA-256 break. The threat-model document (design 09 §2.9) extends this with a NIST-deprecation-tracking commitment (within 6 months of NIST publication) and a dual-algorithm transitional posture once two major HSM vendors ship FIPS-validated Dilithium / SLH-DSA support. This is roughly the right shape but it does not internalize the harvest-now-decrypt-later (HNDL) threat as cleanly as the archival use case would benefit from.

Under HNDL, the adversary records signature output today and decrypts (or in this case, forges retroactively) once a CRQC arrives. For an Ed25519 signature, the relevant attack is Shor's algorithm on the discrete log over Curve25519 — not Grover. Shor is polynomial in the number of qubits relative to the curve size; a quantum computer of ~2,330 logical qubits and ~4 billion Toffoli gates can break Curve25519 (Roetteler-Naehrig-Svore-Lauter 2017; refined estimates by Häner et al. 2020). The scary part for a 30-year retention horizon: every Ed25519-signed seal produced today is a target the moment a CRQC arrives, retroactively. The Merkle root cannot be re-signed (the original `sign_payload` is fixed and the root names a specific moment in time), so a CRQC-equipped attacker who recovers the institution's Ed25519 private key from the public key alone can produce alternate seal records under the same key — the chain's evidentiary value collapses retroactively for every seal day under that key's tenure.

The 30-day patch + 180-day migration window does not address this. The window addresses going-forward signatures; the past is fixed. The hash-based per-event MAC (HMAC-SHA-256) is more robust to HNDL because Grover's algorithm on SHA-256 collisions runs at ~2^85 quantum operations (Brassard-Høyer-Tapp 1997 quantum birthday bound), which is still infeasible for any plausible CRQC of the 2035-2045 horizon. But the seal-layer signature is the soft target.

**What would close this.** A normative commitment to dual-algorithm seal posture (Ed25519 + ML-DSA-65 or Ed25519 + SLH-DSA) for any seal produced after a fixed date — call it 2028 or 2030 — under v1.x. The shipped fixture for case 015 (Finding 4) is the operational predicate. The earlier the dual-algorithm posture begins, the smaller the HNDL exposure. NIST's Round 4 final selections (FIPS 204 ML-DSA, FIPS 205 SLH-DSA, both published August 2024) are stable; HSM vendors are shipping integration roadmaps. The spec's transition machinery (§4.3.2 Variant B per-algorithm `sign_payload`) is ready. What's missing is the date.

### Finding 8 — `[Nit]` The `key_fingerprint[:16]` truncation argument is correct but understates the long-horizon cost

Spec §10.6's "16-byte fingerprint truncation analysis" (informative) makes the argument that the 128-bit truncated fingerprint does not need collision resistance because the per-entry MAC check at §7 step 9 catches any colliding-IKM substitution. The argument is correct as stated — the verifier's defense is composite, and at step 8 the fingerprint mismatch fails before any MAC compute, while at step 9 the MAC mismatch catches a substitution that produced the same fingerprint by accident. The 2^64 birthday bound on a 128-bit truncated SHA-256 is within reach of a well-funded adversary over a multi-decade horizon — by 2046, the cost of finding two IKMs that produce the same `SHA-256(utf8(tenant_id) || ikm)[:16]` truncation is ~2^64 SHA-256 calls, which at $5/GH-hour-equivalent (BMG curve trend) sits in the millions of dollars. Within reach of nation-state and within reach of a sophisticated insider.

The spec's defense holds — the MAC check at step 9 is the load-bearing cryptographic check, the fingerprint is the operational pre-flight — but the language in §10.6 understates the case. Under Grover's algorithm, the quantum collision-search cost on the truncated 128-bit fingerprint is ~2^42 (not 2^64) because Brassard-Høyer-Tapp's quantum birthday provides a ~N^(1/3) speedup over the classical N^(1/2). A 2^42 quantum search is plausible inside the 30-year horizon under any CRQC scenario where Grover-like search is feasible.

**What would close this.** Promote the §10.6.1 wording from "v1.0 retains 16 bytes as the conformance bar because the security argument above does not depend on the fingerprint's collision resistance" to "v1.x institutions with retention horizons exceeding 25 years SHOULD configure the optional 32-byte fingerprint mode" once a v1.x amendment defines that mode. The cost is 16 additional bytes per entry — negligible. The benefit is that the operational pre-flight check stops degrading under quantum search and the composite defense holds independent of the MAC backstop. A nit because the MAC backstop is real and the §10.6 argument is honest, but a long-horizon spec should leave a documented escape hatch on the truncation rather than relying on the operator to reason about "it's not load-bearing."

## Long-term durability posture

I would trust this substrate with **20-year retention** today, with three caveats: (1) the institution's tenant key registry must operate on the regulator-held-fingerprint discipline that Adversary I's defense composition names, (2) the institution's IKM rotation cadence must be tight enough (90 days for high-value, 365 days for standard, per design 02 §4.3) that any single IKM's exposure window is bounded, and (3) the institution must run the JCS edge-case fixture (008) against its implementation before going live, because a Python-Go-Rust-JavaScript disagreement on float canonicalization would break cross-implementation re-verification on the first event whose attributes contain a duration in seconds.

I would trust this substrate with **50-year retention only after Findings 3, 4, 5, and 7 close** — hash-function agility lifted to normative dispatch, a shipped dual-algorithm fixture under FIPS 204 / FIPS 205, a key-transparency mechanism that lets a verifier prove the public key is the right one without out-of-band trust, and a fixed-date dual-algorithm seal mandate that closes the harvest-now-decrypt-later window before a CRQC arrives. None of these are radical changes; the spec's algorithm-dispatch plumbing in the seal record and the per-algorithm `sign_payload` Variant B in §4.3.2 are exactly the right shape for the migration. What is missing is the byte-level conformance gate (the case 015 fixture) and the date.

The earliest year I would expect to need a crypto-agility migration is **2030 to 2032**. Two independent triggers point at that window. First, NIST's FIPS 186-5 includes a deprecation timeline note that names ECDSA-P256 as "Disallowed after 2030" for new federal applications under SP 800-131A Rev. 2 if quantum-resistant alternatives are available; Ed25519 is not on that list as of FIPS 186-5 publication, but the parallel quantum-vulnerability argument applies to both. Second, IBM's quantum roadmap and the Quantum Economic Development Consortium's Q-Day risk-curve estimates put a CRQC at 2030-2035 with ~10-25% probability — under the harvest-now-decrypt-later threat, that is the moment seals produced today become forgeable retroactively. The spec's 30-day spec-patch SLA is generous; the 180-day institution migration window is generous; what is not generous is the gap between today and the first dual-algorithm seal a v1.x amendment makes mandatory. That gap should close before 2030.

The substrate is well-built. Ed25519 is the right primitive for v1.0. RFC 6962, RFC 8785, RFC 5869, FIPS 198-1 are all the right citations. The verifier's twelve-step ordered procedure is the kind of mechanical conformance gate that survives implementation churn. The work that remains is structural — making the migration paths cryptographically explicit rather than verbal — and the corpus work (case 015 byte-level fixture). Both are tractable inside a v1.x amendment cycle. The institution running this substrate today produces evidence that holds up under any first-look review I would give. The institution running this substrate in 2046 needs the v1.x work to land before 2030.
