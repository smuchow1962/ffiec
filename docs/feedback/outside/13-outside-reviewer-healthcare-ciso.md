## Supporting questions

### HQ-1. PHI in chain artifacts — clinical context is PHI, not just clinical identifiers

**Question.** The privacy-by-design.md tokenization pattern (referenced in Elena's drop and again in §10.11's `audit.ecoa.translation.output_hash` precedent) addresses identifier-level PHI cleanly: replace patient_mrn with a token, replace member_id with a token, the mapping lives in a privacy-store the chain never sees. That works for finance because clinical-context-free transactions are common. In clinical AI it breaks. The chain entry for our sepsis-prediction model carries the model's input vector: lactate level, MAP, white-cell count, ICD-10 codes for current admission, recent temperature, recent heart rate. Every one of those is PHI under HIPAA's broader interpretation — HHS-OCR has consistently taken the position that clinical findings combined with admission timing constitute identifying information when the population is small, and ICU sepsis populations are small per institution per day. Tokenizing the lactate value defeats the audit purpose; preserving it puts PHI in the chain artifact, which then inherits the chain's 6-year HIPAA retention floor (45 CFR 164.530(j)(2)) plus the §10.13 evidentiary-artifact retention coupling.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** A healthcare addendum to privacy-by-design.md (or a new `docs/regulator-pack/healthcare-phi-handling.md`) covering three operationally workable postures, with the institution selecting per deployment. **(a) Full-content posture** — the clinical input vector is recorded as captured, the chain entry inherits PHI status, the chain ledger inherits HIPAA Security Rule §164.312 controls (encryption at rest, access logging, workforce training), and the institution names the ledger as a designated record set under 45 CFR 164.501. **(b) Hash-only posture** — modeled on §10.11's `output_hash` pattern: the chain entry records `audit.clinical.input_hash` and `audit.clinical.output_hash` (lowercase hex SHA-256 of the canonicalized input and AI output), and the input/output bytes themselves live in the EHR under existing EHR controls. The chain proves what the AI saw and said without retaining the PHI bytes. The integrity binding is the SHA-256 collision-resistance assumption, parallel to §10.11's translation hash. This is the pattern I would deploy for the sepsis model. **(c) Limited-data-set posture** — under 45 CFR 164.514(e), the chain operates under a Limited Data Set with a Data Use Agreement; the institution accepts that DSAR-equivalent requests under 45 CFR 164.524 (HIPAA Right of Access) flow to the EHR, not the chain. The institution's HITRUST CSF v11 control description names the choice and the SOC team's HITRUST sample tests for evidence that PHI bytes are not co-resident with chain artifacts in postures (b) and (c). Default to (b) unless a specific reason argues otherwise — (a) closes the auditability question cleanly but multiplies the retention-and-breach surface.

### HQ-2. HIPAA minimum-necessary rule applied to the chain itself

**Question.** HIPAA's Minimum Necessary Rule at 45 CFR 164.502(b) limits use and disclosure of PHI to the minimum necessary for the purpose. The chain's purpose, in clinical operations, is decision-integrity audit. The §1.2 epistemic-scope statement is excellent for litigation posture but it does not function as a minimum-necessary justification under HIPAA. HHS-OCR has historically scrutinized "comprehensive logging for compliance purposes" as a category — the regulator's position is that breadth-of-capture must be justified against a documented purpose, not merely declared as compliance-relevant. For the chain to pass minimum-necessary review at deployment, the institution needs a written analysis covering which fields the chain captures, why each is necessary for the integrity purpose, and what alternatives were considered and rejected. Elena's Q-2 surfaces the GDPR Article 5(1)(c) parallel; the HIPAA framing is separate, and HHS-OCR enforcement priorities under the 2024 HIPAA Security Rule update lean toward documented periodic minimum-necessary review for AI logging specifically.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** A new section in the healthcare PHI addendum: the institution conducts a written minimum-necessary analysis at deployment time and reviews it annually under HITRUST CSF v11 control 19.b (Information Privacy and Protection — Minimum Necessary). The analysis answers: (1) purpose statement — the chain's defined purpose under clinical deployment is integrity audit of AI decisions for FDA SaMD post-market surveillance, Joint Commission patient-safety reporting, ONC information-blocking compliance, and CMS prior-authorization timeliness evidence as applicable; (2) field inventory — every field the chain captures, classified as identifier (45 CFR 164.514(b)(2)), clinical finding, decision metadata, or non-PHI infrastructure metadata; (3) per-field necessity — for each PHI-bearing field, the documented reason capture is necessary; alternatives considered (hash-only per HQ-1, exclusion, partial redaction); rejection rationale; (4) annual review trigger — HHS-OCR enforcement guidance update, HITRUST CSF revision, FDA SaMD guidance revision, or AI Governance Committee determination. Signed by the HIPAA Privacy Officer and the AI Governance Committee chair. The institution's CC8.1-equivalent control description (under HITRUST v11 the closer mapping is control 06.d Change Management or 09.aa Audit Logging) cites the analysis by document identifier and review date.

### HQ-3. HIPAA Breach Notification timing under 45 CFR 164.404 — chain artifacts as forensic evidence and as breach surface

**Question.** Elena's Q-10 surfaces the multi-jurisdiction matrix (HIPAA 60 days, FFIEC 36 hours, GDPR 72 hours, DORA 24 hours). The healthcare-specific operational issue is sharper. Under 45 CFR 164.404(b), the 60-day clock starts at the time the breach is "discovered or, by exercising reasonable diligence, would have been known" — and the chain itself is what makes "would have been known" enforceable. If the chain detects an integrity violation (a `key_fingerprint mismatch at seq N` per §7 step 8 that traces to a misconfigured rotation but where forensic analysis cannot rule out IKM exposure), HHS-OCR's position will be that the verifier output IS the discovery moment under §164.404(b) — the institution cannot argue "we did not know" once the verifier has flagged the entry. This is operationally heavier than the FFIEC 36-hour rule because HHS-OCR's position is that any chain-detected anomaly that touches PHI access is a presumed breach unless the institution rebuts the presumption under §164.402(2) (the four-factor risk assessment). The spec's incident-response-playbook references a 72-hour seal-delay threshold for examiner notification but does not address how a chain-detected anomaly composes with the §164.404(b) "reasonable diligence" trigger.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** Extend the breach-notification matrix in incident-response-playbook.md (or in the healthcare addendum) with a HIPAA row: chain-detected anomalies under §7 are §164.404(b) discovery events when the affected entries reference PHI access (clinical AI deployments per HQ-1). The 60-day clock starts at the verifier-output timestamp, not at the institution's incident-determination meeting. The IR playbook MUST include a §164.402(2) four-factor risk assessment as the first step after a chain-detected anomaly involving a clinical deployment: (1) the nature and extent of the PHI involved; (2) the unauthorized person who used or to whom the disclosure was made; (3) whether the PHI was actually acquired or viewed; (4) the extent to which risk has been mitigated. Documented contemporaneously and signed by the HIPAA Privacy Officer. If the assessment concludes low probability of PHI compromise (the §164.402(2) safe harbor), the institution documents the conclusion and HHS-OCR notification is not triggered. If inconclusive or non-low, the 60-day clock applies. The HIPAA clock runs concurrently with the FFIEC 36-hour clock when both apply; the four-factor assessment is the bridging document.

### HQ-4. HIPAA 6-year retention floor vs spec's 7-year framing vs state-law variations

**Question.** The spec (§10.9 + §10.13) establishes 7-year retention as the FFIEC banking standard; Elena's Q-3 surfaces the 7-year choice grounded in 12 CFR 1002. For HIPAA-covered AI logging the floor is different. 45 CFR 164.530(j)(2) requires 6-year retention of documentation of policies, procedures, communications, and actions required by Subparts A and E of 45 CFR 164. AI-decision logs are within scope. Health & Safety Code §123145. A multi-state institution faces a 50-state retention matrix where the longest applicable retention governs. The spec's 7-year framing does not address this and an institution that simply adopts 7 years for all chain artifacts is underretaining for pediatric records in roughly half of US jurisdictions.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** Add to §10.9 (or to the healthcare addendum): institutions deploying the chain for HIPAA-covered AI logging operate under a retention floor of 6 years per 45 CFR 164.530(j)(2), extended by applicable state-law floors. State-law floors commonly extend retention for pediatric records past age of majority (Illinois 10 years, New York 6 years past age of majority, California 7 years). Multi-state institutions maintain a state-by-state matrix and apply the longest applicable floor per record. The chain's IKM retention coupling under §10.9 (an IKM remains recoverable while any chain entry under that key_version is retained) compounds the operational burden — institutions deploying clinical chains plan for 25+ year IKM availability for pediatric-care chains, well beyond the cloud HSM key-lifecycle defaults at AWS CloudHSM, Azure Managed HSM, and Google Cloud HSM. The companion document includes an example matrix for the top 10 states by Medicaid enrollment.

### HQ-5. FDA Software-as-a-Medical-Device — chain artifacts in the 510(k) / De Novo submission and post-market surveillance

**Question.** Several clinical AI systems my institution operates are FDA-regulated software-as-a-medical-device. The sepsis prediction model is a 510(k)-cleared device; a future radiology image-analysis tool is on a De Novo pathway. FDA's December 2024 final guidance on Predetermined Change Control Plans (PCCPs) for AI/ML-enabled device software functions changed the post-market posture significantly: manufacturers can submit a PCCP at the original clearance/authorization that pre-authorizes specific categories of model changes, and changes within the PCCP do not require new submission. Changes outside the PCCP do require new 510(k) or De Novo submission. The chain captures decision history; §4.4.1 routing-decision and §4.4.2 deployment-intent capture parts of the model-change story — but the FDA-relevant question is "does the chain provide evidence supporting the manufacturer's PCCP boundary, distinguishing pre-authorized changes from changes requiring new submission?" The current spec does not name FDA SaMD as a consumer of chain evidence and does not normate any attribute set tied to the PCCP boundary.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** A new informative subsection §4.4.5 "**FDA SaMD evidence capture (informative).**" The subsection describes a recommended attribute set for chain entries representing model invocations of FDA-regulated AI: `audit.fda.samd.device_identifier` (the device's UDI or 510(k)/De Novo identifier), `audit.fda.samd.model_version` (the algorithm version, expected to match the manufacturer's PCCP-bound version registry), `audit.fda.samd.pccp_boundary` (one of `pre_authorized` or `out_of_pccp`, with `out_of_pccp` triggering an FDA submission obligation per the manufacturer's PCCP), `audit.fda.samd.intended_use_population` (the indications-for-use population the invocation served). Institution-emitted under the canonical bytes per §5; the verifier treats them as ordinary `audit.*` attributes. The companion text names the operational consumer: the manufacturer's regulatory-affairs team (which may be the institution itself or a vendor) reads the chain for PCCP-boundary distribution analysis at quarterly cadence; entries with `pccp_boundary = "out_of_pccp"` are an FDA-submission trigger. The institution's HITRUST CSF v11 control 02.b (Authorized Software Use) names the consumption procedure. Forward-scope work — FDA's SaMD landscape is still evolving and the working group should not normate beyond informative until FDA publishes specific audit-log guidance, a candidate work item in FDA's 2025-2026 AI-software workstream.

### HQ-6. ONC §170.315(d)(2) EHR audit-log integration — two audit logs or one composed log?

**Question.** Every ONC-certified EHR — Epic, Cerner/Oracle Health, Meditech, Allscripts/Veradigm, athenahealth — implements 45 CFR 170.315(d)(2), the audit-record content certification criterion: date/time, type of action, patient identification, user identification, originating IP. The Cures Act information-blocking rule (45 CFR 171) makes the audit log itself patient-accessible under the right of access where PHI is disclosed. My institution's existing audit-log infrastructure is the EHR's native audit log; deploying the chain for clinical AI introduces a second audit-log stream that is not natively integrated. Two questions follow: (1) does the chain replace the EHR's §170.315(d)(2) audit log, supplement it, or operate beside it as a parallel stream; (2) when the chain captures information that the EHR's audit log also captures, are the two records consistent, and how does the institution reconcile them at examination time? The spec is silent because the spec is FFIEC-scoped, but the institution that deploys the chain in clinical operations needs an answer before sign-off.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** A new healthcare-specific design document `docs/regulator-pack/healthcare-ehr-audit-integration.md` covering three patterns. **(a) Parallel-stream pattern.** The chain operates beside the EHR audit log without integration; the EHR's audit log retains its §170.315(d)(2) certification posture; the chain captures the AI-decision-specific evidence the EHR's native audit log does not (model version, prompt, parameters, routing decisions). The two streams are reconciled at examination time via a documented mapping in the institution's HITRUST CSF v11 control 09.aa (Audit Logging) description — for any AI-driven event that touches the EHR, the institution can produce both the EHR audit-log entry and the corresponding chain entry, time-aligned to within the institution's NTP-synchronization budget. **(b) Composed-log pattern.** The chain's wire form is consumed by the EHR's audit-log subsystem (Epic Caboodle, Cerner CCL audit views) so the EHR's §170.315(d)(2) report includes the AI-decision evidence inline. Operationally heavy; some EHR vendors expose suitable hooks (Epic's HL7 v2 audit gateway), others do not. Recommended only where the institution has an existing EHR-vendor integration commitment. **(c) Chain-replaces-audit-log pattern.** Not recommended for ONC-certified EHRs — the chain's wire form does not natively conform to §170.315(d)(2)'s data-element set, and substituting the chain would jeopardize ONC certification. Workable only for AI systems operating outside the certified EHR boundary (a standalone clinical-decision-support tool that does not write back to the EHR). The companion document also addresses the Cures Act information-blocking interaction: chain entries are NOT patient-accessible under 45 CFR 164.524 by default — they are institution-internal evidence and not part of the designated record set unless the institution explicitly designates them under 45 CFR 164.501. The HIPAA Privacy Officer makes the designation decision.

### HQ-7. CMS-0057-F prior-authorization timeliness — `received_at` clock vs CMS clock

**Question.** CMS's January 2026 final rule "Advancing Interoperability and Improving Prior Authorization Processes" (CMS-0057-F) requires Medicare Advantage organizations, state Medicaid managed-care plans, CHIP managed-care plans, and Qualified Health Plans on the federal exchange to make prior-authorization decisions within 7 calendar days for standard requests and 72 hours for expedited requests, beginning January 1, 2026. Without an explicit attribute capturing the CMS-clock-start moment under MAC binding, the chain provides decision-integrity evidence but not regulatory-timeliness evidence; the institution has to reconcile chain timestamps against FHIR API logs at examination time.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** A new informative subsection §4.4.6 "**Healthcare regulatory-timeliness evidence (informative).**" Recommended attributes for chain entries representing regulated-timeliness AI decisions: `audit.healthcare.regulatory_clock_start` (RFC 3339 UTC of the regulatory-clock-starting event, e.g., the FHIR API request receipt for CMS-0057-F), `audit.healthcare.regulatory_clock_deadline` (RFC 3339 UTC of the regulatory deadline, computed from clock start plus the rule's window), `audit.healthcare.regulatory_clock_basis` (the regulation imposing the clock — `cms-0057-f`, `ncqa-um`, `state-pa-rule:CA`, etc.), `audit.healthcare.decision_returned_at` (RFC 3339 UTC of the electronic-return moment). Institution-emitted under the canonical bytes per §5 — they receive integrity binding and a coordinated rewrite surfaces as a MAC mismatch at §7 step 9. The operational consumer: the institution's NCQA UM compliance team consumes the chain for the timeliness-percentile distribution at monthly cadence, the CMS-0057-F examination-response file references chain entries for sampled decisions, and audit-procedures.md is extended with a healthcare-specific procedure (P-H-1) sampling for `regulatory_clock_deadline >= decision_returned_at`. The §4.4.1 routing-decision and §4.4.2 deployment-intent schemas compose alongside this — routing shows which AI made the call, deployment-intent shows production/canary/A-B context, and timeliness shows the call was made on time.

### HQ-9. Patient Safety Work Product privilege under PSQIA — chain artifacts and PSO reporting

**Question.** Under the Patient Safety and Quality Improvement Act (PSQIA, 42 USC 299b-21 to 299b-26), institutions can voluntarily report patient-safety events to a federally-listed Patient Safety Organization (PSO); information assembled within the institution's Patient Safety Evaluation System for the purpose of reporting to the PSO is Patient Safety Work Product (PSWP), which is privileged and confidential under 42 CFR Part 3 — not admissible in malpractice litigation, professional disciplinary proceedings, or as evidence in administrative proceedings. AI-driven patient-safety events — a sepsis-prediction false negative leading to delayed sepsis recognition, a clinical-documentation AI drafting an inaccurate problem list that propagates into the medical record — are reportable patient-safety events. The institution's PSO-reporting workflow assembles evidence about the event for transmission to the PSO; chain entries are exactly the evidence the PSO workflow consumes. The legal question: when the institution's Patient Safety Evaluation System pulls chain entries into the PSO-reporting package, do those chain entries become PSWP and inherit the privilege? If yes, the institution's litigation posture for AI-related patient-safety events is significantly stronger; if no, the chain's evidentiary openness (Diego's drop) cuts against the institution's litigation posture. The spec is silent — appropriately, since this is a privilege-doctrine question outside spec scope — but the institution needs guidance before deployment.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** A new healthcare-specific section in `docs/legal-disclosure.md` (or a new `docs/regulator-pack/pswp-and-psqia-handling.md`). The defensible operational posture: (1) Chain entries themselves are NOT inherently PSWP — they are institution-internal records of AI activity, not records assembled within the Patient Safety Evaluation System for PSO reporting. The chain's evidentiary openness under Diego's framing (FRE 901(b)(9), 902(13), 902(14)) applies to chain entries in their default state. (2) Chain-derived analysis assembled within the PSE for PSO reporting IS PSWP — the institution's patient-safety committee analysis of "we observed N false-negatives in sepsis predictions over Q3, here is the root-cause analysis" is PSWP because it was assembled within the PSE for PSO transmission. The institution maintains separation between the chain-as-evidence (open) and the chain-derived-analysis-for-PSO (privileged). (3) The institution documents the separation in its PSE policy: the chain ledger is NOT a PSE component; the PSE consumes chain entries as input but the analytical work-product produced within the PSE is the PSWP, not the chain entries themselves. (4) When the institution receives a discovery request for "all chain entries related to patient X," it produces them. When the institution receives a discovery request for "all PSE analyses derived from chain entries," it asserts PSQIA privilege. The two-track posture is settled doctrine for non-AI patient-safety events; the chain composes alongside it without changing the structure. The litigation-support team and the PSO contract review the posture annually.

Informative and updated as state legislation evolves: (1) **Colorado AI Act** — for high-risk AI deployments, the institution provides Colorado consumer-facing disclosures; the chain provides the audit-trail substrate but the disclosure language and timing are state-specific. The institution's CC8.1 (HITRUST 12.a equivalent) names the Colorado disclosure procedure. (2) **California SB 1120 (Physicians Make Decisions Act)** — for utilization-management AI, the AI-assisted decision must be reviewed by a licensed California physician before becoming final; the chain's routing-decision capture (§4.4.1) records the AI-assist nature, and a new attribute set `audit.california.sb1120.physician_review_id` and `audit.california.sb1120.physician_review_at` on the corresponding chain entry records the physician's review. (3) **State medical board disciplinary proceedings** — chain entries are admissible per state-specific evidentiary rules; the institution's state-by-state disciplinary-response matrix is maintained by regulatory-affairs and medical-staff offices. (4) **General principle** — the chain composes alongside state-specific disclosure, oversight, and admissibility regimes without subsuming them; institutions deploying the chain in clinical operations maintain the state-by-state matrix as a living document under change management. The companion text references Diego's federal-court framing and notes that state-court evidentiary rules generally compose with FRE-equivalent state rules but the litigation-support team validates per state.

### HQ-11. Clinical-workflow operational requirements — chain availability during EHR downtime and code blue

**Question.** Hospital operations are 24/7 with life-safety implications the FFIEC banking framework does not face. Three operational scenarios stress the chain's availability and degraded-mode posture in ways the §10.15 multi-region resilience framework does not fully cover. (1) **EHR scheduled downtime** — every certified EHR has a planned-downtime procedure (Epic's planned downtime is typically 4-8 hours quarterly for upgrades; downtime procedures route clinical activity through paper "downtime forms" and a read-only EHR mirror — Epic's Business Continuity Access, similar functions at Cerner/Meditech). During EHR downtime, AI systems either go offline or operate in degraded mode. What does the chain do? (2) **Code blue / mass casualty / disaster** — clinical AI may be invoked under emergency conditions where the institution's risk-tolerance for chain-availability latency is much lower than planned-operation default. The §4.3.1 "captured events continue to be ingested and chained (the per-event HMAC is independent of the HSM)" is the right posture, but the operational implication is that the IR team needs to know the seal can be deferred up to 72 hours per §4.3.1 SHOULD without escalation. In a clinical disaster, the institution's HSM may be physically affected. (3) **Ransomware** — healthcare ransomware is a 2024-2025 escalation pattern (Change Healthcare February 2024 was the most visible). The chain's posture during institution-wide ransomware is critical: the chain is forensic evidence for the post-incident investigation but only if the chain itself was not encrypted by the ransomware. The wave-5 close-out added an operator-guide section on ransomware DR (per the change log); a healthcare-specific extension is needed.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** A new healthcare operator-guide section, "Healthcare 24/7 operational requirements," extending `docs/operator-guide.md` (or a separate `docs/regulator-pack/healthcare-operational-runbook.md`). (1) **EHR downtime composition** — the chain is decoupled from the EHR by design; chain operations continue through EHR planned downtime. The institution's HITRUST CSF v11 control 12.c (Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery) names the chain's downtime-procedure participation: chain SDKs continue capture; chain ledgers continue ingest; seal jobs continue. The IR posture treats planned EHR downtime as a non-anomaly event provided the chain's components remain operational. (2) **Code blue / mass casualty** — the §4.3.1 72-hour seal-publication SHOULD is operationally appropriate; institutions with elevated incident posture may tighten to 6-hour or 12-hour publication SLA per the institution's risk-tolerance statement. The institution's HITRUST CSF v11 control 11.c (Incident Response) names disaster-mode chain operations and the cadence-relaxation procedure (§4.2.1 cadence relaxation requires examiner approval for FFIEC; the healthcare equivalent is institution-self-governed under HITRUST 11.c). (3) **Ransomware DR** — the chain's append-only storage, separate-key-custody for IKM and HSM, and immutable WORM retention compose against ransomware. The institution's HITRUST CSF v11 control 11.b (Backup) names the artifact backup posture: cross-region replication to a write-once-read-many store at minimum daily cadence, accessible only to a separate IR-team identity. References the wave-5 ransomware DR section in operator-guide.md and adds healthcare-specific elements (PHI handling during recovery, HHS-OCR notification timing, Joint Commission patient-safety-event notification).

### HQ-12. Clinician override and capture — Joint Commission MM.05.01.01 and LD.04.01.07

**Question.** Joint Commission accreditation standards require systematic monitoring of high-risk medication-ordering systems (MM.05.01.01) and oversight of clinical-decision-support tools (LD.04.01.07). AI-assisted ordering systems and clinical-decision-support tools fall within both. A core requirement is that clinicians can override the AI's recommendation and that the override is captured and reviewed for systematic patterns indicating either the AI is mis-tuned (frequent overrides on a specific recommendation type) or the clinician workflow has a usability gap. The chain captures decisions; it does NOT capture the "override" event explicitly in any normative attribute set. The §4.4.1 routing-decision schema is partially analogous (the routing-decision is a meta-decision about which AI to call; the override is a meta-decision about whether to follow the AI's recommendation), but the override has its own discriminator. Without a normative override-capture schema, institutions emit override evidence under ad-hoc attributes and the Joint Commission survey team variably accepts the institution's evidence quality.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** A new informative subsection §4.4.7 "**Clinician-override capture (informative).**" The override is a chain entry of its own (not attributes attached to the AI-recommendation entry), parallel to §4.4.1's treatment of routing decisions. The override entry's `chain_kind` is `"audit"` (the v1 enumeration is closed and override does not warrant a new value at v1.x), and the entry carries: `audit.clinical.override.original_recommendation_run_id` and `audit.clinical.override.original_recommendation_seq` (parent-linkage to the AI recommendation being overridden), `audit.clinical.override.clinician_npi` (the National Provider Identifier of the overriding clinician — PHI, handled per HQ-1's chosen posture, hash-only recommended), `audit.clinical.override.override_action` (one of `accept_with_modification` | `reject_recommendation` | `defer_decision` | `escalate_to_attending`), `audit.clinical.override.override_reason_code` (an institution-defined reason code from a controlled vocabulary; the vocabulary is part of the institution's HITRUST 09.aa control description), `audit.clinical.override.override_reason_freetext_hash` (lowercase hex SHA-256 of any free-text rationale), `audit.clinical.override.timestamp`. The institution's HITRUST CSF v11 control 09.aa and the Joint Commission survey-response file consume override-event chain entries for systematic-pattern analysis at quarterly cadence. The AI Governance Committee reviews override distribution as a model-tuning signal — high override rates on a specific recommendation type are a re-tune trigger. The §4.4.1 routing-decision and §4.4.2 deployment-intent schemas compose alongside this — three orthogonal kinds of meta-decision, each captured as its own chain-entry kind, all under the canonical bytes per §5.

An institution that already operates HITRUST-attested, ONC-certified, FDA-cleared clinical AI systems has the governance infrastructure to absorb the chain cleanly; one that does not is not ready to deploy clinical AI under any audit-trail substrate. **For the FFIEC working group's cross-sector positioning.** The spec is positioned as broadly applicable to regulated AI logging beyond the FFIEC banking core. Healthcare is the largest non-banking regulated-AI sector and the spec lands cleanly into it at the cryptographic and operational layers. The composition gaps surfaced here are the predictable result of a sector-specific spec being adopted across sectors — the analogous gaps for energy-sector AI (NERC CIP), defense-sector AI (CMMC + DoD AI ethics), education-sector AI (FERPA + IDEA), and federal-civilian AI (FedRAMP + OMB M-24-10) will land similarly when those reviewers come through. The pragmatic posture is to maintain the cryptographic and operational core unchanged while welcoming sector-specific companion documents as parallel artifacts. The chain itself is sector-neutral; the regulatory composition is necessarily sector-specific.
