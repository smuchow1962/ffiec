### Q-1. FSS AI Guidelines (2021, updated 2023) and the AI Basic Act 2025 governance pillars — explicit mapping required

### Q-2. PIPA Article 37-2 right to refuse automated decision-making (March 2023 amendment) — how is it evidenced in the chain?

PIPA Article 37-2, substantially strengthened in the March 2023 amendments, gives individuals the right to refuse a fully automated decision that produces legally binding or similarly significant effects on them. A customer denied credit by an AI system can demand a human review. The chain must capture: (a) that the AI made a preliminary decision, (b) that the customer invoked Article 37-2 right, (c) that human review occurred, (d) the human reviewer's final decision, (e) whether the human's decision differed from the AI's. The spec's customer-dispute-procedures.md covers GDPR Article 22 (right to refuse automated decision) but does not specifically address PIPA Article 37-2's mechanics, which in Korean practice triggers a statutory reconsideration channel (이의제기권) that is mandatory by law, not voluntary. **Ask:** Extend customer-dispute-procedures.md with a Korea-specific section: "**PIPA Article 37-2 Reconsideration (이의제기) Process and Chain Evidence.** When a customer objects to a fully automated AI decision (e.g., credit denial, account closure, pricing tier assignment), the institution must: (1) Record the objection in the chain with chain_kind='audit' and attributes audit.pipa.article_37_2.objection_received_at (ISO 8601 UTC), audit.pipa.article_37_2.original_ai_decision_seq (the prior entry's seq), audit.pipa.article_37_2.objection_reason (Korean-language customer explanation). (2) Route to human review: create a chain entry with chain_kind='operational' and attributes audit.pipa.article_37_2.human_review_assigned_to (reviewer identifier), audit.pipa.article_37_2.review_deadline (per institution policy, typically 14 days per PIPA Article 37-4). (3) Record the human review outcome: chain entry with audit.pipa.article_37_2.reviewer_decision (approve / modify / reverse), audit.pipa.article_37_2.decision_rationale (Korean text explanation), audit.pipa.article_37_2.decision_matches_original_ai (boolean). (4) Customer notification: send the customer a notice in Korean (per PIPA Article 37-3 language requirement) citing the verifier output as evidence the review occurred and was documented. The institution's FSS compliance team and the PIPC (Personal Information Protection Commission) can audit the chain entries to confirm Article 37-2 procedures were followed."

### Q-3. PIPA Article 26 cross-border transfer — MyData ecosystem compliance when tokens flow to non-adequate jurisdictions

PIPA Article 26 restricts transfers of personal data to foreign jurisdictions lacking "equivalent protection" status. The US does not have equivalent-protection status per the PIPC's 2023 guidance (unlike the EU). When a Korean institution sends chain tokens (which are pseudonymized personal data under PIPA, because the privacy-store de-mapping is retained for 7 years for dispute resolution) to a US-hosted Herald.Compliance system for customer-dispute handling or external verification, the transfer is restricted unless the institution implements supplementary safeguards. For MyData-enabled institutions (where consumers pull their AI-decision history across institutions), the chain tokens may flow through MyData intermediaries (MyData operators, MyData banks) to downstream consumer-facing services; all these flows are PIPA Article 26-governed and require documented transfer impact assessments (개인정보 이전영향평가). The spec does not address this PIPA-specific constraint for Korean institutions operating in the MyData ecosystem. **Ask:** Add to international-transfers.md or privacy-by-design.md a Korea-specific section: "**PIPA Article 26 Cross-Border Transfer and MyData Ecosystem Compliance.** When a Korean institution (controller under PIPA) transfers chain tokens to a non-adequate country (US, most of APAC except Japan and EU), PIPA Article 26 requires documented supplementary safeguards and prior filing with the PIPC. Recommended postures: (1) **De-mapping before US transfer (strongest):** Korean institution retains the privacy-store mapping on Korean servers under PIPA-compliant custody. US system receives only chain tokens, without reversal capability. Institution files transfer-impact assessment (개인정보 이전영향평가) with PIPC naming the de-mapping as supplementary safeguard. (2) **MyData consent-mediated transfer (medium):** Under MyData regulation (효율적인 정보이용 및 보호에 관한 법률), consumers explicitly consent to their AI-decision data being shared with downstream services. (3) **Institutional decision:** Institution may choose to retain the chain within Korean-only infrastructure, not transferring outside PIPA's equivalence boundary. ### Q-4. CIUPA Article 15 fairness-testing and explainability for credit-AI — chain captures inputs but not statistical fairness

CIUPA (Credit Information Use and Protection Act) Article 15 requires institutions to conduct fairness testing for credit-scoring AI, measure disparate impact across protected characteristics (gender, age, national origin, credit-worthy status), and be able to explain individual credit decisions citing the factors that drove the decision. The chain captures the AI's inputs and outputs per decision (supporting individual explainability per PIPA §37-2), but the spec does not specify how the chain supports **population-level fairness testing** required under CIUPA Article 15. An institution's CIUPA compliance team needs to consume chain data in bulk to run fairness audits (compute denial rates by demographic group, measure score variation by protected characteristics). The spec's privacy-by-design.md applies tokenization; if sensitive fields are tokenized, the fairness team cannot run demographic analysis on chain-captured data. **Ask:** Add to privacy-by-design.md or ai-policy-alignment.md a section: "**CIUPA Article 15 Fairness Testing and Chain Data Tokenization.** The chain captures AI decision inputs (including demographic-correlated data) per the audit.* namespace. For institutions conducting CIUPA Article 15 fairness testing, the tokenization strategy must preserve: (a) raw demographic fields (age, gender if captured) in a separate encrypted-but-decryptable form available only to the institution's fairness-testing team under strict access controls; (b) chain entries themselves remain tokenized for external verification and customer-dispute purposes; (c) the institution maintains a separate fairness-audit dataset (derived from chain data but not itself on the chain) for demographic-disparity analysis. The institution's CIUPA compliance procedures document: which fields are fairness-critical (not tokenized in the fairness-audit dataset), how the fairness team accesses them (encrypted under the institution's audit-team access control, not chain-accessible), and how frequently fairness testing is performed (CIUPA Article 15 does not specify cadence; best practice is quarterly for material credit decisions). The chain itself remains privacy-protected; the fairness-analysis dataset is a separate institutional artifact subject to institutional information-security controls."

### Q-5. EFSR §17 IT general controls and cybersecurity baseline — HSM and KMS custody requirements

EFSR (Electronic Financial Supervisory Regulation) §17 establishes baseline cybersecurity controls for financial institutions, including requirements for cryptographic-key management: keys must be held in secure custody (HSM, KMS), access must be role-segregated, and key operations must be logged. The spec's §10.5 names FIPS 140-2 Level 3 or higher as the HSM standard; EFSR §17 names K-ISMS-P (Korea Information Security Management System Profile) baseline controls as the equivalent standard for Korean institutions. K-ISMS-P is based on ISO 27001 but tailored to Korean banking and is more stringent than FIPS 140-2 in some areas (personnel screening, facility physical security, incident-response timing). The FSS has stated that Korean institutions may use either FIPS Level 3 HSM or a K-ISMS-P certified KMS. The spec should acknowledge this equivalence explicitly so Korean institutions are not forced to choose between US FIPS certification and Korean K-ISMS-P certification. **Ask:** Add to spec §10.5 or design/04-hsm-custody.md: "**International HSM and KMS Certification Equivalence.** The chain requires the master-key (IKM) to be held in a FIPS 140-2 Level 3 or higher device (US NIST standard). Equivalent certifications acceptable for international deployments: (1) **FIPS 140-2 Level 3 (US NIST)** — standard for institutions under Federal Reserve or OCC supervision; (2) **Common Criteria EAL4+ (ISO/IEC 15408)** — equivalent rigorous evaluation standard; (3) **K-ISMS-P certified KMS (Korea)** — Korean banking cybersecurity baseline, acceptable under FSS supervision and EFSR §17 compliance. The institution's control description documents which standard (FIPS, Common Criteria, K-ISMS-P) governs the IKM custody in its jurisdiction and confirms the chosen standard meets the chain's security assumptions."

### Q-8. PIPA §28-2 pseudonymization (가명처리) — chain tokens must be demonstrably irreversible to the examiner

PIPA Article 28-2 (amended March 2023) defines pseudonymization (가명처리) as the processing of personal data such that the data cannot be attributed to a data subject without additional information, provided that such additional information is held separately and subject to technical/organizational safeguards. Under PIPA §28-2, pseudonymized data is treated more leniently than identified personal data (lower consent bars, broader use cases). The chain's tokenization strategy (privacy-by-design.md) uses tokenization; if the tokens are truly irreversible (one-way hash, not encryption with a key held on institution servers), they qualify as pseudonymized under PIPA §28-2. But if the tokens are encrypted (symmetric encryption with a key the institution holds and can use to reverse), they do not qualify and are still treated as personal data under PIPA. During FSS examination, the examiner will ask: "Are your chain tokens pseudonymized per PIPA §28-2, or are they encrypted personal data?" The institution must be able to answer definitively. The spec does not distinguish between pseudonymization (PIPA §28-2) and encryption-with-reversible-key (not pseudonymized). **Ask:** Add to privacy-by-design.md a section: "**PIPA Article 28-2 Pseudonymization vs Encryption-with-Reversible-Key.** The chain's tokenization strategy must choose: (1) **True pseudonymization:** Customer identifier (name, account number, etc.) is one-way hashed or irreversibly transformed; the institution does NOT retain a mapping that would allow reversal. This qualifies as pseudonymized under PIPA §28-2. Advantage: chain entries can be more flexibly shared, analyzed, and disclosed under broader PIPA exemptions. Disadvantage: customer-dispute handling requires the institution to maintain a separate dispute-resolution file indexed by the hashed customer token. (2) **Encryption-with-reversible-key:** Customer identifier is encrypted (AES, etc.) under a key the institution holds and controls; the institution maintains the encryption key separately and can decrypt on demand for customer disputes. This is NOT pseudonymization under PIPA §28-2; it remains personal-data-under-encryption. Advantage: customer disputes are resolvable by decrypting the chain entry. Disadvantage: all PIPA personal-data restrictions apply (narrower consent, stricter sharing rules, mandatory breach notification, etc.). The institution's control description must explicitly name the chosen approach and the rationale. FSS examiners will verify the choice is documented and consistently applied."

### Q-9. AI Basic Act 2025 lifecycle documentation (생명주기 관리) — chain captures inference, but what about training and pre-deployment?

The AI Basic Act 2025 (effective January 2026) introduces mandatory AI lifecycle management (Article 20-2), requiring documentation of: (1) **model development:** training data provenance, hyperparameter selection, fairness testing during training; (2) **pre-deployment testing:** performance testing across demographics, safety testing, model-card preparation; (3) **deployment:** versioning of deployed model, inference-time monitoring, human-oversight assignment; (4) **post-deployment:** performance drift detection, customer-complaint monitoring, periodic fairness re-testing, model updates or retirement. For Korean institutions, the AI Basic Act requires the institution to demonstrate the full lifecycle is documented. The chain covers the deployment and post-deployment phases (inference monitoring, complaint tracking); the institution must produce separate evidence for training and pre-deployment phases. The spec should acknowledge this boundary and recommend how the chain integrates with the institution's AI-lifecycle documentation. **Ask:** Add to design/00-overview.md or ai-policy-alignment.md a section: "**AI Basic Act 2025 Lifecycle Documentation — Chain's Role and Complementary Evidence.** The chain addresses the AI Basic Act's deployment and post-deployment lifecycle phases: (1) **Deployment:** The chain's capture of model version (audit.gen_ai.model), model parameters, and inference-time decisions satisfies AI Basic Act Article 20-2(3) (deployed-model versioning and inference documentation). (2) **Post-deployment monitoring:** The chain's daily event capture and verifier output satisfy Article 20-2(4) requirements for performance monitoring and incident detection. (3) **Training and pre-deployment phases:** The chain does not cover training-data provenance or pre-deployment testing. The institution must produce separate evidence: training-data documentation (source, quality checks, fairness bias audits), pre-deployment test results (performance across demographics, safety testing, model-card), and sign-off from the institution's MRM committee. The institution's control description must name how the chain (inference-monitoring evidence) composes alongside the institution's model-development documentation (training and pre-deployment evidence) to satisfy the full AI Basic Act lifecycle requirement. FSS examiners will review both the chain evidence and the development evidence in parallel during comprehensive examination."

### Q-10. 안내 의무 (duty of guidance) — institution must guide customers about AI-driven decisions, chain is evidence

A unique Korean supervisory concept is the **안내 의무** (duty of guidance), reinforced in the FSS AI Guidelines: financial institutions must proactively guide customers about when AI is involved in decisions that affect them. This is stronger than GDPR's transparency requirement; it is affirmative guidance. When an AI system makes a credit-denial decision, the institution must not just disclose that AI was involved (passive transparency), but must actively explain to the customer the decision factors and offer a reconsideration path (active guidance). ### Q-24. Post-quantum cryptography planning and the quantum-readiness expectation (양자 내성)

**Partial? No.** The spec's technical design is sound for Korean financial institutions and is compatible with all major Korean regulatory frameworks (PIPA, CIUPA, EFTA, AI Basic Act 2025, MyData, FSS AI Guidelines, PIPC expectations). None of the 24 questions constitute blockers to adoption.

A bilateral memorandum of understanding (MOU) between the FFIEC (coordinating US regulators) and the FSC/FSS (Korean financial regulators) could harmonize expectations for institutions operating in both jurisdictions, avoiding the need for parallel implementations or dual-compliance burdens.
