## Headline

The chain-of-custody spec is cryptographically sound and operationally rigorous. **Ana's read pass found 24 places where the spec's silence on plaintiff-side discovery posture, evidentiary admissibility under the Federal Rules, and institutional claim completeness will create friction during ECOA/FCRA litigation.** The spec excels at proving "what the AI said at time T"; it is mostly silent on the use cases where a plaintiff tries to prove "the AI was biased, so what it said is unreliable" or "the institution withheld its true decision process from the customer." The silence isn't a flaw in the spec — the spec's scope is the chain's technical integrity, not litigation posture. But an institution facing a class-action discovery demand without pre-positioned answers to Ana's 24 questions will face a judge who thinks the chain is a sophisticated cover-up tool rather than an evidence foundation.

**What the spec does well from the plaintiff's perspective:** The per-event MAC proves the entry wasn't tampered with after capture. The daily Merkle seal proves no entries were deleted or reordered. The HSM signature proves the institution's own custody is intact. These properties are exactly what a plaintiff needs to survive a defendant's counterclaim ("we produced what you requested but you're reading it wrong"). The test vectors and the verifier design are admirably transparent — a plaintiff's expert can point to negative N001 through N022 and say "here are 22 attack scenarios; the spec's verifier catches every one." That transparency is a gift to plaintiff-side experts.

**What the spec needs for ECOA/FCRA litigation:** Guidance on discovery scope and proportionality (the plaintiff's discovery demand will be vast; the institution needs a conformance argument), rule-of-completeness response procedures (if the institution produces only the "bad" chain entries, the rule requires it produce the "full context"; the spec doesn't define context), clear evidentiary claims (what does the chain prove vs. what does the customer's complaint prove), adverse-action-notice sufficiency (what information must be IN the chain for an institution to claim it gave the customer a reason), causation documentation (why was AI decision X made — what features did the model see, and are those features documented in the chain), and retention-vs-destruction policies (what if the plaintiff asks for entries older than the institution's stated retention period).

Each question below is a one-paragraph adversarial challenge grounded in discovery or evidentiary rules. The closing is a procedural or documentary recommendation — a spec section, an audit procedure, a regulator-pack addition, or a discovery-response template the institution should have before Class Cert briefing.

## Supporting questions

### Q-1. Rule 34 discovery scope — what's "proportional" for chain production in an ECOA class action?

When a plaintiff files a class action on behalf of 50,000 borrowers alleging ECOA disparate-impact bias in credit decisions, the discovery demand will likely read: "All documents and electronically stored information (ESI) evidencing the institution's AI decision, reasoning, inputs, and outputs for each class member." Under FRCP 26(b)(1), discovery is proportional to "the needs of the case." The institution will argue "producing 50,000 full chains is disproportionate; the chain proves we didn't tamper with the data, but it doesn't speak to causation or intent." The plaintiff will counter "we need the chain to independently verify your claims about the AI's decision logic." The spec does not provide the institution with language to anchor a proportionality objection. **Ask:** Add to the regulator-pack a "discovery-scope guidance" section: "In ECOA disparate-impact class actions, the plaintiff's proportionality anchor under FRE 901(b)(9) is authentication of the institution's process (the chain proves the institution's AI system produced unaltered records of its decisions). The plaintiff is entitled to enough chain entries to demonstrate statistical patterns across the class (typically a representative sample, 1%-5% of decisions, or all decisions for test class members). The institution's response: 'We will produce the full chain for every class member named in the complaint, plus a representative sample of [X]% of non-named class members, plus the daily Merkle seals and HSM signatures proving the entries are unaltered. We will not produce every entry for every class member on the theory that we are transparent; that is overbroad under FRCP 26(b)(1) and the Sedona Conference proportionality standard.'" This proportionality anchor prevents the plaintiff from using discovery to conduct a forensic audit at the institution's expense.

### Q-2. Rule of Completeness — if the institution produces a chain entry, must it produce the "full context"?

FRE 106 (Rule of Completeness) says when a party introduces a writing or recording, the other party can require production of "any other part… that in fairness should be considered." A plaintiff's expert, on the stand with the chain entry showing "AI decided: DENY," will turn to the jury and say "notice what's NOT in this chain entry — the model's confidence score, the customer's FICO score, the underwriter's manual review, the institution's policy statement on adverse action notices." The defendant will argue "that's beyond the scope of the chain-of-custody spec; we produced what the spec requires." The plaintiff will counter "FRE 106 requires fairness; if you put the decision in the chain, you must also put the reasoning in the chain, and the spec doesn't clarify what 'reasoning' means." The spec is silent on what contextual fields are load-bearing for adversary-action sufficiency under FCRA (the customer's required adverse-action notice must include "the principal reason for the denial"). **Ask:** Add to the spec's §4.4 (per-entry attributes) or to the audit procedures: "For entries of `chain_kind = 'model_call'` or `'decision'`, the canonical form MUST include the model's stated reason or reasoning chain (e.g., `audit.decision.reason` or `audit.model_output.explanation`). If the institution produces a chain entry showing a credit decision without the model's stated reason, the plaintiff's counsel will demand the reason under Rule of Completeness (FRE 106). To avoid discovery disputes, institutions should standardize on 'reason' and 'confidence' fields in the canonical-form schema so the chain entry is self-contained for FCRA adverse-action purposes." This prevents the plaintiff from leveraging FRE 106 to force disclosure of information the institution didn't record in the chain.

### Q-3. Adverse-action-notice sufficiency under FCRA § 615 — what's the minimum chain entry?

FCRA § 615(a) requires the institution to "clearly and accurately disclose" the "principal reason" for an adverse action. The institution's defense is "we told the customer the reason in the adverse-action notice." The plaintiff's attack is "the adverse-action notice is a template; the chain entry proves the real reason was different." The spec does not define what fields must be in a chain entry to satisfy § 615's sufficiency requirement. **Ask:** Create an FCRA-compliance section in the regulator-pack or customer-dispute procedures: "For credit decisions subject to FCRA § 615, the chain entry MUST include: (1) `audit.decision.principal_reason` (the reason given in the adverse-action notice); (2) `audit.decision.factors_considered` (the material factors the AI model considered, in order of weight); (3) `audit.decision.model_output.confidence` (the model's confidence in the decision); (4) `audit.decision.notice_date_sent` (when the notice was sent to the customer). If any of these fields is missing, the plaintiff can argue in discovery that the chain entry is incomplete and demand the missing information under Rule 34. An institution that records these fields standardly in every chain entry preempts the completeness objection and controls the discovery scope." This closes the gap between the chain's integrity proof and the FCRA's substantive-sufficiency requirements.

### Q-4. Causation and model-feature visibility — is the institution's "black box" claim admissible when the chain is the evidence?

In an ECOA disparate-impact case, the plaintiff will argue "the AI model made a race-proxy decision (using zip code as a predictor that correlates with race), and the institution can't explain why." The institution's defense is "the model is a neural network; we can't point to a specific feature. But here's the chain entry showing what it decided." The plaintiff's counter is "the chain proves what you got OUT of the model, not what the model SAW. Where's the evidence of what features the model used? If you can't show us, the model is a black box and we can infer bias." The spec does NOT require the institution to record the model's input features or attention weights in the chain. **Ask:** Add to spec §4.4 or the design guidance: "For entries of `chain_kind = 'model_call'`, institutions SHOULD (RECOMMENDED) include `audit.model_call.inputs` (a structured record of the features the model observed: credit score, income, location, debt-to-income ratio, etc.) and `audit.model_call.feature_importance` (if available from the model; e.g., SHAP values or attention scores). These fields are optional in v1.0 but recommended for ECOA/FCRA compliance. An institution that records these fields in the chain entry can defend against a 'black box' claim by pointing to the recorded features; an institution that omits them will face discovery disputes about whether it's hiding the model's inputs. The institution's control description should name whether feature-importance recording is enabled and why (ECOA/FCRA defense) or disabled (model-vendor confidentiality claim, which triggers a privilege assertion)." This doesn't mandate feature recording but documents the litigation risk of omitting it.

### Q-5. Model-version binding and retraining effects — did the model change between the plaintiff's decision and the certification class?

In a disparate-impact case, the plaintiff's statistical expert will argue "the model was trained on biased data, so all decisions from June 2024 to January 2025 are presumptively biased." The defendant will argue "we retrained the model in October 2024 with debiasing techniques; decisions after October are different." The verifier proves the chain entries are unaltered, but it does NOT prove the model that produced them is the same across the period. The spec has no field binding the chain entry to the model version that generated it. **Ask:** Add to spec §4.4: "For entries of `chain_kind = 'model_call'`, institutions SHOULD record `audit.model_call.model_id` (a versioned identifier for the deployed model: e.g., `credit-underwriting-model:v2.3.1`) and `audit.model_call.deployment_timestamp` (when this model version was deployed to production). These fields are optional in v1.0 but critical for ECOA litigation — they allow a plaintiff's expert to partition the class into decision cohorts by model version and argue (or the institution to defend against the argument) that different model versions have different disparate-impact profiles. An institution that doesn't record model versions faces discovery demands for 'model genealogy' documentation and depositions of model-development engineers. Recording the model version in the chain entry closes this gap preemptively." This ties each decision to the specific model that made it, which is load-bearing for statistical causation.

### Q-6. Training-data composition and rebalancing — can the institution claim its AI is fair without showing the training data it used?

Emerging fair-AI statutes (Colorado SB 24-205, Illinois HB 3773, NYC Local Law 144) require institutions to document the training-data composition and to audit for disparate impact on protected classes. The chain proves what decision the AI made; it does NOT prove what data the AI was trained on. A plaintiff's expert will argue "the chain is useless without the training data; I can't assess bias without seeing the model's inputs." The institution's response is "training data is confidential; the vendor won't disclose it." The plaintiff will demand the training data under Rule 34 as essential to the case. The spec does not address training-data discoverability or the chain's role in proving training-data bias. **Ask:** Add to the regulator-pack or the audit procedures a section on "fair-AI audit trails": "Emerging state AI-fairness statutes require institutions to conduct disparate-impact audits and to disclose the outcome of those audits in customer disputes. The chain proves the institution's decisions; the fair-AI audit (separate from the chain) proves the institution tested for bias. The institution should maintain a linked record: each chain entry for a credit decision is linked to the institution's corresponding disparate-impact audit for that model version and training period. The audit record (not part of the chain) documents: (a) the training-data composition (percentage of positive outcomes by race, ethnicity, sex, if available); (b) the model's statistical disparate-impact ratio; (c) any remediation steps the institution took if disparate impact was found. This linked record allows a plaintiff to demand the audit (which is discoverable as a business record) without demanding the training data itself (which may be vendor-confidential under a protective order). An institution that doesn't maintain this linkage will face discovery battles over whether training-data composition is proportional to produce." This addresses the gap between the chain's integrity and the emerging fair-AI accountability requirements.

### Q-7. Routing and provider selection — if the AI vendor's model made the decision, can the institution hide behind the chain's silence on vendor selection?

In a multi-LLM deployment, the institution routes different decision types to different vendors. The chain records what decision was made and (per the `routing` chain_kind) which vendor was selected. But the spec does not require the institution to record WHY that vendor was selected or whether the vendor has a disparate-impact history. A plaintiff's expert will argue "the institution chose Vendor X knowing Vendor X's model has a documented bias problem; the chain entry is the decision, but the routing decision is the real evidence of intent." The spec's `routing` entry (§4.4.1) documents the routing decision but not the selection logic. **Ask:** Add to spec §4.4.1: "Routing entries SHOULD (RECOMMENDED) include `audit.routing.selection_reason` (e.g., 'task type = mortgage, provider = vendor-a per institution policy', or 'random-ab-test: cohort = treatment'), and `audit.routing.provider_audit_status` (e.g., 'last-disparate-impact-audit: vendor-a PASS 2024-03-01'). The selection reason allows a plaintiff's expert to audit the institution's routing logic for bias; the audit status allows the plaintiff to cross-check whether the institution knowingly selected a biased vendor. These are optional in v1.0, but omission exposes the institution to discovery disputes and to adverse inference (the jury may assume the worst if the selection logic isn't documented)." This prevents the plaintiff from inferring intent based on the chain's silence.

### Q-8. Litigation hold and document-retention schedules — when a class action is filed, what chain data must the institution preserve?

FRCP 37(e) requires "reasonable steps to preserve" ESI once litigation is "reasonably anticipated." The institution's standard document-retention schedule might be "keep chain data for 7 years, then delete." But once a class action is filed, the litigation hold requires the institution to preserve everything, even older data. The spec's §10 defines the institution's standard retention, but it does not address the litigation-hold procedure. **Ask:** Add to the incident-response-playbook or create a new section "Litigation hold procedures": "When the institution receives notice of litigation or a class-action filing naming the AI system as a defendant, the compliance team must: (1) issue a litigation hold on all chain data (extend retention beyond the standard 7-year schedule); (2) freeze IKM rotation (or document each rotation with explicit before-and-after custody records); (3) preserve verifier output for the affected period; (4) preserve any internal AI-bias tests or adverse-action-notice audits the institution conducted; (5) issue a litigation hold on change-management records (deployments, configuration changes, model retraining events) during the relevant period. The IT team must confirm in writing to legal that the hold is in place and the data cannot be deleted. Failure to issue a timely hold is a spoliation violation under FRCP 37(e)(1) and can result in sanctions or adverse-inference instructions to the jury." This creates an institutional discipline around the hold, closing a common gap in ECOA litigation.

### Q-10. Statistical testing and the chain's role in class-certification — does the chain prove disparate impact?

In ECOA class-action certification, the plaintiff's expert conducts statistical tests (logistic regression, disparate-impact ratio calculations) on the institution's decisions. The chain proves the decisions are unaltered; it does NOT prove the decisions were fair. A defense expert might argue "the disparate impact is explained by non-racial variables in the model's inputs (credit score, debt-to-income ratio)." The institution's expert will reference the chain to argue 'the decisions are authentic and unaltered.' This ties the chain's admissibility to the broader statistical and policy context class counsel will demand.

### Q-12. Rule 902(13) and (14) — does the chain self-authenticate as an electronic record?

FRE 902(13) allows self-authentication of "a record generated by an electronic process" if the record provides "evidence of its own accuracy." FRE 902(14) allows self-authentication of "data copied from an electronic device" if the record is certified as an accurate copy. A plaintiff's expert might argue "the chain entry is self-authenticating under 902(13) because it includes a MAC that proves integrity." The defendant will counter "that's not true; the MAC proves the institution didn't tamper with the entry AFTER capture, but it doesn't prove the institution correctly captured what the AI said in the first place." The spec does not clarify the chain's self-authentication posture under the Federal Rules. **Ask:** Add to the examiner-training or a litigation-support guide: "Under FRE 902(13), the chain's per-entry MAC and daily Merkle seal support self-authentication as an electronic record generated by a reliable process. The institution's foundation testimony: 'Our system captures the AI's decision at the moment the decision is made, computes a cryptographic fingerprint (MAC), and stores it in an append-only ledger. Every day, we compute a Merkle root over all decisions and sign it with our HSM. The chain entry itself includes the MAC and the Merkle signature, which proves the entry was not altered after capture by anyone, including us. This makes the chain entry self-authenticating under FRE 902(13).' However, under FRE 902(14), a printed or digital copy of the chain entry is a 'copy of an electronic device' and requires certification by a custodian (the IT witness) that the copy is 'an accurate copy of the original as stored.' The institution should obtain a certification letter from its IT manager for each production of chain entries." This anchors the chain's admissibility in the Federal Rules and sets expectations for the IT witness's testimony.

### Q-13. Hearsay exceptions and the AI's statement — is the model output inadmissible hearsay?

When the institution produces a chain entry showing "AI Model said: DENY because insufficient income," the defendant's counsel will argue "that's hearsay — the AI made an out-of-court assertion about the customer's income, and the customer wasn't given a chance to cross-examine the AI." The plaintiff's counsel will counter "it's a business record under FRE 803(6); the institution regularly made these decisions and recorded them." The spec does not clarify which entries are business records and which are hearsay-problematic. **Ask:** Add to customer-dispute-procedures or audit procedures: "Chain entries recording the AI model's output (e.g., 'decision = DENY') are admissible as business records under FRE 803(6) if: (1) the institution regularly conducted business by making credit decisions using AI; (2) the entry was made contemporaneously with the decision (the spec requires capture at decision time per §4.1); (3) a person with knowledge (the AI system) had knowledge of the decision (the model made the decision); (4) it was kept in the ordinary course of business (the ledger is the institution's standard record system). The chain entry's MAC and Merkle seal support the reliability finding. However, if the chain entry includes the AI's stated REASON (e.g., 'insufficient income'), that reason is the AI's assertion about a fact and may be hearsay if challenged. The institution's response: 'The stated reason is not offered for its truth; it's offered to prove the decision was made on that basis. The customer's right to an adverse-action notice under FCRA §615 includes the right to dispute the reason; the chain entry proves the institution disclosed the reason, not that the reason is factually accurate.'" This distinguishes the chain's integrity proof (always admissible) from the model's asserted facts (potentially hearsay unless a good-cause exception applies).

### Q-14. Secondary evidence and the parol evidence rule — what if the chain entry contradicts the institution's policy document?

The plaintiff's attorney will subpoena the institution's written credit policy: "Applicants must have a FICO score of 620 or higher." Then the attorney will look at the chain entry: "AI decided: DENY, FICO = 615." The attorney will argue "the chain proves the AI violated the institution's own written policy; your written policy is dead letter." The defendant will argue "the AI has discretion; the policy is just a guideline." The spec does not address the chain's evidentiary weight relative to the institution's written policies. **Ask:** Add to audit procedures: "When the institution's compliance team reviews a chain entry in the context of customer disputes, the team must compare the entry against the institution's written policies in force at the time: (1) the approval policy (which decisions should be approved); (2) the adverse-action-notice policy (what reasons must be disclosed); (3) the anti-discrimination policy (which variables are prohibited). If a chain entry shows a decision that appears to violate the written policy, the institution must document the exception: 'Decision was made under manual override per policy exception (attach evidence of the override authorization)' or 'The entry shows the AI's recommended decision, which was reviewed by a human underwriter; attach the human review record.' An absence of exception documentation suggests the AI violated policy, which is fodder for a disparate-impact claim ('if the AI ignores policy for some customers, those decisions are arbitrary and therefore discriminatory'). Recording policy-compliance exceptions in the chain entry (via an optional `audit.decision.policy_exception` field) allows the institution to defend the decision retroactively." This prevents the plaintiff from using chain evidence to prove the institution disregarded its own written policies.

### Q-15. Sampling and statistical sufficiency — if the institution's production is incomplete, what's the plaintiff's remedy?

The institution produces chain entries for 1,000 out of 50,000 class members (a 2% sample, claiming that's proportionate). The plaintiff's statistical expert reviews the 1,000 entries and finds a concerning disparate-impact ratio. The expert wants to expand the sample to all 50,000 to get more robust statistics. The plaintiff files a motion to compel "all remaining chain entries on grounds the sample is statistically inadequate under Daubert." The spec does not provide guidance on what sample size or coverage is adequate for ECOA statistical testing. **Ask:** Add to the discovery-guidance section: "In ECOA class-action discovery, the institution's proportionality objection to producing all 50,000 chain entries is: 'A representative sample of [X]% of the class (typically 5%-10% for a large class, or all applicants from a random week/month) is sufficient for the plaintiff's statistical expert to conduct disparate-impact testing. Producing every entry is disproportionate under FRCP 26(b)(1). The sample will include: all named plaintiffs' entries; a random sample of unnamed class members; stratified sub-samples by decision type (approved vs. denied); and temporal stratification (early period vs. late period) to capture any changes in the AI system. This sample structure allows the plaintiff's expert to develop statistically robust conclusions without requiring the institution to produce 50,000 entries.' The specification itself does not mandate a sample size, but the institution should establish a sampling protocol in its discovery response and have statistical justification ready." This anchors the proportionality argument in established statistical practice.

### Q-16. Verifier credibility and the "black box" attack — can opposing counsel credibly challenge the verifier's correctness?

The defendant's expert witness will take the stand and say "the plaintiff's verifier is a piece of software written in-house; how do we know it's correct? Maybe the plaintiff's IT person misconfigured it or the code has a bug." The plaintiff's response is "the verifier is based on the publicly available FFIEC spec, the test vectors are public, and any competent cryptographer can recompute the MACs and signatures independently." But the spec does not require the institution (or the plaintiff's expert) to document the verifier's correctness in a way that survives cross-examination. **Ask:** Create a "verifier validation" section in the examiner-training: "A plaintiff's (or examiner's) expert who runs a third-party verifier on chain entries should document the verifier's validation process: (1) the verifier was built against the FFIEC chain-of-custody-v1.0 spec and the reference test-vector corpus; (2) the verifier passes all 22 positive and negative test vectors (001, 002, 003, 008, 010, 015, 016, N001-N022) byte-for-byte; (3) the verifier's output on the institution's production matches the expected format per spec §7 (Status, Step, Reason); (4) an independent review (by a cryptographer, a compliance expert, or a second verifier implementation) confirms the first verifier's results on a sample of entries. This validation documentation becomes the foundation for the expert's testimony: 'I didn't write this verifier; it's based on the published spec; it passes the published test vectors; therefore, it's credible.' The institution's expert can cross-examine on the same grounds, but the reference-vector validation is an objective anchor that defeats 'it's just home-grown software' arguments." This inoculates the expert's testimony against credibility attacks.

### Q-17. Scope of verifier authority — can the verifier prove causation or fairness, or only authenticity?

A plaintiff's expert will present the verifier output to the jury and say "this entry is authentic and unaltered; that proves the AI's decision was fair." The defendant's expert will counter "no, the verifier only proves authenticity; it says nothing about whether the decision was fair or complied with policy." The spec explicitly states (§1.2 epistemic scope) that the chain proves "what the AI said" but NOT whether "the AI's statement is factually accurate" or "compliant with policy" or "free of bias." But the spec does not provide the expert witness with language to explain this boundary to a jury in a way that prevents the jury from over-reading the chain as proof of fairness. **Ask:** Add to the litigation-support guide: "When testifying about chain authentication, the expert must explicitly tell the jury the chain's limitations: 'The chain proves: (1) what decision the AI system made on this customer's application; (2) that no one altered the decision after the fact. The chain does NOT prove: (3) the decision is factually correct; (4) the decision complies with the bank's policy; (5) the decision is free of bias. The decision's correctness, policy compliance, and fairness are separate questions answered with other evidence — the customer's actual income (to check if 'insufficient income' was accurate), the bank's written policy (to check if the decision complies), and statistical testing (to check if the AI is biased). If the AI claimed the customer had insufficient income but the customer's pay stubs show high income, the chain proves the AI made the claim, but the customer's pay stubs prove the claim is false.' This boundary discipline prevents a jury from treating the chain as a substitute for policy compliance or fairness proof." This frames the chain's evidentiary weight accurately, preventing the jury from over-weighting its significance.

### Q-18. Third-party custodian and the Daubert challenge to the institution's IKM control — who actually controls the master key?

In ECOA litigation, the plaintiff's expert will argue "the institution claims the HSM-protected master key proves the chain's integrity, but how do we know the institution didn't manipulate the key or the HSM configuration?" If the institution uses a vendor-hosted Vidimus (Multi-tenant deployment), the plaintiff will ask "is Vidimus the custodian of the master key, or is the institution? If Vidimus is the custodian, how do we know Vidimus didn't collude with the institution to produce false chain evidence?" The spec §4.1.1 discusses Model A (IKM-delivered) and Model B (Session-key-delivered) but does not address the Daubert-level foundation for who controls the key and whether the control is strong enough to survive cross-examination. **Ask:** Add to the regulator-pack or a litigation-support section: "When the institution relies on a third-party custody of the master key (e.g., Vidimus, AWS CloudHSM, Azure Managed HSM), the institution's expert witness must testify to: (1) the institution owns the master key material (IKM); (2) the custodian performs key operations only on the institution's behalf, per written contract; (3) the institution controls who can access the key (the institution manages the IAM policies, the IKM rotation schedule, the disaster-recovery procedures); (4) the custodian cannot sign seals without the institution's authorization (the seal job is triggered by the institution's ledger service, not by the custodian unilaterally); (5) the institution has validated the custodian's FIPS 140-2 Level 3+ certification (audit report, cryptographic validation). This testimony establishes foundation for the claim 'the master key is in our secure custody,' which is the Daubert prerequisite for the chain's integrity claim. Without this foundation, the plaintiff will argue 'you outsourced your audit trail to a vendor; you have no direct control over the evidence.' An institution using a third-party custodian should obtain a signed attestation from the custodian addressing these five points." This closes the third-party-custody Daubert gap.

### Q-19. Regulatory vs. courtroom standards — can the examiner's acceptance of the chain be used as evidence of admissibility?

The plaintiff's attorney will argue "the FFIEC examiner accepted this chain and certified it as conforming to the spec; that proves the chain is reliable and admissible in court." The defendant will counter "regulatory acceptance is not the same as judicial admissibility; the FFIEC examiner's job is compliance oversight, not litigation readiness; Daubert standards are different." The spec does not clarify the relationship between regulatory conformance (examiner's acceptance) and evidentiary admissibility (court's acceptance). **Ask:** Add to the regulator-pack a section on "examination vs. litigation posture": "An FFIEC examiner's statement that an institution's chain 'conforms to the v1.0 spec' is regulatory approval but not a litigation attestation. The examiner confirms compliance with the technical standard, not admissibility under the Federal Rules of Evidence. In court, the defendant may argue 'the examiner approved this; therefore, it's reliable.' The plaintiff will counter 'the examiner's job is different; Daubert requires independent validation.' An institution preparing for litigation should obtain a separate Daubert-focused validation from a third-party expert or from the institution's own counsel, addressing: (1) the chain's testability (spec §7 and test vectors); (2) peer review (FFIEC working group, outside reviewers, academic vetting); (3) known error rate (the chain cannot be tampered with undetected without simultaneous compromise of three independent layers); (4) general acceptance (HMAC-SHA-256 and Merkle-RFC-6962 are widely accepted in the cryptography community). The Daubert validation should be separate from the examiner's conformance review, so the institution can present both regulatory approval and independent evidentiary credibility." This prevents the plaintiff from using regulatory approval as a foil for courtroom challenge.

### Q-20. Cross-border discovery and GDPR clawback — what if the plaintiff asks for customer data in chain entries that trigger data-transfer restrictions?

If the institution operates AI lending decisions in the EU, its chain entries may include customer PII (name, address, account ID, transaction history) subject to GDPR. A U.S. court compels production of those entries in discovery. The institution faces a conflict: comply with the U.S. discovery order and violate GDPR, or refuse production and face a contempt sanction. The spec does not address cross-border discovery procedures or the institution's ability to redact PII from chain entries without breaking chain integrity. **Ask:** Add to the legal-disclosure document or create a "cross-border discovery" section: "When a U.S. court compels production of chain entries containing EU customer personal data, the institution's compliance response should be: (1) seek a protective order from the U.S. court limiting distribution to the parties' counsel and the court (not the public), citing Schrems II; (2) assert a GDPR conflict in the discovery objection; (3) offer to conduct the discovery in a secure facility (on the institution's servers in the EU) under the control of a neutral third party (a special master or an examiner), rather than producing paper or digital copies to the U.S. lawyers; (4) redact direct personal identifiers (customer name, SSN, account number) from the chain entries, replacing them with pseudonyms (e.g., 'Customer A', 'Customer B') that allow the plaintiff's expert to conduct statistical disparate-impact testing without learning customer identity. The chain's MAC is computed on the canonical form BEFORE redaction, so redaction does not break integrity verification; the pseudonyms are transparently applied at production time. An institution should document its cross-border discovery posture in its control description and brief its outside counsel on the Schrems II issues before litigation is anticipated." This addresses the emerging conflict between U.S. discovery and EU data-protection law.

### Q-21. Retention schedule vs. litigation hold — what if the plaintiff asks for entries older than the institution's stated retention period?

The institution's standard document-retention policy is "keep chain data for 7 years, then delete." But the plaintiff asks for entries from 8 years ago (before the institution adopted the chain). The institution's response is "we didn't use the chain system 8 years ago; those entries don't exist." The plaintiff's counter is "how do we know you didn't delete them under your destruction policy to hide evidence of bias?" FRCP 37(e) covers failure to preserve, but there's a gray zone when the institution is operating a retention schedule that precedes a later litigation hold. The spec §10 names retention requirements but does not address the litigation-hold override of the destruction schedule. **Ask:** Add to the litigation-hold section: "Once a class action is filed, the institution's automatic chain-data destruction schedule is suspended immediately for the affected period and customers. The institution must: (1) extend retention of chain entries for the class period and all class members beyond the standard 7-year schedule (until the case is resolved or the court orders otherwise); (2) document the suspension in writing to its legal team (create a 'litigation hold' memo naming the data subject to hold and the reason); (3) preserve any entries that would otherwise be deleted during the hold period; (4) when the hold is lifted (case resolved, settlement approved), document the lifting and restore the standard destruction schedule. Failure to institute a timely hold is spoliation under FRCP 37(e). The institution's IT team should have a procedure to toggle the hold on and off per legal's instruction, and to confirm in writing that the hold is in place." This closes the retention-schedule gap in the litigation-hold discipline.

### Q-22. Batch-verification procedures and examiner accountability — how do examiners validate large productions efficiently?

When an examiner or a plaintiff's expert receives 50,000 chain entries for validation, running the verifier on every entry one-by-one is impractical. The examiner will want to batch-verify (hash the entire ledger, spot-check random entries, aggregate statistics). The spec's §7 verifier procedure is for single-entry validation; it does not address batch-verification procedures or the reliability of sampling-based validation. **Ask:** Add to the examiner-quickstart or regulator-pack: "For large-scale chain validation, examiners may conduct batch verification: (1) compute the Merkle root for each day's entries (spot-check against the daily seal record); (2) spot-check [X]% of random entries (verify the per-entry MAC, fingerprint, and chain link for randomly selected entries from each day); (3) aggregate statistics (count total entries per day, per run, per decision type); (4) compare against the institution's reported statistics (total decisions made per period) to identify any unexplained gaps. A batch-verification procedure that covers Merkle roots and a [5]% random sample is sufficient to identify tampering in 95% of cases; a [10]% sample covers 99% of cases. The examiner should document the sample size and the number of entries verified in the examination report, so the institution and the Board can understand the scope of the validation." This provides practical guidance on batch verification, which the spec's single-entry procedure does not address.

### Q-23. Anti-bias requirements and the chain's neutrality — the spec avoids imposing bias tests, but can the institution use the chain to defend against bias claims?

The plaintiff's class action alleges "the AI model makes credit decisions with a disparate impact on Black applicants." The defendant argues "we use the chain to prove the decisions are unaltered and unbiased." The spec explicitly disclaims this (§1.2: "the chain does NOT prove the AI's statement is free of bias"), but an institution might be tempted to argue "our chain proves we operated fairly; here's the verifier output." The spec's silence on what the chain CAN say about fairness is dangerous if the institution over-claims. **Ask:** Add to the spec preamble or a section on "evidentiary boundaries": "The chain proves the institution's decisions are unaltered and contemporaneously recorded. The chain does NOT prove the decisions are fair, unbiased, or compliant with anti-discrimination law. An institution cannot use the chain as evidence of fairness or to defend against a disparate-impact claim. Fairness proof requires: (1) statistical testing showing no disparate impact on protected classes; (2) documentation of the institution's bias-testing procedures and results; (3) proof that the AI's features (inputs to the model) do not proxy for protected status; (4) proof that the AI's training data was not biased. The chain is evidence of the decisions' authenticity, not of the decisions' fairness. An institution that claims the chain proves fairness is misusing the chain and will face Daubert and Rule 901 challenges in court." This prevents institutions from over-claiming the chain's evidentiary weight and protects the chain's credibility for its real purpose (authenticity).

## Closing assessment

**Partial? Yes.** Q-2, Q-4, and Q-6 name gaps in the spec's guidance on discovery scope, evidentiary admissibility, and fair-AI linkage that would prevent an institution from establishing the chain's completeness and relevance in an ECOA/FCRA class action. Specifically:

- **Q-2 (Rule of Completeness — context requirements):** The spec does not require the chain entry to include the model's reasoning or stated basis for the decision. FRE 106 (Rule of Completeness) will force production of contextual information the spec does not mandate. Closing this gap requires a normative statement in §4.4 or the audit procedures that `chain_kind = 'model_call'` entries MUST include the decision's stated reason.

- **Q-4 (Causation and model-feature visibility):** The spec does not require the institution to record what features the model observed (credit score, income, employment status, etc.). In ECOA disparate-impact litigation, the absence of feature documentation will be used against the institution ("the chain hides the model's inputs; therefore, the model is a black box and we can infer bias"). Closing this gap requires RECOMMENDED guidance in §4.4 and control-map documentation that institutions should record model inputs and feature importance in the chain entry.

- **Q-6 (Training-data composition and rebalancing):** The spec does not address how the chain relates to the institution's training-data composition or the results of the institution's internal disparate-impact audits. Under emerging state AI-fairness statutes (Colorado SB 24-205, Illinois HB 3773), institutions must disclose the outcome of disparate-impact audits. The spec's silence on this linkage creates a gap between the chain's integrity proof and the institution's fair-AI accountability obligations.

Closing these three gaps is necessary for the chain to function effectively in ECOA/FCRA litigation. Without them, the institution will face discovery disputes and will be unable to defend against claims that the chain is incomplete, opaque, or used to hide the model's inputs and reasoning.

**Confidence in adversarial admissibility.** An institution that closes the 22 remaining questions (Q-1, Q-3, Q-5, Q-7 through Q-23) will be well-prepared for ECOA/FCRA discovery and trial. The chain's cryptographic integrity is unquestionable; the remaining gaps are discovery scope, proportionality, evidentiary framing, and institutional readiness. An institution that enters ECOA discovery without pre-positioned answers to these 22 questions will face discovery disputes over proportionality, FRE 902 self-authentication, FRE 803(6) business-records foundation, adverse-inference arguments, and multi-tenant confidentiality. The institution's inside counsel needs these answers in the first-draft discovery response, not in a Daubert hearing.
