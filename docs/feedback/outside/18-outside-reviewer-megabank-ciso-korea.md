## Headline

The FFIEC chain-of-custody v1.0 spec is **cryptographically well-grounded and operationally defensible for US banks under FFIEC oversight and regulatory timelines.** For Korean Tier-1 banks operating under K-ISMS-P recertification, Electronic Financial Supervisory Regulation §17 (EFSR §17 / 전자금융감독규정), and the mandatory 망분리 (network separation) architecture, **the spec has four load-bearing gaps that would prevent deployment without institutional compensating controls and spec clarification.** The gaps are not cryptographic; they are operational, architectural, and regulatory-integration gaps specific to Korean financial baseline.

The four gaps are: (1) **Regulatory-timeline alignment gap — real-time monitoring vs archive-only design.** EFSR §17(2) mandates real-time monitoring; the spec's chain is designed as an append-only audit archive that is verified after the fact. The spec does NOT address how a bank integrates the chain into a 24/7 SOC's real-time detection pipeline without breaking append-only guarantees or creating detection latency. (2) **망분리 (network separation) architectural gap — ledger-server placement.** The spec allows vendor-hosted topologies in US or EU cloud. Korean 망분리 law forbids customer data from leaving Korean jurisdiction without explicit FSS approval. The spec does NOT specify Korean-jurisdiction deployment topology or address the architectural implications of placing the chain's ledger server on-premises while the SDK is in the cloud-hosted AI service. (3) **KISA incident-notification clock integration gap.** The KISA Cybersecurity Notification System requires notification within 2 hours if a breach affects >1,000 customers. The chain's daily Merkle seal detection window is 24 hours. The spec does NOT address how a bank detects a compromise and notifies KISA while the chain is still verifying daily seals, or what the minimum-viable real-time detection mechanism is. (4) **K-ISMS-P control-mapping gap — audit trail completeness for controls 3.2 (cryptography) and 9 (monitoring & logging).** K-ISMS-P requires documented cryptographic key management (control 3.2) and audit logging completeness (control 9.1–9.3) with quarterly personal-data-flow documentation. The spec names HSM custody (control 3.2) but does NOT provide the audit-trail artifacts (seal-operation logs, key-rotation logs, key-access logs) that K-ISMS-P auditors demand. The spec's audit procedures (P-1 through P-10) do NOT align with K-ISMS-P's specific log-retention windows (7 years for certain events) and personnel accountability (SCA-equivalent dual approval).

Min-Jun files a **Partial.** The gaps are manageable through institutional compensating controls documented in the bank's K-ISMS-P control descriptions (control 2.10 for access, control 3.2 for crypto, control 9 for logging) and in its EFSR §17 implementation procedures. But the spec could serve Korean banks better by naming the Korean regulatory baseline explicitly, by clarifying the real-time monitoring integration point, by directing Korean users to the 망분리 compliance framework, and by providing a K-ISMS-P control-to-spec-section mapping matrix.

**Tachles (bottom line, 명확히).** A Tier-1 Korean bank deploying this chain would: (1) self-host the ledger server and seal-job HSM on-premises (rule out vendor-hosted topology for customer-identified data), (2) integrate the chain with the 24/7 SOC's real-time detection pipeline via daily seal-verification results and anomaly triggers (daily seal fail = incident, integration via SIEM), (3) pre-position the chain evidence in incident-response playbooks for KISA and FSS notification (evidence ready, clock doesn't wait for verification), (4) map every seal operation, every key rotation, and every chain event to K-ISMS-P controls 3.2 and 9.1–9.3 (audit trail completeness for the 3-year recertification audit), (5) document the bank's 망분리 architecture and confirm with FSS that on-premises ledger + cloud AI is acceptable (may require addendum to the bank's cloud framework MOU). With these compensating controls, the chain significantly strengthens the bank's cyber-resilience posture under K-ISMS-P, EFSR §17, and the nation-state threat model. Without them, the chain becomes a liability: it creates a single point of integrity (HSM signature) and a high-value evidence target for an APT (stealing the chain compromises decision audit trails for 7 years under K-ISMS-P retention rules). The difference between "strengthens resilience" and "becomes a liability" is institutional discipline plus spec clarification. The spec should make this choice explicit for Korean readers and provide a K-ISMS-P control mapping.

## Supporting questions

### Q-1. Real-time monitoring integration — EFSR §17(2) vs daily-seal detection latency

Electronic Financial Supervisory Regulation §17(2) (전자금융감독규정 제17조 제2항) mandates "실시간 감시" (real-time monitoring) — the bank's 24/7 SOC must detect cyber incidents on production systems within minutes, not hours. The spec's chain design provides daily Merkle seals (verified at UTC midnight cadence). An attacker who compromises the ledger server at 00:01 UTC can forge events for the entire day; detection happens at 24:01 when the verifier recomputes the root and finds it mismatched. This 24-hour detection window does NOT satisfy EFSR §17(2) real-time monitoring. The spec does NOT address how a bank integrates the chain into a real-time SOC workflow without: (a) adding out-of-band real-time detection mechanisms (defeating the point of the chain), or (b) creating false positives (treating every unverified event as suspicious). **Ask:** Add to spec §6 (Deployment topologies) or a new operational-integration guide `13-efsr-17-real-time-integration.md`: "**EFSR §17(2) real-time monitoring integration:** (1) The chain's daily Merkle seal provides evidence of server-side tampering with 24-hour detection latency. This is NOT sufficient for EFSR §17(2) real-time monitoring. (2) Institutions MUST supplement with real-time detection mechanisms independent of the chain: (a) **Host-based detection:** Monitor the ledger server for unexpected process spawning, file modifications, or OTLP receiver changes. (b) **Log anomaly detection:** The OTLP receiver logs every ingest, rejection, and re-verification event; a SIEM with ML-based anomaly detection flags unusual patterns (e.g., 1000x spike in rejections, zero events for >1 minute, out-of-sequence arrival). (c) **Ledger-storage monitoring:** The ledger's append-only storage (filesystem, database transaction log) is monitored for unexpected UPDATE/DELETE attempts; any such attempt triggers an immediate alert (the append-only invariant is violated). (d) **Daily seal-verification result alerting:** The daily seal-verification job (§4.2) is integrated with the SOC's incident-management system; a FAIL result (root mismatch) is immediately escalated as a Severity-1 incident. (3) **For K-ISMS-P control 9.2 (real-time monitoring compliance):** The bank documents all real-time detection mechanisms in the control description and provides SIEM screenshot evidence showing the chain as one component of a multi-layered real-time posture. The bank does NOT claim the chain alone satisfies EFSR §17(2); the chain is the long-tail evidence archive, and real-time detection is the immediate-response mechanism. (4) **SIEM integration:** The chain's daily seal-verification result is exported to the SIEM as a synthetic event (success or failure); the SIEM's incident-correlation engine uses this as one signal among many. If the daily seal fails AND the SOC detected unauthorized ledger-server access logs simultaneously, the events are correlated and the incident is escalated to the IR team."

### Q-2. 망분리 (Network Separation) architecture — where does the ledger server live?

Korean network-separation regulation (망분리 지침, issued by FSS) requires logical and (for tier-1 banks) physical separation between internal banking systems and the internet. Tier-1 banks cannot allow customer-identified data to cross the 망분리 boundary into the internet or into vendor-hosted cloud without explicit FSS approval and a separate network-access control document (정보보호관리방침 추가 사항). The spec allows vendor-hosted ledger topologies (§6.3, multi-tenant in US/EU cloud). A Korean bank using vendor-hosted Herald.Compliance would have chain events (which may contain customer identifiers, decision inputs, or transaction references) flowing outside Korea. Under 망분리, this is likely non-compliant without FSS approval. The spec does NOT name Korean-specific deployment topologies or address the architectural implications of separating SDK (cloud AI service) from ledger (on-premises for 망분리 compliance). **Ask:** Add to spec §6 (Deployment topologies) or a new document `06a-korea-network-separation.md`: "**망분리 (Network Separation) compliance for Korean deployment:** (1) **Regulation context:** Korean Electronic Financial Supervisory Regulation (전자금융감독규정) mandates network separation (망분리) — tier-1 banks must logically and physically separate internal banking systems (customer data, decision engines, fund-transfer systems) from the internet. Cloud SaaS systems that process customer data must either be air-gapped (no internet access) or explicitly approved by the FSS (금융감독원) in writing. (2) **Compliant topologies for Korean tier-1 banks:** (a) **On-premises ledger, on-premises SDK:** The chain ledger server and the SDK host are both on the internal network, air-gapped from the internet except for outbound API calls to the LLM provider. (b) **On-premises ledger, cloud SDK with transit encryption:** The SDK runs in cloud-hosted GenAI service (e.g., AWS SageMaker in Seoul region, approved per the bank's cloud framework MOU). The OTLP wire between SDK and on-premises ledger is encrypted (TLS 1.3, mTLS with client certificate pinning) and runs through the bank's network gateway, which enforces 망분리 controls. The ledger server remains on-premises in a Seoul colocation facility or the bank's own data center. (c) **FSS-approved cross-boundary topology:** The bank submits a 정보보호관리방침 추가 사항 (supplementary control document) to the FSS naming: the vendor (Herald.Compliance), the cloud jurisdiction, the data minimization measures (tokenization of customer identifiers), and the encryption-key custody (the bank holds decryption keys, vendor cannot decrypt in-transit or at-rest data). The FSS approves in writing; the bank appends the FSS letter to its K-ISMS-P control description (control 2.10 access control + 3.2 cryptography). (3) **For K-ISMS-P control 2.10 (접근제어):** The bank documents network boundaries, firewall rules, and the OTLP ingress point. The control description names which IP addresses/VPCs are allowed; the verifier spot-checks the firewall logs. (4) **For K-ISMS-P control 3.2 (암호화):** If OTLP traffic crosses the 망분리 boundary, it MUST be encrypted end-to-end and the key MUST be held by the bank (not the vendor). Key rotation logs are audited quarterly. (5) **Non-compliant topologies:** Vendor-hosted multi-tenant cloud ledger that processes Korean customer data (unless FSS-approved via #2c). The spec MUST be explicit that this topology requires institutional choice and regulatory approval; it is not plug-and-play."

### Q-3. KISA incident-notification clock — 2 hours vs 24-hour seal detection window

The KISA Cybersecurity Notification System (사이버 위협 신고 체계) mandates that if a breach affects more than 1,000 customers, the bank MUST notify KISA within 2 hours of determining the breach occurred. The spec's chain provides 24-hour detection latency (daily seal mismatch). The bank cannot wait 24 hours to determine whether a compromise happened; it must make a preliminary determination within 2 hours and notify KISA, then provide evidence to KISA and the FSS as the investigation proceeds. The spec does NOT address how the chain's evidence integrates with the KISA-notification timeline or what "preliminary determination" means when the chain is still verifying. **Ask:** Add to incident-response-playbook.md a new section "KISA incident-notification clock and chain evidence integration": "(1) **KISA timeline:** 사이버 위협정보 공유 체계 — if a breach affects >1,000 customers, notify KISA within 2 hours of determination. 'Determination' is a preliminary assessment based on available evidence (anomalous logs, SOC alerts, user reports, initial forensics), NOT final forensic conclusion. (2) **Preliminary determination procedure (for KISA clock):** The bank's incident-response team: (a) detects an incident via SOC alerts, intrusion-detection systems, or user reports; (b) performs triage within 30 minutes (is this incident credible? does it affect >1,000 customers?); (c) if yes, notifies KISA within 2 hours of triage completion with: incident type, affected customer count (preliminary estimate), incident start time (estimated), incident discovery time, and the remediation steps initiated. The chain-of-custody evidence is NOT available at this point (the daily seal hasn't been verified). (d) the bank clearly states in the KISA notification: 'Preliminary impact assessment based on logs and SOC alerts; full forensic analysis and chain-of-custody evidence to follow within 24 hours.' (3) **Chain evidence timing (secondary):** 24 hours after the incident is discovered, the daily seal-verification job runs. If the seal indicates tampering (root mismatch), this CONFIRMS the compromise was real and the preliminary KISA notification is validated. The bank then files a follow-up to KISA with the final evidence: 'Daily chain-of-custody seal verification (performed at [time]) confirms the preliminary assessment. Here is the chain-of-custody evidence proving [specific events] were not tampered with.' (4) **If seal passes (no tampering detected):** The bank's follow-up to KISA clarifies: 'The 24-hour chain-of-custody verification found NO evidence of ledger tampering. This does NOT prove the incident did not occur; it proves that IF the incident occurred, it did not involve tampering with our audit chain. We continue forensic investigation of the incident via network logs, endpoint logs, and third-party forensics.' (5) **Control integration (K-ISMS-P control 13 — 사건대응):** The incident-response playbook MUST explicitly separate 'preliminary determination for KISA clock' from 'final determination after chain verification.' The playbook names responsible parties (IR lead, CISO, SOC manager) and escalation paths. The chain is supporting evidence, not the primary detection mechanism. (6) **Examiner expectations (EFSR §17 §5 incident response):** During an examination, if the examiner reviews an incident response file and sees a 2-hour KISA notification, the bank cites this procedure. The examiner cross-checks: was KISA notified within 2 hours? Did the bank use preliminary logs to make the determination (acceptable per KISA guidance)? Did the bank follow up after chain verification? If yes to all, the bank is compliant."

### Q-4. K-ISMS-P control mapping — audit trail completeness for controls 3.2 and 9

### Q-5. Ledger-server operational resilience — EFSR §17(1) 24/7 SOC and redundancy requirements

EFSR §17(1) mandates 24/7 SOC operation. The SOC must have redundant infrastructure, failover capabilities, and incident-response procedures that are operational 365 days per year, including holidays and natural disasters. The spec's ledger server (§6 deployment topologies) does NOT specify high-availability requirements, disaster-recovery procedures, or failover behavior. If the ledger server goes offline (power failure, network outage, data-center collapse), what happens to the chain? Can the SDK buffer events locally and replay them when the ledger recovers? (b) **Active-active (RPO ~1 minute, requires careful design):** Two ledger servers in different facilities. (4) **Key-rotation during outage:** If a key rotation is scheduled and the ledger is offline, the IKM provisioning system holds the new key in escrow; the rotation waits until the ledger is online. The rotation clock is paused, not skipped. Once the ledger recovers, the rotation proceeds within 2 hours (documented in the KMS provisioning SLA). (5) **Verification during outage:** The standalone verifier (§7) reads the ledger from an offline export (copy, USB drive, secure file transfer). The verifier does NOT require the ledger server to be online; it performs all verification on a static copy. This ensures examiners can verify the chain even if the bank's infrastructure is down (e.g., during incident response). (6) **EFSR §17(1) audit checklist:** During an examination, the FSS examiner will ask: 'If your primary ledger facility loses power for 4 hours, what is the impact on your SOC's ability to monitor the AI chain?' The bank should answer: (a) 'The SDK buffers events locally; no events are lost.' (b) 'The daily seal job is delayed, and the seal covers the accumulated events once the ledger recovers.' (c) 'Our backup facility is offline but prepared for promotion within 30 minutes.' (d) 'The SOC continues real-time monitoring via the mechanisms defined in Q-1 (host-based detection, log anomaly, ledger-storage monitoring); the chain is one component of the real-time posture, not the primary detection mechanism.' If the bank cannot answer all four points, the examiner may cite a control weakness related to 24/7 resilience. (7) **Spec requirement:** Add to §6.1 (High-availability options) or §6 (Deployment topologies) explicit guidance on primary-backup failover, active-active coordination (seal time, key version synchronization), and SDK buffer policy during outages."

### Q-6. Nation-state threat model alignment — Lazarus Group and APT37 attack scenarios

Korean financial cybersecurity assumes nation-state adversaries (북한 라자루스 그룹 / Lazarus Group for financial theft, APT37 / Reaper for espionage and disinformation) will compromise the bank within 18 months. The threat model is not theoretical; it is operational baseline. Lazarus Group has demonstrated capability to: (1) compromise banking infrastructure for months with no detection (2014 SWIFT hack, 2016 Bangladesh Bank heist simulation), (2) forge transaction records and authentication logs simultaneously, (3) exploit supply-chain vulnerabilities in SWIFT software and ATM networks. The spec's threat model (09-threat-model.md) describes adversaries A–I but does NOT explicitly name the nation-state threat model. The spec's response to Adversary D (HSM physical compromise) is "accept as residual risk"; against a nation-state, this is not sufficient — the bank must assume the HSM firmware can be compromised via supply-chain attack, not just physical access. **Ask:** Add to threat-model.md §2.9 or a new section "Nation-state threat model (aligned with K-ISMS-P and EFSR §17 baseline)": "(1) **Korean threat baseline:** Korean tier-1 banks operate under the assumption of nation-state adversary compromise (Lazarus Group, APT37, or equivalent) within 18 months. The bank is not defending against theoretical intrusions; it is defending against ACTIVE, PERSISTENT, WELL-RESOURCED ADVERSARIES with stated intent to: (a) steal customer funds via forged payment authorizations (Lazarus Group goal), (b) exfiltrate financial and government data (APT37 goal), (c) disrupt banking operations via ransomware or destructive malware (potential escalation scenario). (2) **Adversary capabilities under K-ISMS-P/EFSR §17 baseline:** The nation-state adversary controls: (a) supply-chain access to HSM firmware vendors, OTLP library maintainers, or SDK build pipelines (Lazarus Group has demonstrated this capability against SWIFT, ATM manufacturers), (b) network position (ability to intercept or inject OTLP traffic on the bank's internal network), (c) process-level compromise (rootkit implant on the ledger server, SDK host, or HSM appliance), (d) long persistence window (18+ months before detection). The adversary does NOT necessarily have physical HSM access, but DOES have supply-chain access to firmware or hardware design. (3) **Existing spec defenses against nation-state threat:** The spec's architecture provides: (a) **Append-only ledger:** Prevents retroactive rewriting of event history without HSM signature (defense against Adversary B). (b) **Daily Merkle seal + HSM signature:** Prevents forging new seals without the signing key (defense against Adversary C, limited by assumption that HSM signing key is not exfiltrated). (c) **Per-event HMAC chain:** Prevents forging events going forward without the session key (defense against Adversary A, limited if session key is exfiltrated). (d) **Verifier supply-chain discipline:** Prevents substituting a false verifier that passes forged chains (defense against Adversary H, limited if cosign keys are compromised). (4) **Residual risk gaps specific to nation-state threat:** (a) **HSM firmware-supply-chain compromise:** If the HSM firmware is modified by a supply-chain adversary BEFORE the bank receives the hardware, the 'secure signing key' assumption collapses — the HSM firmware can sign arbitrary payloads on demand or leak the key. The spec accepts this as residual risk (threat-model.md R3 DFA / fault-injection). Under the nation-state threat model, this is NOT a residual risk; it is an EXPECTED ATTACK VECTOR. (b) **Vendor-code compromise:** The Herald.Compliance SDK or ledger-server code can be poisoned at build time or during dependency fetches (npm, PyPI, Maven Central). The spec's defenses (open source, reproducible builds, cosign signatures) require the bank to execute build verification and supply-chain monitoring as ongoing operational tasks, not one-time setup. Under the nation-state threat model, the bank MUST assume these checks are defeated and develop compensating detection mechanisms (e.g., behavioral analysis of the ledger server, traffic analysis of OTLP, cryptographic verification of key operations). (c) **OTLP network-position compromise:** A nation-state actor on the bank's internal network can inject, replay, or modify OTLP events before the ledger receiver re-verifies them. The spec's TLS 1.3 encryption mitigates passive eavesdropping but not active injection (the attacker is inside the network boundary). mTLS with client-certificate pinning mitigates this further, but the PIN itself can be compromised if the attacker has access to the client process memory. The bank MUST implement additional network segmentation (VLAN isolation, microsegmentation) and network-based IDS (intrusion detection) for OTLP traffic. (5) **K-ISMS-P and EFSR §17 expectations:** The bank's control descriptions (K-ISMS-P controls 2.10, 3.2, 9) MUST assume nation-state compromise and name compensating controls: (a) **Supply-chain assurance (control 2.10 access, 3.2 crypto):** The bank performs or contracts for annual code reviews of Herald.Compliance source code, verifies build reproducibility quarterly, and maintains a verifier binary held in secure escrow (separate from production, air-gapped). (b) **Firmware attestation (control 3.2):** The bank works with the HSM vendor to implement firmware-integrity attestation checks; the SDK or ledger verifies HSM firmware version monthly and alerts if the version changes unexpectedly. (c) **Network detection (control 9.2 monitoring):** The bank's SIEM and IDS are configured to detect anomalous OTLP traffic patterns, TLS handshake anomalies (unexpected certificate chains), and ledger-server process anomalies (unexpected spawning of signing operations, unusual CPU/memory consumption). (6) **Incident-response procedure for supply-chain compromise (Scenario 16):** If the bank discovers that Herald.Compliance source code or binaries have been compromised by a nation-state actor: (a) immediately isolate the affected systems (ledger server, SDK hosts), (b) execute the HSM key-rotation procedure (Scenario 4) and declare all events signed under the potentially-compromised key as 'forensically questionable,' (c) notify the KISA, FSS, and FSI (금융보안원) within 2 hours, (d) engage a third-party incident-response firm and external forensics lab, (e) coordinate with Herald.Compliance vendor and upstream maintainers on remediation and patched release, (f) re-verify the verifier binary's integrity from source before using it again, (g) maintain chain-of-custody evidence for the entire investigation for litigation and regulatory review. (7) **Spec requirement:** Amend threat-model.md to explicitly name the nation-state threat model as the baseline for Korean financial institutions, articulate how the spec's existing defenses apply, name the residual gaps (HSM firmware, vendor code, OTLP network position), and provide pointers to compensating controls the institution MUST implement. Add a new Scenario to incident-response-playbook.md (Scenario 16: 'Supply-chain or firmware compromise by nation-state actor') with the triage, containment, recovery, and notification procedures outlined in #6."

### Q-9. Supply-chain compromise detection and verifier binary escrow

The spec's verifier (§7, 07-verifier-design.md) is the integrity anchor for examiner-side verification. The verifier is released as a statically-linked binary, signed by cosign, with SHA-256 hashes published alongside GPG-signed manifests. But a nation-state actor can compromise the verifier at build time or substituting a malicious binary into the release pipeline. A Korean bank's CISO is accountable for verifying the verifier before using it; the examiner may ask "how do you know your verifier is not compromised?" The spec provides reproducible-build guidance but does NOT require the bank to escrow an independently-built verifier in a secure location (air-gapped, HSM-held, or vault-locked). The bank should maintain a "golden copy" of the verifier binary that is independent from the vendor-supplied release, rebuilt by the bank's own security team and compared byte-for-byte against the published binary. **Ask:** Add to spec §7 (Verifier design) or supply-chain.md: "**Verifier binary supply-chain discipline and escrow procedure:** (1) **Verifier source code review:** Before each new verifier release, the bank's security team (ideally a third-party security firm or the FFIEC working group) performs a code review comparing the previous release to the current release. The review scope: (a) algorithmic changes (HMAC, Merkle, Ed25519 verification logic), (b) dependency updates (which upstream libraries were updated?), (c) new external APIs or network calls (MUST be zero for the verifier). The review report is filed and retained for 3 years. (2) **Verifier reproducible build:** The bank's security team downloads the verifier source code (GitHub release tag), executes `build.sh --release --verifier` (or equivalent per spec), and compiles locally. The resulting binary's SHA-256 is compared against the published binary's SHA-256. (a) **Match:** The binary is unmodified from source. Proceed to #3. (b) **Mismatch:** The published binary does not match the source code. This is a supply-chain compromise event. Stop immediately, escalate to the CISO and the FSS, do not use the binary in production or examination, engage Herald.Compliance vendor and upstream maintainers. (3) **Verifier escrow:** A copy of the locally-built verifier (verified to match source) is stored in an air-gapped, secure location: (a) **Physical escrow:** CD-ROM or USB drive, sealed envelope, stored in the bank's vault with access controls (requires CISO + Audit chair approval to remove). (b) **Cryptographic escrow:** The verifier binary and its SHA-256 hash are signed by the bank's CISO (Ed25519 key held in the bank's own HSM, separate from the chain's signing HSM). The signature is archived. (c) **Digital escrow:** A copy of the binary is stored in a separate air-gapped computer (not connected to the internet, not connected to the bank's main network, powered off except during examination use). This computer is held by the bank's internal audit department. (4) **Examination use:** When the FSS examiner arrives for examination, the examiner and the bank's team jointly: (a) retrieve the escrowed verifier binary from vault or air-gapped storage, (b) verify the binary's SHA-256 against the CISO's signed manifest, (c) verify the SHA-256 matches the published release SHA-256 (the examiner may bring a pre-computed list of trusted SHA-256 values for recent releases), (d) run the verifier on the ledger export. This procedure ensures the examiner is using a verifier that the bank and the examiner jointly trust, not a binary that could have been replaced between release and examination. (5) **Institutional procedure documentation:** The bank documents the verifier-escrow procedure in its operational-security policy (정보보호관리방침 > 공급자 관리 / vendor management section, K-ISMS-P control 2.10). The procedure is reviewed by internal audit annually and tested during table-top exercises (mock examinations). (6) **Spec requirement:** Add to §7 (Verifier design) or supply-chain.md: 'Institutions SHOULD implement verifier-supply-chain discipline per this section: code review, reproducible build, escrow, and examination-time joint verification. For tier-1 banks processing high-value decisions or under nation-state threat assumptions, verifier escrow is RECOMMENDED; for smaller institutions, escrow is optional but code review and reproducible build are mandatory.'"

### Q-10. OTLP transport-layer security — TLS 1.3 enforcement and certificate pinning

The spec §5.1 mandates TLS 1.3 for OTLP encryption. But TLS version negotiation can be downgraded by a network attacker unless the endpoint explicitly rejects TLS 1.2. The spec does NOT prohibit TLS 1.2; it only says TLS 1.3 is the minimum. In Korean banking, 망분리 separates the internal network from the internet; the OTLP traffic travels over internal networks where the threat model includes insider attacks (DBA with network access) or a nation-state actor inside the bank's network. Simple TLS is not sufficient; the bank should implement certificate pinning (HPKP or pin-on-first-use) and mTLS with client-certificate verification. **Ask:** Add to spec §5.1 (OTLP wire security) or a new operational guide `15-otlp-transport-hardening.md`: "**OTLP transport-layer hardening for Korean 망분리 environment:** (1) **TLS version enforcement:** (a) The OTLP receiver endpoint MUST accept ONLY TLS 1.3. TLS 1.2 is deprecated as of [spec version]. Clients attempting TLS 1.2 are rejected with a clear error message: 'TLS 1.2 is not supported; upgrade your client to TLS 1.3.' (b) For institutions with legacy SDK clients that cannot upgrade to TLS 1.3, a documented institutional exception is required (signed by CISO + CTO), approved by the FSS, and with a sunset date. The exception MUST NOT extend beyond [2028-01-01]. (c) The ledger-server configuration explicitly disables TLS 1.0, 1.1, and 1.2 via cipher-suite filtering: only TLS 1.3 cipher suites are enabled (TLS_CHACHA20_POLY1305_SHA256, TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384, etc.). (2) **Certificate pinning (RECOMMENDED for tier-1 banks):** (a) The SDK and the ledger are deployed with certificate-pinning configuration that maps ledger FQDN to the server's certificate public key (SPKI hash per RFC 7469). At TLS handshake time, the client verifies that the server's certificate public key matches the pinned value. (b) A man-in-the-middle attack with a valid-but-different certificate (signed by a CA the client trusts) is rejected because the public key does not match the pin. (c) **Pin management:** The ledger certificate is rotated annually or on demand (key compromise). Each time the certificate is rotated, the new public key is pinned in the SDK configuration. The SDK version is bumped and released; users must upgrade to the new version to avoid pin-mismatch rejections. (d) **Backup pins:** The configuration includes a backup public key (the next certificate to be deployed). If the primary certificate is compromised, the bank can immediately promote the backup certificate without waiting for all users to upgrade SDKs. (3) **Mutual TLS (mTLS) with client-certificate verification:** (a) The OTLP receiver endpoint requires mTLS: every client MUST present a certificate signed by a certificate authority (CA) that the ledger trusts. (b) **Client certificate issuance:** Each tenant (institution) receives a unique client certificate bound to its tenant_id. The certificate's subject CN or SAN includes the tenant_id and the institution's name. (c) **Client certificate validation:** The ledger verifies: (i) the certificate is signed by a trusted CA, (ii) the certificate is not expired, (iii) the certificate's tenant_id (from CN or SAN) matches the request's Resource attribute `ffiec.chain.tenant_id`. A mismatch triggers a rejection: 'Client certificate tenant_id does not match request tenant_id.' (d) **Certificate rotation:** Client certificates are rotated annually or on compromise. The SDK is provisioned with the new certificate; if a certificate expires, the SDK cannot authenticate and the bank is alerted. (4) **OTLP over HTTP/2 with strict encryption:** If the institution uses OTLP/HTTP (not gRPC), the server MUST enforce HTTP/2 and MUST NOT fall back to HTTP/1.1. This prevents downgrade attacks where an attacker forces the connection to HTTP/1.1 over a cleartext channel. (5) **Network segmentation and firewall rules:** The OTLP ingress point (ledger-server IP address and port) is firewalled to accept connections ONLY from the SDK sources. The firewall rule explicitly lists: (a) SDK hosts' IP addresses or CIDR blocks, (b) approved VPCs or subnets, (c) rejected-traffic logging (each rejected connection triggers a log entry; >10 rejected connections in 5 minutes triggers a SIEM alert). (6) **SIEM monitoring for TLS anomalies:** The institution's SIEM is configured to detect and alert on: (a) TLS 1.2 or lower connection attempts (should be zero after enforcement), (b) certificate validation failures (invalid signature, expired, CN mismatch), (c) mTLS client-certificate rejections (client-cert invalid or tenant_id mismatch), (d) cipher-suite downgrades (client negotiating a weak cipher). Each alert is triaged; >5 alerts in 1 hour triggers manual investigation. (7) **K-ISMS-P control alignment:** Control 3.2 (암호화 / cryptography) requires encryption of data in transit. Control 9.2 (감시 / monitoring) requires real-time alerting on transport-layer anomalies. The OTLP transport hardening procedure and SIEM monitoring rules are documented in the control descriptions. (8) **Spec requirement:** Update §5.1 to explicitly require TLS 1.3 (MUST), recommend certificate pinning and mTLS (SHOULD for tier-1, MAY for others), and provide operational guidance on firewall rules and SIEM configuration. Add to the compliance-ingest-contract.md (Herald.Py operational docs) the exact mTLS handshake flow and error codes for certificate validation failures."

### Q-11. Quarterly personal-data-flow documentation (K-ISMS-P control 2.10 and data governance)

The chain may carry personal data (customer names, account numbers, transaction amounts, decision inputs that reference customers) in its audit events. The bank's data-governance team must document the chain as a personal-data processor, the purpose (audit trail for AI decisions), the retention period (7 years per control 9.3), the access controls (who can read the chain), and the data-flow diagram (how events flow from SDK to ledger to verifier). This is not cryptographic; it is administrative, but it is a mandatory quarterly compliance task. **Ask:** Add to spec §10 (Operational procedures) or a new governance document `16-personal-data-governance.md`: "**Quarterly personal-data-flow documentation (K-ISMS-P control 2.10):** (1) **Data governance framework:** The bank's data-governance team maintains a master personal-data inventory (개인정보 처리 현황) using a designated tool (e.g., CASB, DLP platform, or spreadsheet audited by internal audit). The bank provides the PNDA with all flows labeled. - The auditor samples an access-control entry: 'The inventory says the ledger-server ops team has read/write access. ### Q-12. Red-team exercise readiness (EFSR §17(8) biennial testing)

EFSR §17(8) mandates that tier-1 banks conduct red-team exercises (침투 테스트 / penetration testing) every 2 years. These exercises are coordinated with the FSS and (for some banks) the National Intelligence Service (국정원) if classified or critical infrastructure is involved. The red team simulates an attacker and attempts to compromise the bank's infrastructure; the bank's defenders (SOC, IR team) detect and respond. The chain-of-custody system is a potential target: can the red team compromise the chain and forge evidence? The spec does NOT address how the chain integrates into red-team exercises or what the expected outcomes are. The bank's CISO must be prepared to answer: "If the red team compromises the SDK, the OTLP collector, and the HSM firmware simultaneously, what is your response?" **Ask:** Add to incident-response-playbook.md a new section "Red-team exercise readiness and chain-of-custody validation (EFSR §17(8))": "(1) **Biennial red-team scope:** Every 2 years, the bank conducts a red-team exercise in coordination with the FSS. The scope includes: (a) external network attack surface (DMZ, VPNs, public websites), (b) internal network (after red team achieves initial access), (c) critical systems (payment processing, core banking, customer databases, and OPTIONALLY the AI chain-of-custody system if the bank includes it in scope). (2) **Chain-of-custody exercise scenarios:** If the chain is included in red-team scope, the red-team targets include: **(a) SDK compromise:** Red team gains code execution on the AI agent's host. Objective: forge a chain event (e.g., a fake decision that approves a loan that should have been denied). Red team attempts to: - Read the process memory to extract the session key. - Inject a fake event into the local SQLite buffer. - Modify past events in the buffer. (b) OTLP tampering:** Red team gains network position between SDK and ledger. Objective: inject, modify, or replay OTLP events. Red team attempts to: - Perform a TLS downgrade (force TLS 1.2 if the endpoint accepts it). - Inject forged OTLP messages. - Replay past messages. (c) Ledger-server compromise:** Red team gains code execution on the ledger server. Objective: rewrite past events and forge new seals. Red team attempts to: - Modify events in append-only storage (database UPDATE statements). - Recompute the Merkle root to match the modified events. - Extract the HSM signing key (physical or via software vulnerability). (d) HSM firmware compromise:** Red team simulates HSM firmware compromise (not actual physical attack, but simulation via software). Objective: sign arbitrary payloads under the bank's key. Red team attempts to: - Query the HSM to sign a forged root hash that covers a rewritten event history. (3) **Expected outcome per spec design:** If the red team successfully executes any of the above attacks individually: - **SDK compromise (3a):** Red team can forge events going forward (bounded to the compromise window). Past events are already sealed and cannot be retroactively altered. Detection: verifier detects the forged events when re-verifying (HMAC chain mismatch). Red team fails. - **OTLP tampering (3b):** Red team forges OTLP messages. Detection: ledger receiver re-verifies HMAC chain on ingest (per spec §7 step 5–6). Forged messages are rejected. Red team fails. - **Ledger-server compromise (3c):** Red team rewrites events and recomputes the Merkle root to match the modified events. Detection: next daily seal-verification (step 10, §7) recomputes the root from the modified events and discovers it does NOT match the signed root (the old seal was computed from the original events). Tampering is detected. Red team fails. - **HSM firmware compromise (3d):** Simulated scenario. The red team asks the HSM to sign a forged root. Assumption: the HSM firmware can be compromised. Outcome per spec: this is accepted as residual risk (threat-model.md R3). Real-time detection: the bank's compensating control (firmware attestation, if available) detects firmware version mismatch. OR if firmware attestation is unavailable, the bank must rely on post-seal detection or additional network monitoring to detect the compromise. Red team succeeds at this scenario (firmware compromise is residual risk); the bank's IR team is expected to detect the compromise via other means (network anomalies, unauthorized SSH sessions, physical security alerts). (4) **Red-team simultaneous-compromise scenario:** Red team attempts all four attacks simultaneously: SDK + OTLP + ledger + HSM. Can they forge a history that appears valid to the verifier? Expected outcome per spec: only if they compromise all three trust boundaries (application, ledger, HSM). If any one component is defended: - Verifier detects the forgery. OR - Real-time detection (SOC, IDS, firewall logs) alerts to the compromise during the exercise, and IR team isolates systems. (5) **Exercise evidence and documentation:** (a) **Pre-exercise:** The bank documents the chain-of-custody exercise objectives, red-team scope, detection mechanisms to be evaluated, and success criteria. (b) **Exercise execution:** The red team attempts the scenarios. The bank's SOC/IR team monitors and responds. Events are logged: attack attempts, detection triggers, IR response actions, time-to-detection. (c) **Post-exercise report:** The red team documents: (i) which attacks succeeded and why (e.g., 'SDK compromise succeeded because we extracted the session key from memory'), (ii) which attacks failed and why (e.g., 'ledger modification was detected by the next daily seal'), (iii) time-to-detection for each scenario (how long before the bank's SOC detected the attack?), (iv) remediation recommendations. (d) **CISO follow-up:** The CISO reviews the red-team report and maps findings to control improvements: (i) if SDK compromise detection was slow, strengthen process memory protection (mlock, secure enclaves). (ii) if OTLP tampering succeeded due to TLS downgrade, enforce TLS 1.3 and remove TLS 1.2 support. (iii) if ledger modification detection latency was too high (24 hours), consider adopting hourly seals or adding real-time ledger-storage integrity monitoring. (6) **EFSR §17(8) examination checkpoint:** During an FSS examination, the examiner will ask: 'Did your red team exercise include the chain-of-custody system? Show me the findings.' The bank provides the red-team report and the CISO's remediation plan. If the bank has NOT included the chain in red-team scope, the examiner may request that the bank commit to including it in the next biennial exercise. If the red team found gaps (e.g., SDK compromise detection took 4 hours), the examiner will ask: 'What actions are you taking to improve detection speed?' The bank's response (e.g., 'implementing process memory protection via mlock, reducing detection latency to <30 minutes') is expected. (7) **Spec requirement:** Add to incident-response-playbook.md or a new operational-readiness guide `17-red-team-exercise-chain.md`: document the four exercise scenarios, the expected outcomes per spec design, the success criteria (what constitutes passing the exercise?), and the post-exercise documentation requirements. Include a checklist for the CISO: 'Has the chain been included in our biennial red-team exercise? If not, commit to including it in the next exercise [date].' This ensures the CISO is prepared for examiner questions and the bank maintains continuous improvement of chain-of-custody resilience."

## Closing assessment

**Partial? Yes.**

The FFIEC chain-of-custody v1.0 spec is cryptographically sound and operationally defensible for US banks under FFIEC oversight. For Korean Tier-1 banks operating under K-ISMS-P (mandatory 3-year recertification with annual surveillance audits), Electronic Financial Supervisory Regulation §17 (EFSR §17 — 24/7 SOC, dual-approval, red-team exercises), and the mandatory 망분리 (network separation) architecture, **the spec has four load-bearing operational and regulatory-integration gaps that prevent deployment without institutional compensating controls and spec amendments.** The gaps are not cryptographic (the primitives are sound); they are operational (real-time monitoring integration), architectural (네트워크 분리 deployment topology), and regulatory (K-ISMS-P control mapping, KISA incident-notification clock).

**Confidence under K-ISMS-P recertification + EFSR §17 inspection.** A Korean Tier-1 bank can deploy the chain with the compensating controls outlined in Q-1 through Q-14. However, the deployment will require: (1) institutional choice to self-host the ledger server on-premises (no vendor-hosted multi-tenant US/EU cloud), (2) integration of daily seal-verification results into the 24/7 SOC's real-time alerting system, (3) pre-positioning the chain-of-custody playbook in incident-response procedures so KISA notifications are issued within 2 hours with chain evidence following within 24 hours, (4) comprehensive documentation of all seal operations, key rotations, and access logs to satisfy K-ISMS-P auditors, and (5) inclusion of the chain in the biennial red-team exercise (EFSR §17(8)). Without these compensating controls, the chain becomes a liability: it creates a single point of integrity (HSM signature) and a high-value evidence target for a nation-state APT (Lazarus Group, APT37) operating under the assumed 18-month compromise timeline.

With compensating controls, the chain significantly strengthens the bank's cyber-resilience posture under K-ISMS-P, EFSR §17, and the nation-state threat model. The difference between "strengthens resilience" and "becomes a liability" is institutional discipline plus spec clarification.

**Recommended next steps.**

(1) **Spec amendments for Korean financial-sector compliance:** Add or expand the following to the FFIEC spec: (a) A section on real-time monitoring integration (Q-1) clarifying that the chain is a long-tail audit archive and must be supplemented with real-time SOC detection mechanisms (SIEM, IDS, log anomaly detection). (b) A section on Korean network-separation (망분리) deployment topology (Q-2) naming compliant topologies (on-premises ledger, Korean jurisdiction), prohibited topologies (vendor-hosted in US/EU cloud for customer-identified data), and FSS approval procedures. (c) A K-ISMS-P control-mapping matrix (Q-4) cross-referencing the spec's components to K-ISMS-P controls 3.2 (cryptography), 9.1 (logging), 9.2 (monitoring), and 9.3 (log retention). (d) Expanded incident-response playbook (Q-3, Q-13) with Korean-specific procedures: preliminary determination clock, KISA notification templates, FSS notification procedures, FSI coordination, and master-key recovery ceremony (Q-8).
