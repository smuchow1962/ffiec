## Headline

The spec gives an examiner strong technical foundations for testing chain-of-custody controls: the verifier procedure (§7) is precise and testable, the audit procedures (P-1 through P-10) align with FFIEC control-testing methodology, the incident-response playbook (36-hour notification clock) integrates with supervisory reporting, and the test-vector corpus demonstrates attack resistance comprehensively. **During a 4-week on-site engagement, Sophia would walk away confident the institution's AI-decision audit trail is cryptographically sound, operationally controlled, and externally verifiable.**

**What is airtight.** The work-paper trail: requesting the institution's verifier output (a PASS/FAIL report with failure-reason granularity), requesting the daily seal records with the HSM signature dates, requesting the IKM rotation log, and spot-checking 20–30 chain entries byte-for-byte against the verifier procedure replicates the examination cadence well. The incident-response playbook's 36-hour clock aligns with the Computer-Security Incident Notification Rule (12 CFR §53 for Federal Reserve member banks), so the institution's IR team and the examination team use the same clock. The three-lines-of-defense alignment is implicit but testable: the institution's AI governance committee (second line) oversees the chain's operation; the IT operations team (first line) runs the seal job and HSM custody; internal audit or SOC (third line) reconciles and tests sample entries. The spec does NOT require this explicitly, but it's how tier-1 and regional banks already organize these controls, so the gap is one of documentation, not design.

**What would need supplemental documentation.** Sophia would NOT issue an MRA on spec gaps; she would issue an MRA on institution-side **control-description gaps**. The spec allows parameterizable posture (FFIEC-conformant vs vendor-namespaced constants per §4.1.2); the examiner's first question during pre-exam scoping is "which posture are you running, and what is your change-management procedure if you ever flip it?" The institution must have a CC8.1 control description (per FFIEC Booklet 12 — Operations, the control objectives catalog) naming: (1) posture in force, (2) configuration mechanism, (3) change-management discipline. Without it, the examiner has no work-paper baseline to test against and cannot assess whether the institution is operating the control as designed. This is not a spec defect — the spec names the posture choice and says "the institution documents it" — but it is a gap in the examiner's guidance for what to ask for and what work-paper standard applies.

**What's missing for the examination path.** The spec defines the control (chain-of-custody); the audit procedures (P-1 through P-10) define how the SOC team or internal audit verifies it. But the FFIEC examination path — what the examiner asks for on day one, which procedures execute in the 4-week window, what gets cited if the control fails — is implicit in the spec and audit procedures, not explicit. The regulator pack addresses this partially, but does not cover: (1) **Pre-exam scoping questions** the examiner asks the institution's IT risk officer 60 days before the exam (e.g., "Show me the change-management record for HSM rotation during the period; show me any Scenario 9 truncation events and the institution's remediation"). (2) **Evidence-sufficiency standards** — when is one year of verifier output PASS records sufficient, and when does the examiner demand stratified sampling across the 7-year retention window? (3) **Control-failure MRA thresholds** — at what percentage failure rate does a sampling defect rise to an MRA? If 50 of 1000 spot-checked chain entries fail the verifier, is that a control-design defect (MRA) or an operational incident (documented in the exam findings but not cited as formal MRA)? The spec's silence on these examiner-consumption questions is deliberate and appropriate — the spec defines the control, not the examination protocol — but the gap means each examiner will set thresholds independently, creating inconsistency across institutions.

**Partial verdict.** Not a Partial. The spec is examination-ready. The gaps are in the institution-side control-description requirements and the examiner-training materials, not in the spec itself. Sophia would recommend that the FFIEC working group produce a short **Examiner Guidance for AI Decision Logging** addendum to this spec (one to two pages, binding to the existing FFIEC Booklet 12 examination standards and the 2023 TPRM guidance) that names the control-test procedures, the pre-exam scoping questions, the deficiency-severity thresholds, and the integration with the Computer-Security Incident Notification Rule.

### Q-2. URSIT rating methodology — how does the chain affect the IT risk rating?

The Uniform Rating System for Information Technology (URSIT) rates each IT function on a 1–5 scale based on risk. The institution's audit trail is part of the Operations function's rating. The chain is either (a) a compensating control that **improves** the Operations rating if it was previously deficient, or (b) evidence of **ongoing effectiveness** if the Operations function was already well-controlled. During the examination, the EIC assesses: Does this chain materially reduce the likelihood of undetected AI-decision tampering (a risk the institution would otherwise face)? Does the chain operationally integrate with the institution's existing incident-response program? Does it produce evidence that supports the SOC team's control testing? If the answer to all three is yes, the chain likely warrants a URSIT rating upgrade for AI-relevant operations (e.g., from 3 = Satisfactory but with residual risk, to 2 = Strong, with the chain being the key control). The spec does not address URSIT mechanics. **Ask:** Add a short section to ai-policy-alignment.md or a new `ursit-rating-guidance.md`: "The chain of custody is evidence of operational control over AI decision logging. During URSIT assessment, the EIC evaluates whether the chain's presence and operating effectiveness warrant an upgrade to the Operations function rating from 3 (Satisfactory with issues) to 2 (Strong) or even 1 (Outstanding) for institutions with mature AI governance. The presence of the chain alone does not automatical change the rating; the integration with the institution's incident-response program, the SOC team's demonstrated testing procedures, and the absence of control-failure findings during the examination period are the basis for the upgrade."

### Q-3. Control failure severity matrix — when is a chain verifier failure an MRA vs an operational finding?

During the 4-week examination, the examiner runs the verifier over a sample of chain entries (typically 1–2% of the daily volume for a regional bank, or a stratified sample across business lines). Suppose 5 out of 200 sampled entries fail the verifier with "key_fingerprint mismatch at seq N." Is that an MRA (Matters Requiring Attention, requires a remediation plan within 90 days), or an operational finding (documented in the workpapers, monitored by the institution's SOC team, not formally cited)? The severity depends on context: (a) If the 5 failures trace to a known, documented incident (e.g., the IKM was rotated on day 45 and the institution failed to update the fingerprint registry for one business line), the examiner assesses it as a control-design deficiency (MRA). (b) If the 5 failures are isolated to a specific date or process, the examiner may assess it as an operational incident, confirmed via the institution's own incident-response log. (c) If the failure rate exceeds 1% of the sample, the examiner escalates to MRIA (Matters Requiring Immediate Attention) and may restrict the institution's further AI deployment pending remediation. The spec's audit procedures (P-1 through P-10) do not name these severity thresholds. **Ask:** Add to the examiner-scoping-checklist.md or to regulator-pack/examination-response-workflow.md a "Control failure severity matrix": (1) Verifier failure rate 0–0.1% of sample = operational finding, documented in workpapers, no MRA. (2) Verifier failure rate 0.1–1% of sample = control-design deficiency, requires MRA with 90-day remediation plan. (3) Verifier failure rate > 1% of sample or multiple failures on the same entry = MRIA, requires immediate action plan and restricts further AI deployment pending fix. (4) Structural failure (missing seal record, negative-test-vector match) = MRIA, immediate escalation to RFI and possible consent-order discussion. These thresholds let examiners across the FFIEC coordinate on severity and keep institutions from being surprised by an MRA finding that another institution's examiner would have documented as operational.

### Q-4. IKM custody handoff audit trail — what evidence proves the HSM key entered on day 1?

The chain-of-custody spec (§4.1.1) mandates IKM custody in an HSM or KMS under authentication (mTLS, SPIFFE, or equivalent) and confidentiality (TLS 1.3). The institution's HSM supplier (e.g., AWS CloudHSM, Azure Managed HSM, Thales Luna) maintains its own audit log of who loaded the IKM, when, and with what credentials. During the examination, Sophia would ask: "Show me the HSM audit log proving the IKM was generated and loaded on [date]. Show me the change management record of the institution's sign-off. Show me the evidence that the IKM has not been exported since loading (HSM audit log, change-control record, or HSM-side attestation that the key was generated and never extracted)." The spec names the property (IKM non-extractability, HSM custody) but does not specify what work-paper evidence the institution must produce to prove it. The regulator pack's examiner-approval-template.md touches on this but is not specific enough. **Ask:** Add a section to examiner-scoping-checklist.md: "IKM Custody Evidence — Request the institution produce: (1) HSM vendor audit log for the examination period showing key-load events and any extraction attempts (confirmation that the key has been held securely). (2) Institution change-management record of the date the IKM was loaded. (3) HSM attestation certificate (if the HSM vendor provides it) confirming the key was generated on-HSM and never extracted. (4) Access-control log showing which operators, with what credentials, have accessed the HSM for key operations. The examiner confirms the HSM access is restricted to the chain-custody team (CC8.1 segregation of duties) and that any key operations are documented per the institution's change-management process." This makes the audit trail for the control's foundation testable and consistent across institutions.

### Q-5. Incident-response playbook integration — when does a Scenario 4 master-key compromise trigger the 36-hour notification?

The spec's incident-response-playbook.md (Scenario 4: "Master-key compromise or suspected compromise") says "If the IKM is exposed, notification to the examiner within 36 hours per 12 CFR §53." But what triggers the 36-hour clock: (a) the moment the institution suspects the IKM might be exposed (best-case agility, worst-case noise if the suspicion is unfounded), (b) the moment the institution confirms the IKM is exposed (higher confidence, but longer delay), or (c) the moment the institution detects anomalous chain signatures inconsistent with a known compromise window (forensically certain but potentially much later)? The spec intends (a) — "suspected" suggests the precautionary principle — but examiners and institutions across the FFIEC system may interpret differently. During the examination, Sophia would ask the institution's IR coordinator: "Walk me through your decision process. How did you confirm this was a real compromise vs a false alarm?" The spec allows flexibility here (appropriately); the examination consistency requires the institution to document its decision criteria in advance. **Ask:** Extend incident-response-playbook.md Scenario 4 with a decision tree: "The 36-hour notification clock starts when the institution assesses a credible risk of IKM exposure. Credible risk includes: (a) monitoring alert indicating the IKM was accessed by an unauthorized process (Adversary F in the threat model), (b) HSM audit log showing an extraction attempt or unexpected use pattern, (c) anomalous chain signatures that would only be possible if the IKM were compromised. The institution's IR coordinator is authorized to notify the examiner immediately upon credible-risk assessment without waiting for full forensic confirmation; the 36-hour clock runs from notification, not from discovery of the root cause. The institution documents the decision criteria and the IR coordinator's escalation protocol in the security incident-response playbook, reviewed annually." This makes the decision uniform across institutions and removes ambiguity about what "suspected" means.

### Q-6. Seal-age monitoring and the 72-hour FFIEC clock — when does Sophia cite an MRA?

The spec's incident-response-playbook.md Scenario 3 ("Seal not published within the SLA") says "If the seal is delayed more than 72 hours, notify the regulator." The 72-hour window is longer than the spec's normative 60-minute SLA for publishing the root (§4.3). Why the gap? The 60-minute SLA is what the spec requires the institution to implement; the 72-hour window is the *examiner's* SLA for how long the examiner will wait to detect and escalate the issue before it becomes a supervisory concern. During a 4-week examination, Sophia would run P-8 (Seal-age monitoring): she pulls the daily seal records for the entire examination period and verifies that every seal has a signed_at timestamp within 60 minutes of the UTC day boundary. If seals on 3 of 90 days exceed 60 minutes (e.g., they were signed at 90 minutes or 120 minutes), the question is: Is this (a) an operational incident that the institution caught and resolved (no MRA), (b) a control-design gap where the HSM sign-off is not meeting the SLA (MRA), or (c) a systemic issue indicating the seal job is under-resourced or the HSM is operating at capacity limits (MRIA)? The spec does not distinguish these failure modes. **Ask:** Add to audit-procedures.md an extension of P-8: "Seal-age monitoring and SLA failure severity. (1) Sample: pull seal records for 5 random days per month in the examination period (60 seals for a 12-month period). (2) Verify: for each seal, confirm `signed_at` timestamp is within 60 minutes of UTC midnight (`seal_date` 00:00:00 + 60 min). (3) Failure disposition: (a) Zero failures = control operating effectively. (b) 1–2 seal ages exceeding 60 min = operational incident; cross-reference the institution's incident log for documentation of cause and remediation. No MRA unless the remediation was inadequate. (c) 3 or more seal-age failures = control-design deficiency; issue MRA requiring the institution to increase HSM capacity or rebalance the seal-job timing. (d) Seal-age failures correlating with a single date or process issue (e.g., the HSM was offline for patching on 2026-02-15, and two seals were delayed) = operational incident, confirmed via IT change-management log. No MRA if change-management documented the HSM downtime and the recovery time met expectations." This makes the severity determination mechanical and consistent.

### Q-7. Sample-selection methodology for transaction testing — does Sophia test 50 entries or 500?

The FFIEC examination standards (AT-C Section 105) require sample selection to be statistically valid or judgmental. For the chain, the population is the daily volume of entries (ranging from hundreds for a small bank to millions for a tier-1 institution). Sophia would use a stratified random sample: divide the examination period (e.g., 12 months) into quarters; for each quarter, select a random date; for that date, select 20–30 chain entries at random; run the verifier on those entries; count passes and failures. This gives her statistical confidence about the population. But the spec does not name a standard sample size or methodology. Different examiners may choose: (a) 30 entries per quarter (120 total for the year, ~0.00001% of a tier-1 institution's entries), (b) 1% of daily volume (1 million entries for a tier-1 institution, infeasible in a 4-week exam), or (c) adaptive sampling (start with 50, if failures emerge, expand to 500). The variation creates examination-consistency problems. **Ask:** Add to audit-procedures.md or create a new section: "Sample-selection methodology for chain verifier testing. (1) **Community bank (< $10B assets):** Stratified random sample of 50 entries per quarter (200 total per year). (2) **Regional bank ($10B–$250B assets):** Stratified random sample of 100 entries per quarter (400 total per year) plus additional entries from any incident-identified period. (3) **Tier-1 institution (> $250B assets):** Stratified random sample of 300 entries per quarter (1200 total per year) plus a focused sample of 50–100 entries from any incident-identified period. Sampling frame: divide the examination period into quarters; for each quarter, identify 3–5 random dates; for each date, select 25–50 entries at random; run the verifier on all sampled entries. Confidence target: sampling should have 90%+ confidence of detecting a 1% failure rate in the population. Document the sample design, the random-number seed, and the results in the examination workpapers." This standardizes the approach and ensures consistency across examiners and institutions.

### Q-8. Examination follow-up cycles — what does an MRA remediation look like?

Suppose Sophia issues an MRA: "The institution's IKM rotation procedure did not include re-derivation of the `key_fingerprint` in the public-key registry, resulting in 15 entries with fingerprint mismatches during the examination period. Corrective action required within 90 days." The institution then fixes the procedure, performs a manual re-verification of the entries with corrected fingerprints, and submits a remediation plan to Sophia. During the follow-up examination (18 months later), the next EIC (or Sophia returning) verifies the remediation: she samples entries from the month after the corrective action was implemented and confirms the fingerprint mismatch defect is no longer present. But the spec and audit procedures do not specify what the institution should do with the historical entries that had fingerprints mismatches — are they re-signed, re-hashed, left as historical artifacts? The verifier will still reject them (the fingerprints are still wrong), which is correct from an integrity perspective, but the examination follow-up needs to distinguish between "the entries are still wrong and the control is still broken" vs "the entries are historical artifacts now corrected by the updated procedure." **Ask:** Add to the incident-response-playbook or a new section "MRA remediation and historical-entry handling": "When corrective action addresses a chain-integrity defect (e.g., fingerprint mismatch, seal-age SLA breach, missing seal record), the institution may treat affected entries as historical artifacts only if the root cause was procedural, not cryptographic. Procedural: the fingerprint registry was not updated when the IKM was rotated; the institution updates the registry and documents the correction in its IR log. The historical entries remain in the ledger with the old fingerprints (verification still fails, correctly identifying the procedural error period), and the institution's incident response explains the defect and the remediation. Cryptographic: the HMAC computation was buggy; the verifier output cannot be trusted for historical entries. The institution engages the auditor and the examiner to determine whether the defect scope can be bounded, and the follow-up examination focuses on confirming the fix. The spec and the verifier do not require historical re-hashing; the examination team's assessment of root-cause scope determines the follow-up burden." This clarifies what the institution does after an MRA and what the examiner verifies in the follow-up.

### Q-9. Three-lines-of-defense alignment with the chain — implicit vs explicit?

The FFIEC framework (Booklet 12 — Operations; supplemented by OCC Bulletin 2013-29 on the three lines of defense) expects IT controls to have: (1) first-line responsibility: the IT operations team owns and executes the control (the seal job runs daily, the HSM is maintained, the key-fingerprint registry is refreshed), (2) second-line oversight: risk management or compliance (e.g., the MRM committee or an IT risk committee reviews the chain's operation monthly), and (3) third-line assurance: internal audit or SOC tests the control's operating effectiveness. The spec's audit procedures (P-1 through P-10) implicitly assume this structure: P-1 is a first-line procedure (the institution runs the verifier daily); P-5 and P-6 are second-line procedures (reconciliation and anomaly review); P-8 and P-9 are third-line procedures (seal-age monitoring and external verification). But the spec does not *require* the institution to explicitly document this alignment. During the examination, Sophia would ask the IT Risk Officer: "Who is responsible for the seal job? Who reviews the verifier output? Who reports anomalies to the audit committee?" The institution should have explicit owner-and-reviewer assignments; many institutions leave this implicit ("the operations team runs it; if something breaks, we'll escalate") and discover during the exam that the alignment is fuzzy. **Ask:** Add to the regulator-pack/ai-policy-alignment.md or to the control-map a section "Three-lines-of-defense alignment for the chain-of-custody control": "(1) **First line (IT Operations):** Owns the seal job, the HSM operations, the IKM rotation schedule, and the daily verifier run. Escalates any anomalies (verifier failures, seal delays, fingerprint mismatches) to the IT Risk Officer within one business day. (2) **Second line (IT Risk or MRM Committee):** Reviews the institution's chain-operation metrics monthly: seal-age trend, verifier failure rate, fingerprint reconciliation results. Assesses whether anomalies indicate a control-design issue or an operational incident. Escalates any suspected defects to the Chief Risk Officer and schedules remediation. (3) **Third line (Internal Audit or SOC):** Tests the control's operating effectiveness quarterly via sample verification (P-8 procedure). Issues an annual attestation to the audit committee confirming control effectiveness or naming any observations. The written control description (per CC8.1) explicitly names the owner and reviewer at each line." This makes the governance structure explicit and testable.
