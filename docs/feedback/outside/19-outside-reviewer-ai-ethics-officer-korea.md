## Headline

The FFIEC chain-of-custody spec provides a technically sound, integrity-bearing audit trail for AI decisions — **essential infrastructure for defending high-impact AI deployments under the AI Basic Act.** However, the spec was designed for US regulatory compliance (ECOA, FFIEC, potential discovery) and does not explicitly address **three critical Korean AI-ethics obligations: (1) the right to explanation (설명요구권) and the human-review process; (2) fairness and discrimination prevention, especially region-of-origin bias; and (3) social legitimacy (사회적 정당성) — the requirement that AI systems be trustworthy and transparent to the public and civil society, not just to regulators.** Seo-Yeon's read pass found **25 points where the AI Basic Act and Korean AI-ethics frameworks create expectations the spec does not articulate.**

**What the spec enables for Korean AI governance.** The per-entry integrity chain (HMAC + daily Merkle + HSM signature) means a bank can prove in an AI Governance Committee meeting or to PIPC enforcement staff: "this is what the AI decided, when it decided it, and on what basis — and it hasn't been tampered with." The `audit.*` namespace captures decision reasoning, enabling post-hoc fairness audits and discrimination detection. The customer-dispute-procedures.md section on GDPR Article 22 (right to human review) is adaptable to the AI Basic Act's human-review mandate. The privacy-by-design.md tokenization aligns with PIPC PIPA §37-2 automated-decision-making safeguards.

**What Korean governance needs that the spec doesn't clarify.** Explicit mapping to AI Basic Act Articles 15–20 (high-impact AI obligations); detailed procedures for the right to explanation (설명요구권) and how chain evidence supports it; fairness-audit procedures specifically addressing Korean-sensitive characteristics (age, region of origin, disability, marital status); integration with the bank's AI Governance Committee oversight (the AI Basic Act mandates such committees for large financial organizations); guidance on AI Impact Assessment (AI 영향평가) content and chain-derived evidence; PIPC enforcement-readiness procedures (PIPC examination is coming; what evidence does the bank provide?); social-legitimacy narrative (how to communicate the chain's existence to customers and civil-society stakeholders); AI Trustworthiness Verification integration (if the bank pursues voluntary certification); and fairness-labeling and discrimination-detection schemas specifically tuned to Korean protected characteristics.

Each question below is grounded in the AI Basic Act, MSIT AI Ethics Standards, or Korean social-legitimacy norms. The closing is an operational procedure, a spec addition, or guidance the bank must have before deploying high-impact AI under Korean regulation.

### Q-2. Right to explanation (설명요구권) — is the chain sufficient evidence for customer-facing explanation?

AI Basic Act Article 17 grants users the right to demand explanation of an AI decision. The bank must provide the explanation in plain language, in Korean, within a reasonable time. The chain captures what the AI decided and (in the `audit.*` namespace) why. But the chain does not guarantee the explanation is comprehensible to a customer. A customer asking "Why was my loan denied?" deserves an answer like "Your application scored below the threshold because your debt-to-income ratio was 45%, and our policy requires <40%." The chain entry might have `audit.fraud_score = 45, audit.fraud_threshold = 40`, but translating that to customer-language is the bank's job, and the chain alone doesn't ensure the translation is clear. **Ask:** Create `customer-dispute-procedures-korea.md` or extend existing procedures: "**Right to Explanation (설명요구권) Process Using Chain Evidence** (1) Customer submits explanation request: 'Explain why you denied my loan application.' (2) Bank's dispute team extracts the chain entry for the customer's decision. (3) Bank's AI explainability function (a separate, customer-service-facing tool) translates the chain entry's `audit.*` fields into plain-language Korean. Example: `audit.fraud_score=45, audit.fraud_threshold=40` becomes '귀하의 신용도 점수가 40% 기준을 초과하여 승인되지 않았습니다' (Your creditworthiness score exceeded the 40% threshold, resulting in non-approval). (4) Bank sends the explanation to the customer within [time frame, e.g., 10 business days]. (5) If the customer disputes the explanation or requests further detail, the bank escalates to the human-review process (per AI Basic Act Article 18). (6) The bank documents the customer's request and the bank's response in a customer-dispute log (part of the chain or chained separately) for audit and fairness-review purposes. The chain's evidence is necessary but not sufficient; the bank's explanation procedures are the critical bridge to the customer's right."

Preparation: The bank's Compliance team should assign a staff member to prepare a 'PIPC examination package' quarterly, collating governance docs, procedure updates, fairness-audit results, and incident logs. When PIPC schedules the exam, the package is ready to go."

### Q-10. Regional discrimination (출신지역 차별) detection and remediation

Region-of-origin discrimination (출신지역 차별) is a live concern in Korea. Provinces and regions outside Seoul face historical disadvantages; the concern is that AI models, trained on historical data, may perpetuate or amplify this bias. For example, a loan-decisioning AI might learn to approve Seoul applications at higher rates than provincial applications, purely because the historical training data had different demographics or credit-quality distributions by region. The bank's fairness audit must explicitly test for this. **Ask:** Add to `fairness-audit-korea.md`: "**Region-of-Origin Fairness Testing** As part of the quarterly or semi-annual fairness audit (Q-5), the bank explicitly tests for regional discrimination: (1) **Stratification by region**: The bank's data team uses the de-identification mapping to compute approval rate for Seoul vs each province (경기도, 인천, 강원도, 대전, 세종, 충청남도, 충청북도, 전주, 전라남도, 경상남도, 경상북도, 부산, 울산, 제주도). (2) **Statistical testing**: If Seoul's approval rate is 70% and a province's rate is 60%, is the difference statistically significant (not due to random variation)? Chi-square test or other statistical method confirms. (3) **Remediation if differential impact detected**: If a province has significantly lower approval rates, the bank's AI team investigates: (a) Data bias: Do applicants from region X have worse credit profiles (lower credit scores, higher debt-to-income ratios) than Seoul applicants? If yes, the bias is in the input data, not the model. The bank documents this in fairness-audit report. (b) Model bias: If input credit profiles are similar, but the model approves region X at lower rates, the model has learned to discriminate. The bank retrains the model with a fairness constraint (e.g., 'enforce equal approval rates across regions for otherwise-identical applicants') or adds human review for region X edge cases. (4) **Documentation and reporting**: The bank's fairness-audit report documents regional analysis and remediation. The bank reports to MSIT per AI Basic Act Article 20 if discrimination is detected. (5) **Social legitimacy**: If the bank detects and remediates regional bias, the bank can publicly communicate this: 'We detected regional bias in Q3; we retrained our AI with fairness constraints; we now monitor approval rates by region monthly to ensure no bias recurs.' This demonstrates social legitimacy."

### Q-11. Age-discrimination detection — Korean society's age sensitivity

The fundamental technical design is jurisdiction-neutral: a per-entry HMAC chain, a daily Merkle seal, and an HSM-backed Ed25519 signature satisfy the integrity requirements of every major regulatory framework I have examined (US FFIEC, EU GDPR, Korea AI Basic Act + PIPC). The differences are in governance framing (how the US names AI governance differs from how Korea names it), fairness priorities (Korean sensitivity to age and region-of-origin discrimination), and social expectations (Korean emphasis on 사회적 정당성, social legitimacy and public trust).

My recommendation to the bank's board and AI Governance Committee: Proceed with adoption, subject to closing the 25 questions via (1) institutional procedures documented in the AI Impact Assessment and AI Governance Committee charter, (2) fairness-audit procedures specifically addressing Korean protected characteristics, (3) customer-communication procedures aligned with the right to explanation (설명요구권) and social-legitimacy principles, (4) coordination with MSIT and PIPC on enforcement-readiness, and (5) optional engagement with MSIT on AI Trustworthiness Verification integration. The chain is a principled, ethics-forward, independent-verification-capable control that works equally well in Korean regulatory contexts once the framing is made explicit and the cultural/regulatory gaps are bridged.

The working group should consider elevating the Korea-specific guidance (AI Basic Act Articles 15–20 mapping, fairness-audit procedures for Korean protected characteristics, PIPC enforcement-readiness, social-legitimacy communication, AI Trustworthiness Verification alignment) to formal regulator-pack status so that all Korean deploying institutions benefit from consistent, reviewed guidance. This broadens the spec's applicability from US-only to truly multi-jurisdiction, and it positions the FFIEC chain as a global standard for trustworthy AI in banking — a standard that respects both regulatory compliance and cultural values across jurisdictions.
