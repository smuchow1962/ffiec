## Headline

The chain-of-custody spec is cryptographically sound and operationally achievable for the compliance function's audit and litigation needs. **Marcus's read pass found a gap between what AML compliance programs need to prove to FinCEN examiners and what the spec's current scope addresses.** The spec captures AI decisions and proves they were not tampered with after the fact. It does NOT capture or normalize the AML-specific decision-classification context examiners demand: was this decision a true positive, a false positive, a policy-conscious override, a threshold-tuning artifact, or a suppression the model made and the compliance team later remediated? The spec is the integrity foundation. It is not the AML-decision-attribution foundation. AML compliance teams will need supplementary schema design and operational procedures to capture AML decision rationales in a way that the chain proves their authenticity but the compliance team can cite to examiners without guessing.

**What the spec does well for AML.** The spec's per-event MAC and daily Merkle seal prove no alert or decision was inserted, deleted, or modified after capture—the core integrity requirement for defending against allegations of "you hid the alert" or "you altered the decision after the fact." The verifier procedure (spec §7) is mechanically auditable: a compliance officer can run the verifier, get a PASS/FAIL, and confidently cite it to an examiner ("here is the independent verification that these 50,000 TM alerts were not tampered with"). The operational-events schema (spec §4.4) can carry AML-specific events (alert triage decision, false-negative recheck, policy-override reason). The incident-response playbook (incident-response-playbook.md) covers the 36-hour notification window for key compromise, which aligns with FinCEN breach-notification expectations.

**What the spec needs for AML program operations.** An AML-conformance extension to the spec normalizing the events AML programs emit and the decision-attribution metadata they must carry: `audit.tm.alert_generated` (the model generated a TM alert), `audit.tm.alert_suppressed` (the model suppressed an alert it might have generated), `audit.tm.alert_triage` (the compliance team triaged the alert true/false/review), `audit.ofac.match` (OFAC screening result: hit, false positive, waived), `audit.aml.sar_decision` (the bank decided to file or not file a SAR), `audit.aml.lookback_remediation` (a past decision was audited and updated). Without normalization, each institution will home-grow its own AML audit-event taxonomy, examiners will see three different taxonomies across three sampled institutions, and the compliance-evidence trail will be institution-specific instead of industry-standard. An AML extension, plus an audit-procedures entry on AML-decision completeness, plus an examiner-training addendum would close the gap.

## Supporting questions

### Q-1. Transaction-monitoring alert generation — event structure and false-positive accounting

When the bank's TM system (whether AI-powered or rules-based) generates an alert on an anomalous transaction, what event does the chain capture? Spec §4.4 lists the audit.* namespace and chain_kind enumeration, but does not name a normative `audit.tm.alert_generated` event structure. An alert is a model decision with forensic importance (the model detected this transaction as suspicious). Without a normalized schema, Compliance A captures `"alert_id": "12345", "transaction_id": "tx_999", "score": 0.87`, while Compliance B captures `"alert_generated_by": "random-forest", "transaction": {...full transaction...}, "flags": ["velocity", "geography"]`. During an examination, the OCC sampler who compares the two banks' TM practices sees incompatible evidence trails. **Ask:** Add to spec §4.4 (Audit event schema) or create a new design document `12-aml-event-schema.md`: define `audit.tm.alert_generated` with normalized fields: `alert_id` (unique identifier for the alert in the bank's TM ledger), `transaction_id` (the transaction that triggered it), `model_name` (the TM model or rules engine), `score_or_rule_match` (the numeric score if ML, or rule name if rules-based), `flags` (list of detected factors: `velocity, geography, customer_typology, amount_threshold`), `alert_severity` (normalized: `high, medium, low`). Signature: Compliance teams will emit this event immediately when the TM system fires an alert, before any human triage. The verifier proves the event was not backdated or modified after filing. For future scope (v1.x), this event may be exported to FinCEN as part of the e-filing wire spec.

### Q-2. Alert suppression — why the model or rules withheld an alert

AML examiners increasingly ask: "Show us an alert your TM system did NOT generate but probably should have." This is the false-negative discovery question. The chain captures alerts the model generated. It does NOT capture alerts the model suppressed. A sophisticated TM system may suppress low-confidence alerts (score 0.2 when the threshold is 0.5), deduplicate recent alerts on the same customer (only alert once per hour), or apply policy overrides (suppress alerts on whitelisted corridors). When the Compliance team performs a look-back and discovers a suppressed-and-missed alert, how does the chain help reconstruct what the TM system saw?

Today, the answer is: the chain doesn't help directly. The compliance team must query the TM system's separate audit logs (if they exist and are retained) to retrieve the suppressed-alert history. The chain captures the downstream decision (the SAR that should have been filed), not the upstream suppression.

**Ask:** Add to design `12-aml-event-schema.md` and audit-procedures: define `audit.tm.alert_suppressed` with fields: `would_be_alert_id` (hypothetical ID if the alert were emitted), `transaction_id`, `model_score` (what score the TM system computed), `suppression_reason` (enum: `below_threshold, deduplication_recent, policy_exception, cooldown`), `emitted_at` (the timestamp when the model made the suppression decision). Behavior: The TM system is configured to emit this event for every alert it suppresses (not just the ones that later turn out to be misses; all suppressions). This may generate high volume. The Compliance team can then run queries like "show me all suppressed alerts on customer X in the past year" and use the chain to prove the suppression decision was made at a specific time and not fabricated after the fact. For FinCEN look-back response: "On 2026-03-15 at 14:32:10 UTC, our TM system suppressed a potential alert on transaction TX_88888 because the customer was on our low-risk corridor whitelist. Here is the chain proof that suppression was made in real time, not after the fact when we discovered the transaction was actually suspicious."

### Q-3. Alert triage and disposition — true positive, false positive, policy exception

After the TM system generates an alert, the Compliance team triages it: is this a true positive (send to SAR decision), false positive (dismiss), or policy exception (customer flagged but OK per policy, no SAR needed)? The chain captures the alert event (Q-1). But who captured the triage decision, and when? If the triage happens in a separate system (the SAR-management platform, a compliance case-management system), the chain doesn't see it directly—the chain sees only the downstream SAR decision (if one is filed). 

FinCEN examiners sample TM alerts and cross-check: "We found 100 TM alerts in your ledger from March. We sampled 10. For 7 of the 10, you filed a SAR. For 3, you didn't. What was the triage decision on those 3?" If the answer is "we triaged them as false positives and Compliance dismissed them," the examiner asks: "Show us the triage record." If that record is not in the chain, the bank is citing a separate system, and the chain doesn't contribute to the defense.

**Ask:** Add a normative `audit.aml.alert_triage` event: Compliance team emits this event when they complete triage of a TM alert. Fields: `alert_id` (cross-references the Q-1 event), `triage_decision` (enum: `true_positive, false_positive, policy_exception`), `triage_reason` (free text: "customer is a known MSB with legitimate high activity" or "score was driven by FX volatility on wire outside bank's CDD scope"), `triaged_by` (Compliance officer's ID), `escalated_to_sar` (boolean: is this alert escalated to SAR decision workflow?). When the examiner asks "why didn't you file a SAR on alert X?", Compliance can point to the chain entry: "Here is the chain entry from 2026-02-15 16:47:33 UTC where Compliance triaged this as a false positive because [reason], chain-verified with SHA-256 MAC and HSM-signed Merkle seal. We did not escalate because the triage determination made the SAR decision straightforward."

### Q-4. OFAC sanctions screening — hit disposition and false-positive management

The bank screens customer names against the OFAC SDN (Specially Designated Nationals) list and other screening databases. A name-matching algorithm flags a potential hit; the Compliance team investigates and determines: true hit (block the transaction, escalate), false positive (the customer is John Smith but the hit was on a different John Smith), or waived (the customer is a known SDN but under a policy exception per Treasury guidance).

The chain can capture the screening result event. But what metadata is needed to allow an examiner (or a FinCEN investigator) to understand why a hit was waived? The spec's audit.* namespace does not define normative fields for OFAC screening decisions.

**Ask:** Add to design `12-aml-event-schema.md`: define `audit.ofac.screening_result` with normalized fields: `screening_id` (unique ID for the screening run), `customer_id` (tokenized per privacy-by-design, or a customer reference), `screening_list` (which list was screened against: `SDN, SDGT, DPL, EU_consolidated, etc.`), `hits_found` (number of potential name matches), `match_scores` (list of similarity scores for each hit, using the bank's matching algorithm), `disposition` (enum: `true_hit, false_positive, waived_per_policy`), `waiver_authority` (if waived: "Treasury Ofac General License GL-101" or "internal policy exception approved by Chief Compliance Officer"), `resolved_by` (Compliance officer), `resolved_at` (timestamp). When the examiner audits OFAC compliance and asks "why did you waive this hit on Petrov?", the bank produces the chain entry with the waiver authority visible. The chain proves the waiver decision was made in real time and not backdated.

### Q-5. SAR decision attribution — when did the bank decide to file?

The bank captures the TM alert (Q-1), triages it (Q-3), and eventually files a SAR with FinCEN if the facts warrant one. The SAR narrative is drafted by the Compliance team (increasingly with AI assistance). The filing date is recorded in the SAR repository. But the decision date—when did Compliance commit to "we will file a SAR on this customer"—is often fuzzy. Was it immediately after the TM alert? After investigation? After management review?

FinCEN look-back orders and OCC examinations demand clarity: "Walk us through the SAR decision timeline for customer X." If the bank says "we filed a SAR on 2026-03-15," the examiner asks "when did you *decide* to file it? What triggered the decision?" The chain can help close this gap if the Compliance team emits a clear `audit.aml.sar_decision` event at the moment they commit to filing.

**Ask:** Add to design `12-aml-event-schema.md`: define `audit.aml.sar_decision` (fired when the Compliance team commits to filing a SAR) with fields: `customer_id` (tokenized), `decision` (enum: `will_file, will_not_file, undecided_pending_investigation`), `decision_rationale` (free text summarizing the decision reason), `supporting_alert_ids` (list of TM alerts or investigation activities that led to the decision), `filed_by_officer` (the Compliance officer who made the decision), `decided_at` (UTC timestamp when the decision was made). If the decision is `will_file`, a second event `audit.aml.sar_filed` is emitted once the actual SAR is submitted to FinCEN (this captures the submission timestamp, the SAR ID from FinCEN, any FinCEN feedback). The gap between `sar_decision` and `sar_filed` timestamps is the investigation window; examiners can audit whether the institution took appropriate time or rushed the filing.

### Q-6. SAR narrative AI attribution — which parts did the model write?

The 2019 SAR Narrative Rule requires banks to file narratives that adequately describe the facts and basis for suspicion. Increasingly, AML compliance teams use AI (GPT, Claude, etc.) to draft narratives, either generating candidate language or co-authoring with human reviewers. FinCEN and OCC examiners are beginning to ask: "Was this narrative drafted by AI or by a human analyst?" The distinction matters for model-risk governance (SR 11-7) and for the SAR's reliability (if the narrative was hallucinated or factually wrong because the AI model misunderstood the inputs).

The chain can capture `audit.aml.sar_narrative_ai_assisted` at the moment the Compliance team decides to use AI for drafting. But the spec does not define the normalized fields or the audit procedure to verify AI-assisted narratives during examination.

**Ask:** Add to design `12-aml-event-schema.md`: define `audit.aml.sar_narrative_drafted` with fields: `sar_id` (the SAR identifier), `drafted_by` (enum: `human_only, ai_assisted, ai_generated_human_reviewed`), `model_if_ai` (if AI-assisted: which model, e.g., "gpt-4-turbo"), `model_version` (the version tag for reproducibility), `human_reviewer_id` (the Compliance officer who reviewed the AI-drafted narrative), `reviewed_at` (UTC timestamp), `facts_verified_by` (the source of the facts in the narrative: TM system, manual investigation, SAR case file), `narrative_integrity_check` (the bank's internal QA: hallucination scan, factual accuracy verification, FinCEN guidance alignment check). For an OCC examiner asking "show us one AI-assisted SAR," the bank can produce the chain entry with full provenance. For model-risk testing (SR 11-7 model validation), the Compliance team can query the chain for all AI-assisted narratives and audit a sample for accuracy.

### Q-7. KYC/CDD enrichment via ML — customer-risk-score attribution

Many banks use ML models to enrich KYC/CDD (Know Your Customer / Customer Due Diligence) data: the model infers customer risk from transaction history, network behavior, geolocation patterns, or third-party data sources. These risk scores feed into customer typology decisions ("is this a money-services business or a retail customer?"), which in turn feed into transaction-monitoring thresholds and SAR decision logic.

If the model inferred "this customer is high-risk based on transaction velocity," and the bank later filed a SAR citing that risk determination, an examiner asks: "What evidence supports this customer classification?" If the Compliance team responds "our ML model scored them high risk," the examiner asks: "Show me the model's inputs, the version, the training data," etc. (SR 11-7 model governance). The chain can help the Compliance team prove "at the moment we classified this customer as high-risk, here is what the model saw and what it output."

**Ask:** Add to design `12-aml-event-schema.md`: define `audit.kyc.customer_risk_score_computed` with fields: `customer_id` (tokenized), `model_name` (e.g., "customer-risk-scorer-v2"), `model_version` (build hash or version tag), `score` (numeric risk score), `risk_category` (enum: `low, medium, high, critical`), `primary_factors` (top 3-5 risk factors the model identified, e.g., `["velocity_high_percentile", "geography_high_risk_country", "network_connection_to_sdn_adjacent"]`), `computed_at` (UTC timestamp), `inputs_used` (list of data sources: `transaction_history_12m, network_analysis, sanctions_screening_history`). When the Compliance team assigns a customer to a risk tier (affecting monitoring thresholds), they emit `audit.kyc.customer_tier_assigned` referencing the risk score: `customer_id, tier (enum: low/medium/high), assigned_on_date, assigned_by, rationale (free text: "customer_risk_score is high; assigned to enhanced monitoring per policy")`. The chain ties the customer classification to the model's decision.

### Q-8. False-negative discovery and lookback remediation

Periodically, the Compliance team performs a look-back: they select a cohort of SAR filings, verify that each SAR filed was appropriate, and also verify that no SAR-worthy alerts were missed. When a missed alert is discovered (Q-2: the TM model suppressed an alert that should have been escalated), the Compliance team must document what they did about it. Did they re-file a late SAR? Did they add new training data to the TM model? Did they adjust the model's threshold? The examiner wants to see evidence of remediation.

The chain can capture both the missed-alert discovery and the remediation decision. Without this, the Compliance team writes a memo saying "we found 3 missed alerts in cohort Y and retrained the model," but there is no integrity-bound proof that the remediation was actually implemented or that the model was actually retrained (it could have been a memo exercise, not a real fix).

**Ask:** Add to design `12-aml-event-schema.md` and audit-procedures: define `audit.aml.lookback_initiated` (when Compliance starts a look-back: `lookback_id, cohort_description (e.g., "all SARs filed Jan-Mar 2026"), initiated_by, initiated_at`), then `audit.aml.lookback_finding` (when a finding is discovered: `lookback_id, finding_type (enum: inappropriate_sar_filed, sar_should_have_been_filed_false_negative, threshold_drift), alert_or_sar_id_affected, description`), then `audit.aml.lookback_remediation` (when Compliance decides on remediation: `lookback_id, finding_id, remediation_action (enum: model_retrain_with_new_data, threshold_adjustment, policy_exception_removed, enhanced_manual_review), action_details (free text and metrics: "retrained random forest on 5000 false-negative examples from 2024"), action_start_date, action_completion_date, responsible_officer`). The Merkle seal on the remediation date proves "on 2026-04-10 we decided to retrain the model." If the OCC asks "you found a missed alert on 2026-03-15—when did you remediate?" the Compliance team can produce the chain entry: "We initiated look-back on 2026-03-20, discovered the missed alert on 2026-03-25, and deployed retraining on 2026-04-10, chain-verified."

### Q-9. Tuning decision attribution — when thresholds change

AML compliance teams periodically tune TM thresholds in response to false-positive rates, regulatory feedback, or business changes. "We're raising the velocity threshold from $100k/day to $150k/day for wire transfers" is a high-impact compliance decision. During an examination, the OCC asks: "When did you change this threshold? What was the justification? Did you test it?" The Compliance team must prove the change was made intentionally and documented, not a silent code push or a misconfiguration.

The chain can capture a normative event recording the threshold change. But the spec does not define what fields are needed to make the event auditable.

**Ask:** Add to design `12-aml-event-schema.md`: define `audit.tm.threshold_tuned` (emitted when Compliance makes a deliberate threshold change) with fields: `model_name_or_rule_name`, `parameter_name` (e.g., "velocity_threshold_usd_per_day_wire"), `old_value` (e.g., `100000`), `new_value` (e.g., `150000`), `justification` (free text: "reducing false positives on corporates; Q1 2026 data showed 35% FP rate"), `approved_by` (Compliance officer and date), `tested_in_sandbox` (boolean: was the change tested in a non-production environment first?), `test_results_summary` (if tested: "retest on Q1 2026 data showed FP rate dropped to 18%; TP rate unchanged at 97%"), `deployed_at` (UTC timestamp when the change went live), `expected_impact` (free text: "expect 10-20 fewer false-positive alerts per day, no impact on true positives"). If the OCC later asks "this TM change seems to have missed suspicious activity on customer X," the Compliance team can produce the chain entry showing the rationale and test results.

### Q-10. Customer dispute response and chain evidence production

When a customer disputes a TM block or a SAR decision, the Compliance team must produce evidence to the customer (and potentially to the customer's lawyer or a court). The bank may cite the TM alert, the triage decision (Q-3), and the SAR decision (Q-5) as evidence that the decision was not arbitrary. The chain proves these decisions were not forged after the fact.

But the spec does not address the evidentiary format the Compliance team should produce to the customer. Is it the raw chain entries (JSON with hashes and MACs)? A human-readable summary? A verifier report? Without guidance, each bank will produce different formats, and a customer's lawyer will challenge "how do we know this chain entry is authentic?"

**Ask:** Add to customer-dispute-procedures.md or create a new `13-chain-evidence-customer-disclosure.md`: "When a customer disputes an AML decision (TM block, SAR filing), the Compliance team may produce chain evidence to support the bank's position. The production format: (1) **Verifier Report**: Run the standalone verifier on the ledger span covering the customer's activity and dispute window. Produce the verifier's PASS/FAIL report to the customer with a cover letter: 'This report proves our AML system's records on this matter were not altered after capture.' (2) **Chain Entry Extracts**: Extract the specific chain entries relevant to the customer (alert generated, triage decision, SAR decision) in human-readable form (canonical JSON with field labels) and include them in the dispute response package. (3) **Certification**: The Compliance Officer signs a certification: 'These entries are true and accurate copies from our ledger, verified by [verifier version] on [date].' (4) **Redaction Guidance**: Redact any sensitive information (other customers' data, enforcement actions, investigations) per the bank's privacy policy, but preserve the customer-facing findings (why the decision was made about this customer). The verifier report is the integrity anchor; the certified extracts are the readable evidence. Together, they show the customer 'here is what our system did on your account, chain-verified.'"

### Q-11. FinCEN look-back order response — chain as the primary evidence source

When FinCEN issues a look-back order ("provide all records and decisions relating to customer X for the period Y-Z"), the Compliance team currently responds by exporting flat files of SAR filings, TM alert logs, and Compliance case notes from multiple systems. The examiners then must manually cross-reference and verify consistency.

A chain-of-custody ledger could be the primary response mechanism: "Here are all AML-related decisions on customer X during the period, verified by independent verifier report. The chain proves no records were inserted, deleted, or modified after capture." But the spec does not address the operational mechanics of preparing a FinCEN response or certifying that the chain is complete for the requested cohort.

**Ask:** Add to regulator-pack or create `14-finCEN-response-procedures.md`: "**FinCEN Look-Back Order Response Procedures** 1. **Scope the request**: Identify all chain entries matching the customer_id (tokenized per privacy-by-design) and the date range. Query the ledger for `(customer_id_token, event_date >= Y, event_date <= Z)`. 2. **Completeness assertion**: Confirm all relevant AML events are in scope (TM alerts, triage decisions, SAR decisions, KYC updates, look-back findings). If any events are missing (e.g., TM alerts exist but triage decisions are in a separate system not yet integrated into the chain), the Compliance team explicitly states what is in the chain and what is outside scope. 3. **Verify the ledger span**: Run the verifier on the requested period, produce a signed verifier report showing PASS or FAIL. If FAIL, investigate (usually indicates a missing key or configuration mismatch); if PASS, include the report in the response. 4. **Produce the response package**: (a) Verifier report (PASS); (b) chain-entry exports in CSV or JSON, one line per entry, with human-readable field labels; (c) A Compliance Officer certification statement: 'These entries are complete and accurate copies from our chain-of-custody ledger, independently verified by [verifier version]. The chain's integrity proves no entries were inserted, deleted, or modified after capture. The redactions below are applied per [policy reference]; no material facts have been redacted.' (d) Redaction legend (if any). 5. **Audit trail of the response**: The Compliance team logs that the response was prepared, the verifier was run, the certification was signed, and the package was delivered to FinCEN, per the 'operational events' schema. This creates an audit trail of the response itself."

### Q-13. Alert volume trending and model performance monitoring

FinCEN's 2024 AML Effectiveness Rule emphasizes **effective AML programs**, not just compliant structures. Effectiveness includes: alert volume trends, false-positive rates, SAR filing rates, and model-performance metrics. The Compliance team monitors these KPIs monthly to detect drift (model is generating too many false alerts, triggering alert fatigue). The chain can capture performance metrics events, allowing the Compliance team to prove they monitored model effectiveness.

**Ask:** Add to design `12-aml-event-schema.md`: define `audit.aml.performance_report_generated` (emitted monthly by Compliance team) with fields: `reporting_month`, `model_name`, `alerts_generated` (count), `alerts_triaged_true_positive` (count), `alerts_triaged_false_positive` (count), `false_positive_rate` (percentage), `sars_filed` (count), `lookback_findings` (count of missed alerts found in prior-month look-back), `model_threshold_changes_this_month` (count), `threshold_rationale_summary` (summary of why any changes were made), `reported_by` (Compliance officer), `reported_at` (UTC timestamp). The monthly performance report, when captured in the chain, becomes an integral part of the Compliance team's documented risk assessment (FinCEN 2024 Rule pillar 2). The examiner can pull a year of monthly reports from the chain and see: "July 2026, alert volume 1200; August 2026, alert volume 1800; Compliance investigated and lowered the threshold; September 2026, 1300 alerts and stable." The chain proves the performance monitoring was ongoing and documented, not reconstructed after the fact.

### Q-14. OFAC regulatory response — SRCs and Advisory documentation

When FinCEN issues an OFAC advisory (e.g., "beware high-risk jurisdictions for trade finance"), the Compliance team assesses applicability and may issue an Specific Regulatory Compliance (SRC) or an internal advisory to the front office. The chain can capture when advisories are received and how the institution responded.

**Ask:** Add to design `12-aml-event-schema.md`: define `audit.ofac.advisory_received` (when FinCEN or another regulator issues an OFAC advisory) with fields: `advisory_id` (e.g., "FIN-2024-A101"), `advisory_date`, `advisory_subject`, `institution_response_required` (boolean), `response_due_date`. If a response is required, emit `audit.ofac.advisory_response_completed` with fields: `advisory_id, response_taken` (enum: `enhanced_screening, customer_update, threshold_adjustment, policy_clarification`), `response_details` (free text: "added [country] to high-risk jurisdiction list; notified front office on 2024-03-20"), `completed_at`. During an examination, the OCC asks "did you respond to FIN-2024-A101?" The Compliance team can quickly query the chain for all OFAC advisories and responses, proving the institution tracked and responded to every advisory.

### Q-15. Model explainability and factor attribution — what drove the decision?

When a TM alert fires on a customer, the Compliance team needs to know why. Modern ML models (tree ensembles, neural networks) can be "black boxes"—the output is a score, but the reasoning is opaque. Some AML platforms provide model-explainability features (SHAP, LIME) to decompose the score into contributing factors. The chain can capture these factors, proving what the model saw when it made the decision.

**Ask:** Extend Q-1 (`audit.tm.alert_generated`) to include `explanation_factors`: a list of the top factors the model considered (e.g., `[{"factor": "customer_transaction_velocity_percentile", "contribution": 0.34}, {"factor": "geography_customer_is_in_high_risk_country", "contribution": 0.28}]`), using SHAP or a normalized explainability format. Compliance teams can then audit: "The model flagged customer X because of velocity and geography. That seems reasonable. Triaging as true positive." An OCC examiner can ask "why did your model flag this customer?" and Compliance can point to the chain entry showing the factors. This supports SR 11-7 model governance (the institution understands what the model is doing) and supports customer disputes (the bank can explain the reasoning).

### Q-16. Beneficial Ownership (BO) AML determination — ML-assisted inference

AMLA requires banks to collect beneficial-ownership information for legal-entity customers. Some banks use ML to infer BO from public filings, corporate databases, and transaction patterns ("this customer is likely owned by Person X based on transaction flows"). If the model infers BO and the Compliance team relies on that inference for customer classification or SAR decision, the chain should capture the inference.

**Ask:** Add to design `12-aml-event-schema.md`: define `audit.kyc.beneficial_owner_inferred` (when an ML model or data-enrichment service infers beneficial ownership) with fields: `customer_id` (tokenized), `legal_entity_name`, `inferred_bo_id` (tokenized identifier for the inferred owner), `confidence_score` (if ML: the model's confidence), `inference_method` (enum: `ml_model, public_filings_match, transaction_flow_analysis, third_party_data_provider`), `supporting_evidence` (summary of the evidence), `inferred_at`, `confirmed_by_customer_disclosure` (boolean: did the customer confirm this BO?). If the inferred BO is later found to be incorrect (customer disputes it or internal audit finds an error), the Compliance team can emit a correction event and the chain records both the original inference and the correction.

### Q-17. Continuity of monitoring — cross-border customer tracking

When a customer initiates a transaction to a foreign jurisdiction or when a customer relationship spans multiple regions (e.g., a multinational customer with operations in US + EU + APAC), the bank's AML monitoring must remain continuous. The chain can help prove continuous monitoring across regions if each region emits events to a consolidated ledger.

Per spec §10.15 (multi-region deployment), Pattern A uses a canonical tenant_id across regions and replicates events to a seal region. **Ask:** Add to the AML extension: confirm that a Compliance team operating in multiple regions can query the consolidated chain for a customer and see all AML events across all regions in one audit trail. Provide an audit procedure: "For multi-region institutions, sample 10 customers with cross-region activity; verify the chain contains TM alerts from all regions where the customer was active; verify no region was silently dropped from monitoring."

### Q-18. CTR automation and cash-transaction reporting

Many banks are automating Currency Transaction Reporting (CTR) via ML models that flag cash transactions meeting reporting thresholds. The chain can capture CTR filing decisions, proving they were not made post-hoc or altered.

**Ask:** Add to design `12-aml-event-schema.md`: define `audit.aml.ctr_filed` (when the bank files a Currency Transaction Report) with fields: `transaction_id`, `customer_id` (tokenized), `amount_usd`, `filing_reason` (enum: `meets_reporting_threshold_10k, meets_reporting_threshold_aggregated, filed_at_customer_request`), `filed_by_system` (was it automated or manual?), `filed_at` (UTC timestamp), `ctr_id_from_FinCEN` (if filed to FinCEN, the acknowledgment ID). For automated CTR systems, the model name and version. For manual CTR, the officer's ID. When FinCEN inquires about a CTR filing, the bank can produce the chain entry.

### Q-19. Customer-to-beneficial-owner mapping and name-similarity tolerance

When the bank screens a transaction, it screens the customer AND the beneficial owner (if known). If the customer "John Smith" has a beneficial owner "Jon Smyth," does the bank screen both variations against the SDN list? How does the bank prove it screened the BO? The chain can capture which variations were screened and which were suppressed (e.g., "screened customer 'John Smith', also screened BO 'Jon Smyth'; both passed SDN").

**Ask:** Extend Q-4 (`audit.ofac.screening_result`) to include `additional_names_screened` (list of related names, e.g., BO, guarantor, authorized users) and the screening result for each. This proves comprehensive screening.

### Q-20. Regime-specific AML tuning — jurisdictional policy adjustments

Different jurisdictions have different AML requirements (EU has different thresholds and risk factors than US). When a bank operates across jurisdictions, it may tune its AML parameters per regime. The chain can capture regime-specific tuning decisions.

**Ask:** Extend Q-9 (`audit.tm.threshold_tuned`) to include `jurisdiction_scope` (e.g., `"US", "EU", "APAC"`) to document which regime the tuning applies to. This allows examiners to verify regional compliance separately.

### Q-21. Model-risk governance over AML models — alignment with SR 11-7

OCC Bulletin 2021-53 (Model Risk Management for Third-Party Models) and SR 11-7 (Guidance on Model Risk Management) require banks to monitor, validate, and govern AI models. AML models are subject to these requirements. The chain can capture model-governance events (validation testing, performance monitoring, model retirement) in a way that proves governance was ongoing and documented.

**Ask:** Add to the AML extension: define `audit.model_risk.validation_test_completed` (when the Compliance team or independent audit tests an AML model) with fields: `model_name, test_date, test_type` (enum: `annual_backtesting, fairness_audit, false_positive_analysis, threshold_sensitivity_analysis`), `test_results_summary`, `passed_validation` (boolean), `findings_and_remediation` (if failed: what remediation was taken). When the OCC asks "did you validate your AML models annually?", the Compliance team can query the chain for all validation events. When the OCC asks "did you identify that model X was underperforming and did you take corrective action?", the chain can show validation event → failure identification → model retraining event → post-retraining validation. The chain is the proof of ongoing governance.

### Q-22. Adverse-action notice interaction with AML decisions

When a bank denies credit based partly on AML factors (e.g., "customer failed OFAC screening" or "customer flagged as high-risk by AML monitoring"), the bank must provide an adverse-action notice per ECOA / Reg B. The Compliance team's AML decision (Q-5: SAR decision) may inform the credit decision, and the adverse-action notice must cite a reason. The chain can prove what the AML system said when the credit decision was made, supporting the adverse-action notice's accuracy.

**Ask:** Add to design `12-aml-event-schema.md`: define `audit.aml.adverse_action_notice_issued` with fields: `customer_id` (tokenized), `adverse_action_reason` (e.g., `"failed_aml_customer_risk_assessment"` or `"transactions_inconsistent_with_stated_business"`), `supporting_aml_decision_ids` (cross-references to TM alert, triage decision, or KYC risk score), `notice_issued_at`, `issued_to_customer` (date). During a CFPB fair-lending examination, the examiner can verify: "Customer A received adverse action notice citing AML reasons. Here is the chain entry showing what the AML system actually said. The notice accurately reflects the AML determination." This supports fair-lending compliance.

---

## Closing assessment

**Partial? Yes.** The spec's integrity-bearing chain is solid and operationally achievable for AML compliance. However, the spec does not address AML-specific event schema, audit procedures, or examiner-guidance documents that would allow Compliance teams to use the chain as the primary evidence source for FinCEN, OCC, and FDIC examinations. The 22 questions above are not defects in the chain's cryptography or operations—they are scope gaps between "chain-of-custody can capture any event the institution emits" and "AML teams know exactly what events to emit and how examiners will interpret them." An AML extension to the spec (a new design document `12-aml-event-schema.md`, audit-procedures entries for AML-decision completeness, an examiner-training addendum, and a compliance-procedures guide for FinCEN response) would close these gaps. The extension is within scope—it's not changing the chain's primitives, just normalizing the audit-event taxonomy for AML use cases. Without it, each AML compliance team will home-grow its own event schema, examiners will see inconsistency, and the chain will become "integrity-bearing but not directly usable for AML compliance proof."

**Confidence in BSA/AML program coverage.** The chain, as extended per the 22 questions, would provide the Compliance team with a tamper-evident, independent-verifier-auditable ledger of all AML decisions: alerts, triage, SAR filings, OFAC disposition, KYC risk scores, model tuning, and false-negative remediation. A FinCEN examiner running the verifier would see PASS and know "every alert and decision in this cohort was made in real time and not altered." A customer disputing a SAR decision could be shown chain evidence proving "on this date at this time, our system determined you were a high-risk customer and escalated to SAR investigation." An internal audit team could query the chain for all model-governance events and confirm "we tested the TM model annually, documented findings, and took corrective actions." The chain becomes the backbone of the Compliance program's audit trail, supporting both regulatory examination and litigation defense. This is high-impact for the institution's AML effectiveness rating under the 2024 Rule (pillar 4: independent testing and monitoring) and the Compliance Officer's annual certification to the board.

**Recommended next steps.**

(1) **AML schema extension (v1.x roadmap).** The FFIEC working group should task a subgroup with AML domain expertise to draft `design/12-aml-event-schema.md` and `design/13-aml-audit-procedures.md`, defining the normative audit-event types and procedures for Compliance teams. The subgroup should include practitioners from tier-1 banks with AML compliance programs, a FinCEN examiner, and an independent compliance consultant. Deliverable: approved schema and procedures for v1.1 or as a v1.0 amendment if immediate clarity is needed.

(2) **Examiner training addendum.** The regulator-pack (docs/regulator-pack/) should include an `aml-examiner-training.md` document (analogous to the existing examiner-training.md for IT examiners) that equips OCC, FDIC, and FinCEN examiners with the knowledge to evaluate a bank's AML chain-of-custody implementation during exam. Content: how to sample AML events from the chain, what to look for in the event schema, how to use the chain as evidence for look-back orders, false-negative discovery procedures, and model-governance verification.

(3) **Compliance-procedures guide.** Banks need `compliance-procedure-guide.md` (for the Compliance program, not the IT team) that walks through the operational steps: "Here is how to emit TM alert events. Here is how to triage them. Here is how to tie alerts to SAR decisions. Here is how to respond to a FinCEN look-back order with chain evidence." This guide translates the schema into Compliance ops language.

(4) **Privacy-by-design for customer-identity tokenization.** The AML extension should clarify how customer-identity tokenization (per Elena's privacy-by-design.md review) interacts with AML's need to track customers across regions and over time. A customer ID token must remain consistent within a tenant_id so that "customer X is flagged as high-risk" can be tracked across a year of transactions. The privacy-store (privacy-by-design.md) maintains the token-to-original mapping; the chain never sees the original customer name. This is operationally complex; the guidance should provide examples.

(5) **Future v1.x: FinCEN e-filing wire format.** Once the AML schema stabilizes, a future v1.x spec can define a FinCEN e-filing format (akin to the SAR filing standard currently in XML). The chain's normalized AML events can be exported directly to FinCEN in a machine-readable format, reducing manual SAR drafting and improving data quality. This is a v1.x roadmap commitment, not v1.0, but the schema design should anticipate it.

**For the next FinCEN exam cycle.** Banks deploying the chain for AML purposes should expect FinCEN examiners (starting mid-2026 as early adopters go live) to ask: "Show us your chain for this customer cohort. What events did you capture? How did you verify completeness?" Banks should prepare by (i) implementing the AML schema extensions above, (ii) running the standalone verifier on sample cohorts and documenting the results, (iii) drafting their own look-back-order response procedures (per Q-11 guidance), and (iv) conducting a mock FinCEN examination internally (compliance team practices responding to examiner inquiries using chain evidence). The examiner's job will shift from "manually audit 100 SARs and cross-reference to TM logs in multiple systems" to "run the verifier on the chain, sample 20 events, verify they align with the SAR file, ask the Compliance Officer to explain any discrepancies." A chain that is operationally complete and examiner-ready will reduce exam burden and increase confidence in the Compliance program's effectiveness rating.
