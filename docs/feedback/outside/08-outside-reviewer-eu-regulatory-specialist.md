## Headline

The technical primitives are sound and the spec's writing discipline is unusual for a US-regulator product — the test-vector corpus, the byte-level reasoning at §4.3, the verifier output normativity at §7, all exceed what I typically see in EU-side technical standards. **My read pass found nine places where EU regulatory architecture creates obligations the spec does not address — not because the spec is defective for its US scope, but because the spec is FFIEC-scoped and EU institutions deploying it would need conformance-overlay documents the spec does not provide.** A pragmatic close-out path exists for each of these: most are informative annexes or supplementary regulator-pack documents, not normative spec changes. The spec's substantive choices (HMAC-SHA-256 + HKDF-SHA-256 + Ed25519 + RFC 8785 + RFC 6962 Merkle) are NIST-aligned and EU-conformance-friendly; the conformance gaps are at the documentation and operational-procedure layer, not at the cryptographic-primitive layer.

**What the spec does well for EU institutions.** RFC 8785 canonical JSON is internationally standardised and not US-jurisdiction-locked. The `chain_kind` enumeration is technology-neutral. The HSM custody requirement at §10.5 names cloud HSM products that have EU regions (AWS CloudHSM EU regions, Azure Managed HSM EU regions, Google Cloud HSM EU regions). The §10.15 multi-region resilience patterns (Pattern A active-active with seal-region pinning; Pattern B per-region tenant_id) align with EU institutions' multi-jurisdiction operations. The verifier output is byte-for-byte reproducible, which translates well to EU evidentiary-discovery procedures even though the FRE 702 / Daubert framing at §1.1 is US-specific.

**What the spec needs for EU regulatory conformance.** Annex documenting AI Act Article 12 mapping; clarification of eIDAS qualified electronic signature posture (the Ed25519 HSM seal is structurally close to a Qualified Electronic Signature but not currently QES under eIDAS); EU-Trusted-List timestamping authority guidance for §10.14's RFC 3161 trusted-time posture; EBA Guidelines on Outsourcing flow-down for multi-vendor topologies; multi-jurisdiction supervisory mapping (ECB SSM + national supervisors); EU regulatory incident-notification timing matrix (DORA 4-hour + NIS2 24-hour + GDPR 72-hour, separate from FFIEC's 36-hour); explicit EU cloud HSM listing in §10.5; multilingual artifact-handling generalisation beyond §10.11's ECOA-specific schema.

Each question below is a one-paragraph gap. The closing is an annex document, a supplementary regulator-pack file, an informative spec paragraph, or a supplementary EU-conformance overlay the working group could endorse without disrupting the spec's normative core.

## Supporting questions

### EQ-1. AI Act Article 12 logging — chain-attribute mapping is missing

**Question.** EU AI Act (Reg 2024/1689) Article 12(1) requires high-risk AI systems to "automatically record events ('logs') over the lifetime of the system." Article 12(2) requires logs to "ensure a level of traceability of the AI system's functioning that is appropriate to the intended purpose of the system" and to "facilitate the post-market monitoring referred to in Article 72." Article 12(3) lists specific recording requirements (period of use, reference databases, input data, identification of natural persons checking outputs). The chain captures substantively the right content (§4.4 attribute schema covers inputs, outputs, model identity, decision rationale, and `audit.routing.*` decisions), but the spec doesn't speak Article-12 vocabulary. An EU institution deploying the chain for an AI Act high-risk-system requires an "AI Act mapping document" naming which chain attributes/events satisfy which Article 12 requirements. Every EU institution writing this independently is a substantial duplication of effort; a mapping annex in the spec saves the effort and produces a consistent posture across institutions.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** A new informative annex (Annex C: "AI Act Article 12 mapping") naming each Article 12 requirement and the chain attribute(s) that fulfill it. For example: Art 12(2)(a) "the period of use of the AI system" → `chain_id` + `received_at` first/last entries delineate per-run usage period; Art 12(2)(b) "reference databases used" → `gen_ai_parameters.reference_databases` (RECOMMENDED new optional attribute) or institution's own ECOA-style attribute schema; Art 12(2)(c) "input data" → canonical JSON of application content per §4.1 inviolate property 7; Art 12(2)(d) "identification of natural persons checking outputs" → `audit.routing.reviewer_identity` (RECOMMENDED new optional attribute). The annex is informative — the institution can extend it with operational-context attributes — but it provides the baseline mapping every EU institution would otherwise write themselves.

### EQ-2. eIDAS Qualified Electronic Signature qualification — the Ed25519 HSM seal is not currently QES

**Question.** eIDAS Regulation (Reg 910/2014) Article 25(2) confers legal equivalence between Qualified Electronic Signatures (QES) and handwritten signatures. To qualify as a QES, a signature must be (a) created using a Qualified Electronic Signature Creation Device (QSCD) listed under the EU Trusted Lists, (b) issued by a Qualified Trust Service Provider (QTSP) on the EU Trusted Lists, and (c) the signing certificate must be a qualified certificate per Annex I. The §10.5 cloud HSM products (AWS CloudHSM, Azure Managed HSM, Google Cloud HSM) are FIPS 140-2 Level 3 but most are NOT EU eIDAS QSCD-certified. The Ed25519 seal under FIPS 140-2 Level 3 custody is structurally close to a QES — it satisfies the cryptographic strength requirements — but it is not currently QES under eIDAS because the QSCD certification is missing. EU institutions need to know: are v1.0 chain seals "Advanced Electronic Signatures" under eIDAS Art 26 (which is sufficient for most evidentiary purposes but does NOT carry the Art 25(2) handwritten-signature equivalence), or "Qualified Electronic Signatures" under Art 25? The answer affects evidentiary weight in EU courts and supervisory acceptance under ECB SSM.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** A new §1.3 (or addition to §1.2) titled "eIDAS posture (informative for EU institutions)" stating: "v1.0 chain seals signed under FIPS 140-2 Level 3 cloud HSMs are Advanced Electronic Signatures under eIDAS Art 26 — they satisfy the cryptographic-strength and identification-binding requirements but do NOT carry the EU Trusted List QSCD certification required for Qualified Electronic Signature equivalence under Art 25(2). EU institutions deploying the chain treat seals as Advanced Electronic Signatures for evidentiary purposes; this is sufficient for ECB SSM supervisory-examination access and EU-court evidentiary admissibility under most national procedural codes (Schoeller, MaxPlanck IPL Vol XXVII §3.4). v1.x defines a QES posture when EU eIDAS QSCD-certified HSM products meeting §10.5's FIPS 140-2 Level 3 requirements ship in EU regions; institutions requiring QES today operate compensating controls (paper-signed examiner attestation, qualified timestamp under eIDAS Art 42, or the v1.x QES extension)."

### EQ-3. eIDAS Qualified Electronic Time Stamp — §10.14 should name EU-QTSA-listed authorities

**Question.** eIDAS Reg 910/2014 Article 42 defines Qualified Electronic Time Stamps (QTS) issued by Qualified Trust Service Providers on the EU Trusted Lists for timestamping. A QTS carries a legal presumption of accuracy of the date and time it indicates and of the integrity of the data to which the date and time are bound. RFC 3161 timestamps are technically equivalent to QTS only if the TSA is EU-QTSA-listed (DigiCert, GlobalSign, Sectigo, Atos all operate EU-QTSA endpoints alongside their US/global endpoints). §10.14 names RFC 3161 as v1.0 RECOMMENDED + v1.x candidate for normative status, but doesn't name EU-QTSA-listed authorities specifically. EU institutions deploying RFC 3161 trusted-timestamps under the chain need guidance: which TSAs are conformant for EU evidentiary purposes? An RFC 3161 timestamp from a non-QTSA TSA is technically valid as an integrity binding but does NOT carry the eIDAS Art 42 legal presumption.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** §10.14 adds a paragraph: "**EU eIDAS QTS conformance (informative for EU institutions).** EU institutions deploying RFC 3161 trusted-timestamps under the chain SHOULD use a TSA on the EU Trusted Lists for QTS conformance under eIDAS Art 42; chain artifacts from non-QTSA TSAs are technically valid as integrity bindings but do NOT carry the eIDAS Art 42 legal presumption of accuracy. Conformant EU-QTSA TSAs include DigiCert EU QTSA, GlobalSign EU QTSA, Sectigo EU QTSA, and Atos QTSA; the institution's CC8.1 (or EBA-equivalent) control description names the TSA the institution uses and the institution's tenant configuration includes the TSA's certificate chain for integrity-binding verification. Multi-jurisdiction institutions may use multiple TSAs (one EU-QTSA for EU operations, one US-RFC-3161 for US operations); the chain's per-event canonical bytes accommodate either via the existing `ffiec.chain.*` attribute schema. The v1.x normative trusted-time extension MUST name EU-QTSA-listed conformance for institutions in scope."

### EQ-4. EBA Guidelines on Outsourcing — multi-vendor flow-down is not addressed

**Question.** EBA Guidelines on Outsourcing Arrangements (EBA/GL/2019/02) require institutions to retain audit rights, supervisor access, and chain-of-control assertions over all underlying providers in a multi-vendor topology. The chain's typical deployment involves an SDK from vendor A, ledger storage from vendor B, KMS / HSM from cloud provider C, and possibly an application-layer integration from vendor D. EBA GL 2019/02 paragraph 87 requires the institution's contract with vendor A to flow audit rights through to vendors B, C, D. The FFIEC spec's §10.5 HSM custody language addresses HSM custody but does not address the multi-vendor flow-down. EU institutions deploying a multi-vendor chain need contractual templates and a supervisor-pack document that demonstrates the flow-down. Each EU institution writing this independently is a substantial duplication; a supplementary regulator-pack document covers it once.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** A new supplementary document `docs/eu-regulator-pack/outsourcing-flow-down.md` or similar, covering: EBA GL 2019/02 contractual checklist (audit rights, supervisor access, sub-outsourcing notification, exit strategy, business continuity); ICT third-party risk register entries for each vendor in the chain topology; the multi-vendor topology diagram showing which vendor is responsible for which spec section; supervisor-pack examples showing how an examiner accesses chain artifacts across vendors. This is an EU-conformance overlay, not a normative spec change.

### EQ-5. Multi-jurisdiction supervision — Pattern B's EU mapping is implied but not stated

**Question.** A EU bank operating in DE/FR/IT/NL has BaFin, ACPR, Banca d'Italia, and DNB as national supervisors plus the ECB as the consolidated supervisor under SSM (for significant institutions) or as an indirect supervisor (for less significant institutions). Each may demand chain access during an examination — examination-data sovereignty rules typically require the institution to produce evidence accessible from within the supervisor's jurisdiction. §10.15 Pattern B (per-region tenant_id) covers this when "regions" map to "jurisdictions," but the spec doesn't cite the EU supervisory architecture as a motivating example. EU institutions reading §10.15 don't immediately see Pattern B as the recommended posture for multi-jurisdiction supervisory access. The §3 `Region` definition says "an institution-defined geography (e.g., us-east-1, eu-central-1) tied to operational considerations" — operational, not regulatory. Without explicit EU mapping, institutions may default to Pattern A (single seal-region pinning) and find that German operations' chain artifacts are stored under a French seal-region tenant — defensible technically, awkward when BaFin asks for German-jurisdiction-only chain artifacts.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** §10.15 Pattern B adds a paragraph: "**EU-jurisdiction posture (informative for EU institutions).** Pattern B (per-region tenant_id) is the recommended posture for EU institutions where per-jurisdiction event isolation supports per-supervisor examination access. Mapping per-jurisdiction tenant_ids onto EU national supervisors (e.g., `bank-de`, `bank-fr`, `bank-it`, `bank-nl` corresponding to BaFin, ACPR, Banca d'Italia, DNB respectively) aligns the chain with the EU SSM/national-supervisor topology and supports examination-data sovereignty rules under each national procedural code. ECB SSM consolidated supervision (for significant institutions) reads cross-tenant correlation as institution-side evidence; the institution's CC8.1 (or EBA-equivalent) control description documents the cross-jurisdiction correlation procedure. Per-jurisdiction tenant_id is also compatible with the §10.5 HSM custody requirement when each jurisdiction's tenant is custody-pinned to a HSM in the corresponding EU region (e.g., `bank-de` → AWS CloudHSM `eu-central-1`, `bank-fr` → AWS CloudHSM `eu-west-3`)."

### EQ-6. EU regulatory incident-notification timing matrix is missing

**Question.** EU institutions face simultaneous incident-notification clocks under multiple regulations:

- DORA (Reg 2022/2554) Art 19: "without undue delay" for major ICT-related incidents — interpreted as 4 hours from classification under the EBA RTS on incident reporting under DORA. Plus 72-hour intermediate report and 1-month final report.
- NIS2 Directive (Dir 2022/2555) Art 23: 24-hour Early Warning + 72-hour Notification for significant incidents.
- GDPR (Reg 2016/679) Art 33: 72 hours for personal-data breaches.
- National variants (BaFin reporting under MaRisk AT 7.2, ACPR reporting under instruction 2017-I-24, etc.).

The spec's incident-response-playbook supplementary document (referenced in the change-log under Diego's Wave-3 close-out) mentions FFIEC's 36-hour clock but doesn't provide an EU regulatory timing matrix. EU institutions writing their own incident-response runbooks need the matrix; absent it, institutions either write their own (substantial duplication) or risk incorrect notification posture during an actual incident.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** The supplementary `incident-response-playbook.md` adds a new section: "EU regulatory incident-notification timing matrix" with a table showing each regulation, its trigger event, its notification clock, its reporting authority, and the chain artifacts that support the notification. This supplementary document is an EU-conformance overlay layered on the existing FFIEC-side playbook; it does not change the spec's normative core.

### EQ-7. EU cloud HSM listings + Schrems II posture in §10.5

**Question.** §10.5 names cloud HSM products: AWS CloudHSM (cloudhsm-classic, Vendor's KMS, ResultedKMS), Azure Managed HSM, Google Cloud HSM, Thales Luna, Entrust nShield. All have EU regions, but §10.5 doesn't name the EU-region availability explicitly, and doesn't address the Schrems II-and-after data-residency posture. An EU institution deploying chain artifacts to a US-cloud HSM raises Schrems II concerns even when the HSM region is EU — the underlying contractual framework with the US-headquartered cloud provider is a Standard Contractual Clauses + Transfer Impact Assessment scenario. EU institutions evaluating §10.5 don't see this addressed; they have to do their own SCC + TIA work. Additionally, EU-headquartered HSM vendors (Atos, Idemia, Utimaco — none of whom are listed in §10.5) are FIPS 140-2 Level 3 conformant and would address Schrems II by being EU-headquartered with no US data-flow.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** §10.5 adds a paragraph: "**EU cloud HSM and Schrems II posture (informative for EU institutions).** EU-region availability of the cloud HSM products listed above includes: AWS CloudHSM (`eu-central-1`, `eu-west-1`, `eu-west-2`, `eu-west-3`, `eu-north-1`, `eu-south-1`, `eu-south-2`); Azure Managed HSM (Germany West Central, France Central, Italy North, Sweden Central, North Europe, West Europe); Google Cloud HSM (`europe-west*`, `europe-central*`, `europe-north*`); Thales Luna and Entrust nShield as on-premises deployments. EU-headquartered HSM vendors meeting §10.5's FIPS 140-2 Level 3 requirement include Atos Trustway, Idemia IDPrime, and Utimaco SecurityServer; institutions selecting these vendors operate without US-data-flow exposure to Schrems II analysis. Institutions deploying US-headquartered cloud HSM products in EU regions perform a Transfer Impact Assessment under the Schrems II framework and document the assessment in the institution's records of processing activities and in the institution's CC8.1 (or EBA-equivalent) control description. The chain's cryptographic primitives are unaffected by Schrems II — HMAC-SHA-256, HKDF, Ed25519, RFC 8785 are all NIST/IETF-standardised and not subject to data-flow restrictions; Schrems II concerns the legal framework around the HSM custody, not the cryptographic primitives running inside the HSM."

### EQ-8. Multilingual artifact handling beyond §10.11's ECOA scope

**Question.** §10.11 (ECOA adverse-action notice translation) addresses US-required translations into Spanish, Vietnamese, Korean, Chinese, and Tagalog. EU institutions operate across 24 official languages. The chain's canonical bytes are UTF-8 (RFC 8785-conformant), so the encoding handles arbitrary Unicode without modification — that's not the gap. The gap is the schema attribute layer: `audit.ecoa.translation.*` is jurisdictional in name, and an EU institution operating in DE/FR/IT/NL would have to either (a) reuse the `ecoa` namespace (semantically wrong — these are not ECOA notices), (b) define a national-equivalent attribute schema (each institution writing their own), or (c) wait for a v1.x extension. None of the three are operationally clean.

**Status:** Nit.

**Closing language proposal.** §10.11 generalises the schema: rename to `audit.adverse_action.*` (or add a parallel `audit.adverse_action.*` schema covering the same eight attributes) without dropping the `audit.ecoa.*` schema (US institutions continue to use it). EU institutions use `audit.adverse_action.*` with national-jurisdiction-specific values for `audit.adverse_action.regulatory_basis` (e.g., German Allgemeines Gleichbehandlungsgesetz §19, French Loi anti-discrimination, Dutch Algemene wet gelijke behandeling). The schema is the same; the regulatory-basis field carries the jurisdiction-specific reference. This is an editorial change and a candidate v1.x extension; v1.0 institutions either wait for v1.x or use the existing `audit.ecoa.*` schema with EU jurisdiction-specific values, accepting the namespace-mismatch as a known operational compromise.

### EQ-9. DORA Article 25 testing — chain test-vectors as TLPT inputs

**Question.** DORA Article 25 requires institutions to test ICT business continuity, including "advanced testing" via Threat-Led Penetration Testing (TLPT) under TIBER-EU framework. The FFIEC spec ships a substantial test-vector corpus (`spec/test-vectors/`) covering positive and negative cases including tampered chains, cross-tenant lifts, MAC tampers, fingerprint mismatches, and seal forgeries. EU institutions performing TLPT against their chain deployments could feed these test vectors as adversarial scenarios — the corpus is already structured as failure-mode-by-failure-mode. There's no current document that names this composability or provides a TLPT-mapping playbook. EU institutions writing their own TLPT scenarios reproduce the corpus's logical content; a supplementary document covers it once.

**Status:** Nit.

**Closing language proposal.** A supplementary document `docs/eu-regulator-pack/dora-tlpt-mapping.md` (or similar) showing the test-vector corpus mapped to TIBER-EU TLPT testing scenarios. The mapping is informational; the test vectors themselves are unchanged. Institutions performing TLPT cite the document as their TLPT scenario library and add institution-specific scenarios on top. This is an EU-conformance overlay; not a normative spec change.

## Per-role roll-up

**For the spec working group.** Seven Partials and two Nits against the v1.0-final-amendment spec as it stands today, all clustered around EU regulatory-architecture conformance overlays. ## Where I would prioritize

1. **EQ-2 (eIDAS QES posture) and EQ-3 (eIDAS QTS).** These are the substantive evidentiary questions for EU institutions. An EU bank's Legal department needs to know whether the chain is QES or AES under eIDAS before signing off on deployment; today the answer is "we have to figure it out ourselves." 2. **EQ-1 (AI Act Article 12 mapping).** Highest leverage of the conformance-overlay items — every EU institution deploying the chain for a high-risk AI system writes this document, and the substance is identical across institutions. An informative annex saves the duplicated effort and produces a consistent posture.

3. **EQ-6 (EU regulatory incident-notification timing matrix).** Operationally pressing — an EU institution facing an actual incident under DORA + NIS2 + GDPR composition needs the timing matrix immediately. A supplementary `incident-response-playbook.md` section closes it.

4. **EQ-5 (Multi-jurisdiction Pattern B mapping) and EQ-7 (EU cloud HSM + Schrems II).** Both are operational-architecture clarifications layered on existing §10.15 and §10.5 normative material. One paragraph each.

5. **EQ-4 (EBA Outsourcing flow-down)**, **EQ-8 (multilingual generalisation)**, **EQ-9 (DORA TLPT mapping).** Supplementary EU regulator-pack documents; lower immediate pressure but would round out the EU-conformance posture meaningfully.
