## Headline

The spec is US-FFIEC-centric. For a Japanese institution deploying the chain across US and Japanese supervisory perimeters, the spec **does not address Japanese regulatory expectations explicitly — not because they are incompatible with the chain's design, but because the spec was written from the US supervisory perspective.** Hiroshi's read identified 24 points where Japanese law, Japanese regulatory practice, and Japanese cultural/linguistic norms differ from the US/EU baseline the spec assumes.

**What the spec does well for Japanese institutions.** The chain's integrity-bearing, append-only, independently-verifiable design is compatible with Japanese regulatory frameworks. The FSA's 2024 AI Discussion Paper emphasizes governance, explainability, and human-in-the-loop — all supported by the chain's transparency and audit-trail properties. J-SOX Article 24-4-4 ICFR extends to AI decisions affecting financial reporting; the chain provides the integrity evidence J-SOX examiners will demand. APPI 2022 tokenization via the privacy-by-design.md approach is operationally sound. The incident-response playbook accommodates Japanese breach-notification timing (APPI Article 27, in some cases faster than GDPR). The spec's cryptographic primitives (HMAC-SHA-256, Ed25519, Merkle trees) align with CRYPTREC recommendations (all three are on the CRYPTREC E-list for financial-sector use).

**What the spec needs for Japanese institutions.** Explicit mapping of the chain to FSA AI Discussion Paper governance pillars; acknowledgment of J-SOX Article 24-4-4 ICFR scope for AI; guidance on APPI Article 24 cross-border-transfer restrictions when tokens flow to US Compliance systems; PIPC supervisory-expectation alignment (administrative orders, on-site inspection workflows); clarification on BoJ operational-risk classification (which Basel III operational-risk category does the chain address); character-handling guidance for Japanese tenant identifiers and multi-language audit output; FBO supervision acknowledgment (Federal Reserve + FSA dual examination); FATF AML-decision audit-trail expectations; and BCP/disaster-recovery considerations for earthquake-prone, highly-redundant Japanese deployments. Hiroshi does **not** file a Partial for most of these because the spec's design is sound; the gaps are informational and procedural, not design defects. ### Q-3. APPI 2022 Article 24 — cross-border transfer restriction when tokens flow to US-hosted Compliance system

APPI Article 24 restricts transfers of personal data to foreign jurisdictions to only those with "equivalent protection" status. EU has equivalent-protection status; US does not. When a Japanese institution sends chain tokens (which are pseudonymized personal data under APPI, because the privacy-store mapping is retained for 7 years) to a US-hosted Compliance system for customer-dispute handling, the transfer is restricted. The spec does not address this APPI-specific constraint. The institution must operate either (a) de-mapping before transfer (tokens become anonymized, outside APPI scope), (b) separate US-hosted systems without access to the privacy-store, or (c) EU-held privacy-store keys mediating access. **Ask:** Add to international-transfers.md or privacy-by-design.md a Japan-specific section: "**APPI Article 24 Cross-Border Transfer for Japanese Institutions** When a Japanese institution (controller under APPI) transfers chain tokens (pseudonymized data) to a non-adequate country (US, Singapore, etc.), APPI Article 24 requires equivalent-protection safeguards. Recommended postures: (1) **De-mapping before transfer (strongest)**: Japanese institution retains the privacy-store mapping on Japanese servers. US Compliance system receives only chain tokens, without reversal capability. (2) **Separate custody (medium)**: US-hosted privacy-store keys are held in a US-based HSM controlled by a Japanese subsidiary or parent company, blocking access from US-resident staff. (3) **Documented transfer agreement**: Japanese institution files a transfer-impact assessment with the PIPC naming the supplementary safeguards per APPI Article 24 guidance. The institution's control description documents which posture is adopted per tenant and data residency policy."

### Q-4. APPI 2022 sensitive personal data (yō-hairyo kojin jōhō) — broader than GDPR Article 9

APPI Article 2(3) defines sensitive personal data (yō-hairyo kojin jōhō) as race, creed, social status, medical history, criminal record, abuse history, and (in some administrative-guidance interpretations) sensitive financial information (credit history, account status). The category is broader than GDPR Article 9 and includes data types the GDPR does not specifically restrict. If the chain captures decision inputs including sensitive-category data (e.g., a wealth-management AI system routing high-net-worth customers; a medical-finance AI system processing healthcare-related lending), the entries fall under APPI sensitive-category restrictions, requiring heightened consent and handling. The spec's privacy-by-design.md applies tokenization generically; Japanese institutions must specialize it to APPI sensitive-category data. **Ask:** Add to privacy-by-design.md or create `07-appi-sensitive-data.md`: "**APPI Sensitive Personal Data (yō-hairyo kojin jōhō) Handling** When the chain captures APPI-defined sensitive categories (race, creed, social status, medical history, criminal record, abuse history, sensitive financial information as interpreted by APPI guidance), the institution applies **mandatory tokenization** to these fields before chain capture. The institution's privacy configuration (privacy-by-design.md §SDK configuration) explicitly lists which fields are APPI sensitive-category for the institution's business context; the configuration is approved by the institution's privacy officer and reviewed annually. For healthcare-related AI (medical-expense financing, health-insurance-guided lending), health data is APPI sensitive-category and MUST be tokenized. For wealth-management AI routing based on account tier, the account-tier information (which correlates with sensitive financial status) is tokenized. The institution's APPI-specific consent management ensures explicit consent is obtained for processing sensitive categories; the consent is recorded and the institution produces consent records on request to the PIPC."

The chain provides technical controls addressing BoJ's expectations: (1) **Detection**: the verifier detects tampering or integrity failures in real time; per incident-response-playbook.md, detected failures are escalated per the 36-hour notification rule (satisfying BoJ's expectation for prompt detection). ### Q-7. CRYPTREC algorithm recommendations — spec uses FIPS but should cite CRYPTREC

The spec commits to SHA-256 (HMAC-SHA-256) and Ed25519 for v1.0, with Dilithium for post-quantum (v1.x). Japanese institutions prefer to cite CRYPTREC rather than NIST FIPS when reporting to Japanese regulators, because CRYPTREC is Japan's authoritative standard-setting body for cryptography. The spec names FIPS/NIST exclusively; this forces Japanese institutions to reverse-map to CRYPTREC. **Ask:** Add to spec §10 (Implementation conformance) or crypto-security.md: "**Cryptographic Algorithm Recommendations Across Jurisdictions** The chain uses HMAC-SHA-256, Ed25519, and (future) Dilithium. These algorithms are: (1) NIST-approved (FIPS PUB 198-1 for HMAC, FIPS 186-5 for Ed25519), (2) CRYPTREC-E-listed (Japan's standard-setting body; CRYPTREC-R-E-010 for HMAC-SHA-256, CRYPTREC-R-E-001 for Ed25519 as of 2024), (3) ISO/IEC-standardized (ISO/IEC 9797-2 for HMAC, ISO/IEC 8949 for CBOR encoding compatible with Ed25519 deployments). ### Q-8. JIS X 0208 character handling — tenant identifiers with Japanese names

Spec §3 mandates tenant_id matching `^[A-Za-z0-9_.\-]{1,255}$` to ensure the HKDF info-parameter is unambiguously parseable. A Japanese institution wanting tenant identifiers like `三菱UFJ-本店` (MUFG-Tokyo-head-office, using kanji for the business-line name) cannot conform without transliteration. For domestic Japanese deployments or when institution-internal naming uses Japanese characters, this creates friction. **Ask:** Add to spec §3 or design/01-primitives-spec.md: "**Tenant Identifier Character Set — Multi-Language Support** The spec requires `^[A-Za-z0-9_.\-]{1,255}$` for cryptographic parsability. Institutions using characters outside this set (e.g., Japanese tenant identifiers with kanji, Chinese identifiers with hanzi, Cyrillic, Arabic) must transliterate the identifier for chain purposes and document the mapping. Recommended approach: (1) The institution defines the canonical tenant_id as UTF-8-safe and transliterates it to ASCII-only for the chain's info-parameter. Example: the internal identifier `三菱UFJ-本店` is transliterated to `mufg-tokyo-hq` for chain use; the institution maintains a mapping. (2) The institution's audit output (verifier reporting, customer-facing disclosures) can use the original non-ASCII identifier for readability; the chain's internal bytes remain ASCII. (3) Documentation: the institution's control description names the transliteration scheme (e.g., 'romaji per RFC 3492 punycode for domain names,' or 'hash-of-UTF8-identifier mapped to opaque ID') and confirms the mapping is retained for examination and dispute purposes. This does not change the chain's behavior or security; it is a naming-layer adaptation."

### Q-14. Cross-border discovery under MLAT — Japan-US Mutual Legal Assistance Treaty

When Japanese chain evidence is needed in a US court proceeding, or US chain evidence is needed in a Japanese administrative proceeding or criminal investigation, the parties typically invoke the Mutual Legal Assistance Treaty (MLAT) between Japan and the US. The MLAT has specific procedures for requesting evidence across borders, including restrictions on data use. APPI Article 24 cross-border restrictions may conflict with US court discovery obligations or MLAT request procedures. The spec does not address MLAT-assisted discovery. **Ask:** Add to docs/litigation-support.md or create a Japan-US MLAT addendum: "**MLAT-Assisted Discovery for Cross-Border Chain Evidence** When a US court requests chain evidence held by a Japanese institution (via MLAT), the request goes through the US Department of Justice (DoJ) to the Japanese Ministry of Justice (MoJ), which coordinates with the institution. Conversely, when a Japanese court or the FSA requests chain evidence from a US subsidiary, the request follows the reverse MLAT path. The institution's legal team coordinates with both regulators. Key points: (1) MLAT requests are typically slower than direct discovery (process: 6-12 months vs 30-day discovery deadlines in US civil procedure), so institutions should plan for parallel domestic discovery while the MLAT request is pending. (2) APPI Article 24 cross-border-transfer restrictions may require the requesting jurisdiction to provide assurances that the evidence will be used only for the stated purpose (litigation, regulatory investigation) and will not be retained indefinitely. (3) The institution's chain evidence (particularly privacy-store mappings if requested) may be delivered under protective order limiting use and confidentiality. Japan's FSA mandates BCP with active-active deployment across geographically separated data centers. Typical topology: Tokyo and Osaka, each with full redundancy and sub-10-minute failover. The chain accommodates this topology per design/02 §Multi-region deployment: each region has its own ledger, HSM, and seal-job infrastructure. Recommended Japanese topology: (1) **Primary region (Tokyo)**: ledger, HSM with master key, seal job, privacy-store, live SDK deployment. (2) **Secondary region (Osaka)**: standby ledger, failover HSM, standby seal job, replicated privacy-store, live SDK deployment with same configuration. (3) **Cross-region replication**: entries are replicated from Tokyo to Osaka in near-real-time; the Osaka ledger can take over seal operations within minutes if Tokyo is unavailable. (4) **Key rotation during failover**: If the Tokyo HSM is unavailable, the Osaka seal job activates and signs with the Osaka key. (5) **Recovery from earthquake damage**: If Tokyo facility is destroyed, the Osaka region becomes primary indefinitely. ### Q-21. Threat model alignment — Japanese nation-state adversary assumptions

The spec's threat model (design/09) classifies adversaries (Adversary I: insider, Adversary F: application compromise, Adversary R: hardware/cryptanalysis). The threat model does not discuss jurisdiction-specific adversary assumptions. Japanese institutions, when filing with the FSA, may be asked whether the threat model accounts for nation-state-level APT activity targeting Japanese financial institutions (a known category of APT activity per Japanese and US government threat-intelligence reports). The spec's threat model assumes commercial-grade adversaries; Japanese institutions must confirm the security properties hold against stated APT threat profiles. **Ask:** Add to design/09-threat-model.md or threat-model-japan-addendum: "**Japanese Threat Model Considerations — Nation-State APT Activity** Japanese financial institutions are targets of sustained nation-state APT activity, documented by NISC (Japan's National center of Incident readiness and Strategy for Cybersecurity), NPA (National Police Agency), and US CISA reports. The chain's threat model assumes the IKM (master key) is protected by FIPS 140-2 Level 3 HSM hardware tamper-detection and per-entry MAC integrity checks. Against nation-state APT adversaries, the chain provides the following guarantees: (1) **Detection of tampering at action time**: If an APT compromises the chain ledger and modifies entries, the tampering is detected at verification time (per spec §7 MAC verification). Long-dwell adversaries (6-18 months of undetected presence) do not produce detectable chain modifications unless they act. (2) **Assumption: HSM is tamper-resistant**: The chain assumes the FIPS 140-2 L3 HSM cannot be remotely compromised; physical access to the HSM is required for key extraction. This assumption is reasonable for Japanese tier-1 institutions operating FIPS L3 HSMs in secured data centers; the assumption may not hold if the HSM is operated remotely or with inadequate physical security. (3) **IKM custody is the critical security boundary**: The nation-state APT's objective is to extract the master key (IKM) and forge new chain entries. The chain's control is per-entry MAC integrity; if the adversary has the IKM, the control is bypassed. The institution's IKM custody procedures (per §10.6 HSM custody) and incident-response playbook Scenario 4 (master-key compromise) are the institution's defenses against this threat. The FSA's examination will focus on IKM custody procedures, not on the chain's cryptography. The institution's posture is: 'The chain detects tampering; the institution's IKM-custody controls prevent the adversary from obtaining the key to begin with.' Japanese institutions should expect FSA examiners to ask about the institution's IKM-custody procedures in detail, particularly around: HSM physical security (locked facility, access logging), personnel security (background checks for HSM operators), and incident response (immediate key rotation if compromise is suspected)."

### Q-22. Retention periods and multi-jurisdiction compliance — reconciling 7-year US norm with Japanese law

The spec defaults to 7-year retention, aligned with US FFIEC banking requirements (12 CFR 1002 ECOA records). Japan does not have a single financial-services retention standard; instead, APPI Article 5(1)(e) requires retention be limited to the period necessary for the purpose, and specific laws (FEFTA for AML records, FIEA for document retention) may impose different periods. A Japanese institution must reconcile US 7-year retention (required for the US subsidiary's Fed compliance) with Japanese retention requirements (which may be shorter or longer per the purpose). **Ask:** Add to ai-policy-alignment.md or international-transfers.md: "**Multi-Jurisdiction Retention Reconciliation for Japanese Institutions** The chain's default 7-year retention satisfies US FFIEC requirements (12 CFR 1002 ECOA records retention). For Japanese parent-company compliance: (1) **APPI Article 5(1)(e) necessity**: Retention must be limited to the time necessary for the stated purpose. For AI-decision audit trails, the purposes are: (a) customer disputes (APPI Article 26 breach notification, investigation), (b) fairness audits (FSA AI governance), (c) regulatory examination (FSA, BoJ, PIPC). The necessary retention period for (a) and (b) is typically 3-4 years (covering statute of limitations on customer claims, plus audit cycles). For (c), examination records may be retained 7 years per the examiner's requirements. (2) **Institution choice: APPI-shorter retention**: The Japanese institution may adopt a shorter retention period (e.g., 4 years) for APPI purposes, and extend to 7 years only for records that touch US subsidiary operations. This requires separate IKM retention per (tenant_id: US subsidiary, retention: 7 years) vs (tenant_id: Japan parent, retention: 4 years). (3) **Institution choice: uniform 7-year retention**: The institution may adopt 7-year retention uniformly, treating the US FFIEC requirement as the binding floor across both jurisdictions. This simplifies operations at the cost of exceeding Japanese APPI necessity (accepted if the institution documents the business rationale — e.g., 'retention period chosen for consistent compliance across all jurisdictions'). (4) **Documentation**: The institution's control description explicitly names the retention period per jurisdiction and the legal basis for the choice. Japanese examiners (FSA, PIPC) will verify the retention rationale; US examiners (Fed) will verify the 7-year floor. The institution's approach must be defensible under both frameworks."

Japan's three megabanks (MUFG, SMBC, Mizuho) operate at volumes, complexity, and regulatory intensity that are comparable to or exceed large US banks. The spec's reference implementation assumes cloud-native or x86 deployment; Japanese megabanks may need guidance on mainframe-specific implementation. **Ask:** Create an operator-guide addendum or deployment-patterns.md section: "**Japanese Megabank Deployment Patterns** Japanese tier-1 institutions operating nationwide networks face specific deployment constraints: (1) **Mainframe AI integration**: Some legacy AI systems run on mainframe (COBOL, PL/I) with outputs fed to downstream x86/cloud systems. ### Q-24. Post-quantum cryptography and algorithm migration — v1.1 forward commitment

The spec's v1.0 uses Ed25519 + HMAC-SHA-256. Post-quantum cryptography (Dilithium, etc.) is planned for v1.1 with dual-algorithm cosigned seals. For Japanese institutions with 7-year retention horizon, chain entries created in 2026 will be evidence in cases and regulatory reviews in 2033. The spec does not address Japanese institutions' quantum-readiness expectations; the FSA or BoJ may ask whether the institution has a quantum-migration plan. **Ask:** Add to design/09-threat-model.md or a forward-commitment section: "**Post-Quantum Cryptography Transition Planning** The chain uses Ed25519 (quantum-resistant under current assumptions, but not post-quantum-proof) for v1.0. v1.1 will introduce Dilithium (NIST-approved post-quantum signature algorithm) with dual-algorithm cosigned seals. Institutions deploying v1.0 with 7-year retention should plan for algorithm transition: (1) **Quantum risk window**: Current cryptanalytic consensus is that quantum computers capable of breaking Ed25519 are not anticipated before 2030-2035 (20+ years from the algorithm's first use). For v1.0 entries created 2026-2027, this window is comfortable. (2) **Regulatory expectations**: Japanese regulators (FSA, BoJ, NISC) will not require immediate post-quantum migration for v1.0 entries. However, institutions should monitor NIST's post-quantum standardization progress and plan migration to dual-algorithm seals (Dilithium + Ed25519) during the v1.0 → v1.1 transition. (3) **Institutional posture**: The institution documents a quantum-risk acceptance in the control description: 'We deploy Ed25519 v1.0; quantum computers are not anticipated to break Ed25519 within the 7-year retention horizon; if NIST updates algorithm recommendations during the retention period, we will re-evaluate and migrate per NIST guidance.' (4) **v1.1 adoption timeline**: When v1.1 is available (anticipated 2027-2028), the institution plans a migration window (3-6 months) to deploy v1.1 dual-algorithm seals for new entries and retroactively seal historical entries with the post-quantum algorithm. This is a planned operational procedure, not an emergency response."
