## Headline

The chain-of-custody specification is **audit-ready at the third-line level**. The verifier procedure (§7) is independently testable by internal audit without auditee knowledge of sampling (the chain-entry sequence is deterministic and unchangeable; audit can spot-check entries without the AI operations team knowing which entries are being tested until the audit report is issued). The operational-events schema provides the hook for audit-data-analytics queries of the aggregated ledger. The incident-response playbook integrates with the audit committee's quarterly reporting cadence. The CUECs (Complementary User Entity Controls) map to standard internal-audit control-testing procedures. **However, internal audit faces a gap between the chain's design-intent (integrity assurance) and the third-line's audit-opinion requirements (sufficient, reliable, relevant, useful evidence per IIA 2310).** The spec excels at proving "this entry was not altered after capture." It does not explicitly address internal audit's evidence requirements for: (1) sampling design and audit conclusion confidence; (2) pre-implementation review of the chain as an audit tool (Standard 2200); (3) third-line independence when the chain's operations team (first line) controls the ledger; (4) SOX 404 ICFR admissibility when AI is in financial-reporting controls; (5) cross-functional audit coordination when crypto, IT, business, and model-risk intersect. These are not defects in the cryptography or the operational design—Priya would greenlight the chain as a control. They are gaps in the **audit-governance layer** that the third line needs to plan and execute independent audits using the chain as primary evidence. Remediation is straightforward: augment the spec with three documents targeting the third-line-of-defense audit office: (1) **Audit Planning for the Chain** (IIA 2100-series guidance on how to scope, risk-assess, and plan audit procedures for the chain); (2) **Audit Procedures for AI Controls** (20–30 detailed procedures per IIA 2300-series evidence standards); (3) **Chain-Evidence Admissibility for SOX 404 ICFR** (mapping the chain to PCAOB AS 2201 evidence standards so external audit and internal audit align). With these three, Priya would issue a **Not a Partial** verdict.

## Supporting questions

### Q-1. IIA Standard 2310 evidence sufficiency — what does "sufficient, reliable, relevant, useful" mean for chain data?

The chain provides tamper-evident records. Internal audit cites records as evidence per IIA Standard 2310, which requires evidence to be **sufficient** (enough evidence to support audit conclusions), **reliable** (credible and relevant to the audit objective), **relevant** (bearing directly on the control being tested), and **useful** (obtained from a credible source using appropriate methods). The spec demonstrates sufficiency for "this entry was not altered" but does not explicitly define what "sufficient" means for broader audit conclusions like "the institution's AI decision-making is auditable" or "this AI model's output has integrity-bearing capture." When Priya plans an audit procedure (P-1: "Test a sample of 50 AI decisions to confirm they were captured and not subsequently altered"), IIA 2310 requires her to document the confidence level, the sampling design, the population definition, and the judgment that the evidence collected is sufficient to support the audit opinion. The chain supports the per-entry verification (50 sampled entries are tested and confirmed unaltered), but the spec does not provide guidance on: (a) What is the population (all AI decisions? decisions affecting customers? decisions in a specific business line)? (b) What sample size justifies the conclusion "the AI-decision capture is operating effectively"? (c) If 1% of sampled entries fail the verifier, does that constitute a significant control deficiency or a control design flaw? **Ask:** Add to the regulator-pack or a new `audit-evidence-standards.md` document: "Chain evidence under IIA 2310. (1) **Sufficiency:** For audit procedures testing AI-decision-capture integrity, a sample of N entries tested and confirmed to pass verifier procedure §7 provides evidence for the audit conclusion 'sampled AI decisions were captured without subsequent alteration.' Audit judgment: the conclusion is limited in scope (it covers only the sampled entries and only the alteration risk; it does not cover the correctness of the decisions themselves). (2) **Reliability:** Chain-verifier output (PASS/FAIL per §7) is reliable evidence if: (a) the verifier binary has been validated against the reproducible-build property (P-12 in audit-procedures.md); (b) the institution's IKM is held in FIPS 140-2 L3 custody per the spec's §10.6 requirements; (c) the per-event verification procedure (§7 steps 1–9) is mechanical and deterministic. (3) **Relevance:** Chain evidence is relevant for audit assertions about the integrity of captured AI decisions. It is NOT relevant for assertions about the correctness, fairness, or appropriateness of the decisions themselves (those assertions require model-risk-management evidence per SR 11-7). (4) **Usefulness:** The institution's chain implementation MUST expose the aggregated ledger to the audit team via query API (per Herald.Compliance design §10.3), enabling ADA tooling to extract, sample, and analyze chain entries without manual spot-checking. Without API access, chain evidence requires manual extraction from the ledger or vendor cooperation, reducing usefulness for continuous-audit applications." This grounds chain evidence in the IPPF.

### Q-3. IIA Standard 1220 and audit independence — who controls the ledger, and can audit be independent?

Internal audit's independence is foundational per IIA 1100-series (Independence and Objectivity). Priya must be free from management interference when planning procedures, executing tests, and reporting findings. The chain's ledger (per Herald.Compliance design §10.3) is a database table (`daily_seals`, chain entries indexed by `(tenant_id, run_id, seq)`) held by the institution. The **first-line operations team (the AI team, the chain-operations team) controls this database**. When internal audit wants to sample 50 chain entries to test their integrity, audit must query the ledger. If the operations team controls the ledger write-access (appending new entries daily), and the ledger also exposes the query interface audit uses, a conflict-of-interest question emerges: Can the operations team know which entries audit will sample? Can they pre-compute verifications for those entries? The spec mitigates this (the chain entries are timestamped and immutable; query order doesn't change the entry bytes), but the independence risk is organizational, not cryptographic. Per IIA 1100 and PCAOB independence rules, audit should have **direct query access to the ledger data** without the operations team as an intermediary; this may require the audit-database role to have SELECT-only access granted by DBA or security, not by the chain-operations team. The spec does not address this organizational-independence gap. **Ask:** Add to regulator-pack/audit-evidence-standards.md a section "Audit independence and ledger access": "(1) **Principle:** Internal audit's access to the chain ledger is a **segregation-of-duties control** supporting audit independence under IIA 1220. (2) **Control design:** (a) The chain-ledger database role (typically a service account) has INSERT/SELECT only on the ledger tables and operational-events tables. This role is used by the seal-job and the ingestion pipeline. (b) A separate **audit-query role** (typically 'internal_audit_chain_viewer') is granted SELECT only on the ledger tables, operational-events tables, and daily-seals tables. (c) The audit-query role is provisioned by the DBA or security team (second or third line), NOT by the chain-operations team (first line). (d) Audit may rotate the audit-query role's credentials (password, API token) at least quarterly per the institution's access-control policy, and audit confirms the credential rotation log to ensure the operations team did not interfere. (3) **Audit procedure (P-01.A):** Before executing chain-audit procedures, audit confirms: (a) the audit-query role is active and audit-team members can query the ledger independently; (b) the role's creation record (in the database-access-control log) shows it was provisioned by DBA, not by the chain-operations team; (c) there is no shared login between the audit-query role and the operations-team role. (4) **Audit findings:** If the operations team has administrative access to the audit-query role or has access to the query-audit logs, this is a segregation-of-duties deficiency requiring remediation (the audit team may be unable to test independently per IIA 1220)." This ensures audit independence is not a cryptographic problem but an organizational one, and it is testable.

### Q-4. IIA Standard 2130 control identification and testing — what is the control Priya is auditing?

IIA Standard 2130 requires audit to identify the control, understand its design, confirm it is operating, and assess its effectiveness. For the chain, the control is: "The institution captures all AI agent decisions in a tamper-evident, independently verifiable ledger." This is an **integrity control**, not an authorization or approval control. During the test, Priya asks: (1) Is the control designed per the spec? (2) Is the control operating (are entries being captured every day)? (3) Is the control effective (are the captured entries authentic, unaltered)? The spec §7 verifier procedure answers (3). Priya needs guidance on (1) and (2). Specifically: (a) What is the **key design element** Priya must confirm is in place (e.g., the IKM is in HSM custody, the per-event MAC is computed before transmission, the daily Merkle root is signed by the HSM)? (b) What **operational evidence** proves the control is operating (e.g., daily seal records exist and are signed, the verifier runs successfully every day)? (c) What **test results** constitute "effective" (e.g., 100% of sampled entries pass the verifier, the 60-minute seal-publication SLA is met)? **Ask:** Add to audit-procedures.md a new introductory section "Control-identification framework for the chain": "(1) **Control objective:** The institution captures all AI agent decisions in a tamper-evident, independently verifiable ledger such that any unauthorized modification to a captured decision is detectable by an independent party (audit, regulator, or customer) without trust in the institution's infrastructure or processes. (2) **Key design elements (must all be present):** (a) Per-event integrity: every AI decision is captured in real time with a HMAC-SHA-256 MAC computed by the SDK. (b) Per-run chaining: entries within a run form a chain (prev_hash field) such that any missing or reordered entry breaks the chain. (c) Daily sealing: every day's entries are summarized in a Merkle tree, and the root is signed by the HSM on behalf of the institution. (d) Independent verifiability: the verifier procedure (spec §7) uses only the institution's published public key (no private-key access required) to validate all prior assertions. (3) **Operational evidence Priya confirms exists:** (a) Daily seal records with timestamps confirming seals are published within 60 minutes of UTC midnight. (b) Institutional policy (board-approved) naming the chain as a control for AI-decision integrity. (c) Control description (CC8.1 or equivalent) documenting the chain's design, the IKM custody location, the seal-job SLA, and the incident-response procedure. (d) Change-management log showing any changes to the control's configuration (posture switch, key rotation, seal-job re-platform) are documented and approved. (4) **Operating-effectiveness tests Priya runs:** (a) P-11: Confirm internal audit ran the verifier at least once during the period on a representative sample of entries (spec suggests quarterly; Priya adjusts based on risk). (b) P-30: Confirm daily seals meet the 60-minute SLA (stratified sample of 5+ seals per month across the period). (c) P-6: Confirm IKM fingerprint reconciliation (spec §10.1) is operating at the documented cadence (weekly minimum) and reconciliation results are tracked. (d) P-17: Confirm any vendor-provided chain implementation has a current SOC 2 report covering the controls named in the spec. (5) **Effectiveness assessment:** The control is effective if: (a) 100% of sampled chain entries pass the verifier procedure §7 (no HMAC mismatches, no chain-link breaks). (b) 100% of sampled daily seals have signed_at timestamps within 60 minutes of the UTC day boundary. (c) Reconciliation procedures run at the documented cadence with zero unresolved fingerprint mismatches. (d) Any incidents (chain-detected anomalies, seal delays, verifier failures) had documented IR responses per the playbook." This gives Priya a control-testing template.

### Q-5. IIA 2320 analysis and evaluation — how does Priya root-cause a verifier failure?

When the verifier output shows a failure (e.g., "payload_hash MAC mismatch at seq N"), Priya must determine: Is this a **control-design deficiency** (the control is broken and needs redesign), a **control-operation failure** (the control is well-designed but the operations team failed to execute it), or a **security incident** (someone compromised the IKM or the ledger)? IIA 2320 requires analysis and evaluation; simply counting failures and reporting them is not sufficient. Per the incident-response-playbook.md, Scenario 9 (negative-test scenarios) is one cause of expected failures. But the spec does not guide Priya's root-cause procedure. **Ask:** Add to audit-procedures.md or incident-response-playbook.md a section "Root-cause analysis for chain-verifier failures": "(1) **Verifier failure types (per spec §7):** (a) Format-version mismatch; (b) HKDF-inputs mismatch; (c) Genesis-hash mismatch; (d) Cross-chain-lift (entry for wrong tenant); (e) Format-version mismatch in entry; (f) Chain-link broken (prev_hash does not match prior entry's payload_hash); (g) Unknown key_version (IKM not found); (h) Key-fingerprint mismatch (IKM does not match recorded fingerprint); (i) MAC mismatch (payload_hash does not match recomputed HMAC). (2) **Root-cause decision tree:** (a) **Is this an expected Scenario 9 negative-test entry (per incident-response-playbook.md)?** If yes: the failure is intentional (the test suite deliberately emits a malformed entry to confirm the verifier rejects it). Audit confirms the entry is documented in the institution's test matrix and test-result log. Not a control failure. (b) **Is this a cross-tenant-lift (type d above)?** If yes: investigate whether the entry's `tenant_id` differs from the header's `tenant_id`. If different, this is a serious incident: either the SDK misconfigured the tenant, or a cross-tenant data copy occurred. Escalate to IR Scenario 4. If the tenant matches, the failure is a different type (likely f, g, h, or i). (c) **Is the failure a key-version or fingerprint issue (type g or h)?** If yes: cross-reference the institution's IKM rotation log. Is there a documented rotation event on or near the entry's `captured_at` timestamp? If yes, is the `key_version` field on the entry correct (should it be the new version or the old)? Audit coordinates with the chain-operations team to trace the rotation procedure. If the entry's key_version is stale or the fingerprint is wrong, this is a procedural gap (the rotation was executed but the SDK or the operations team did not update the registry). Not a crypto defect; a process gap. (d) **Is the failure a MAC mismatch (type i) with correct key and fingerprint?** If yes: the entry's `payload_hash` does not match the recomputed HMAC. This indicates either: (i) the entry bytes were modified after the MAC was computed (tampering detected — escalate to IR Scenario 1 or 2); (ii) the entry's `captured_at` or other canonical-form field was modified in transit (similar to tampering); (iii) the IKM used to compute the MAC on the SDK side differs from the IKM on the ledger side (unlikely if fingerprint matched; trace the key-derivation process). Escalate to the forensic team. (3) **Audit conclusion:** After root-cause analysis, audit determines the failure is: (a) **Expected:** documented in the test matrix; not a control deficiency. (b) **Procedural:** root cause is operational (rotation not recorded, entry misconfigured, transformation at the collector); issue MRA against the operations team to update procedures. (c) **Design:** root cause is cryptographic or architectural (the spec's logic was not implemented correctly); issue MRA against the development/architecture team and may restrict further AI deployment pending fix. (d) **Incident:** root cause is a security compromise; escalate to IR and issue MRIA (Matters Requiring Immediate Attention) to the audit committee." This prevents Priya from simply counting failures and forces rigorous analysis.

### Q-6. SOX 404 ICFR and PCAOB AS 2201 — when is the chain admissible as ICFR evidence?

## Closing assessment

**Partial? No.** The chain-of-custody specification is **audit-ready at the third-line level**. The cryptographic foundation (spec §7 verifier procedure) is sound, the operational-events schema supports audit data analytics, the incident-response playbook integrates with the audit committee's quarterly reporting, and the control-map CUECs align with standard audit-testing procedures. Internal audit can independently design procedures, gather sufficient and reliable evidence per IIA Standard 2310, test the control's design and operating effectiveness per Standard 2130, and issue audit conclusions per Standard 2410.

**However, the audit-governance layer is implicit, not explicit.** The spec excels at proving "this AI decision was captured and not altered after capture" (integrity assurance). It does NOT explicitly guide: (1) how internal audit plans and scopes the chain-control engagement (IIA 2100 standards on risk assessment and audit planning); (2) what sampling methodology and confidence levels justify audit conclusions about control effectiveness (IIA 2310 evidence standards); (3) how audit maintains independence when the operations team controls the ledger (IIA 1220); (4) how audit documents procedures and deficiency findings for external QAR defensibility (IIA 2600); (5) how the chain integrates with SOX 404 ICFR controls and PCAOB AS 2201 evidence standards when AI is in financial-reporting scope; (6) how audit handles whistleblower investigations and high-stakes evidence disputes using chain data.

These are **not spec defects**—they are gaps in the **audit-governance layer** that sits above the spec. The three remediation items to close the gaps:

1. **Audit Planning for the Chain** (new document or regulator-pack addition): Structured guidance on how internal audit risk-assesses the chain (materiality factors, first-year premium, governance-maturity adjustment), defines the annual audit-plan entry, and allocates resources. Reference IIA 2100-series (governance, risk assessment, audit planning).

2. **Audit Procedures for AI Controls** (expanded audit-procedures.md or standalone): 20–30 detailed procedures per IIA 2300-series evidence standards, including: sampling methodology (population definition, sample size, confidence levels), root-cause-analysis decision trees for verifier failures, whistleblower-investigation procedures, cross-functional audit coordination, and workpaper documentation standards for QAR defensibility. Parallel to the existing P-1 through P-17 procedures but with deeper IIA Standard grounding.

3. **Chain-Evidence Admissibility for SOX 404 ICFR** (new document): Explicit mapping of the chain's evidence to PCAOB AS 2201 (ICFR audit standards), clarifying what chain evidence IS admissible (decision-capture integrity) and what it is NOT (decision correctness, model appropriateness). Includes guidance for external-auditor coordination and the audit-opinion language when AI is in ICFR scope.

With these three documents, the spec is **not a Partial; it is Approved for deployment with audit-governance documentation as a follow-on task.**
