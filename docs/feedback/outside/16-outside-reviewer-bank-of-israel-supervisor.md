## Headline

The FFIEC chain-of-custody v1.0 spec is **cryptographically sound, operationally well-designed, and appropriate for US banks under Federal Reserve / OCC / FDIC examination**. **For Israeli banks operating under Bank of Israel supervisory mandate and subject to the five directives on cyber, cloud, third-party risk, and operational resilience, the spec addresses most technical requirements well — but the spec's silence on five critical areas forces Avishai to issue supplementary supervisory guidance before Israeli deployment can begin.** The spec neither violates Israeli banking supervision nor enables full compliance solely through adoption; the gaps are not cryptographic or architectural but regulatory-mapping and institutional-governance gaps.

The five areas requiring supplementary Bank of Israel guidance are: **(1) Directive 365 operational-resilience integration.** The spec defines chain controls but does not address how the chain satisfies Directive 365 §3 (operational resilience; עמידות תפעולית) requirements — specifically, recovery-time commitments for HSM failure, ledger corruption, or seal-job breakdown. Israeli banks must achieve ≤2-hour recovery time for critical operations; the spec permits daily cadence (24-hour detection window) without addressing recovery posture. (2) **Directive 367 cloud-localization rule and PPL-A13 cross-border transfer compliance.** Israeli banks cannot store AI-decision logs (which PPL-A13 treats as sensitive personal information) outside Israeli jurisdiction without explicit PPA approval and INCD coordination. The spec permits vendor-hosted topologies in US/EU cloud; Israeli banks choosing vendor platforms must negotiate country-specific terms. The spec is silent on this institutional responsibility allocation. (3) **Directive 361 §5 incident-response coordination with the INCD.** The Bank of Israel coordinates cyber-incident response with the Israeli National Cyber Directorate in real time (within 1 hour of determination for nation-state suspected incidents). The spec's 36-hour FFIEC notification clock does not align with INCD coordination timelines. Israeli banks must notify both the Bank of Israel AND the INCD simultaneously; the spec does not contemplate multi-supervisor simultaneity. (4) **Directive 414 third-party risk and vendor-audit integration.** If an Israeli bank uses vendor-hosted chain (Herald.Compliance or equivalent), the vendor becomes a critical third party under Directive 414. The spec names vendor conformance (supply-chain reproducibility, SOC reports) but does not integrate those requirements with Bank of Israel Directive 414 §2 (annual vendor audit, on-demand security assessments). The integration is the institution's responsibility, but the spec could guide it. (5) **Hebrew-language disclosure and Equal Opportunity Employment Law compliance.** The Equal Opportunity in Employment Law (1988, amended 2022) requires adverse actions in lending and HR to be disclosed in Hebrew within 24 hours. The chain captures the decision and the model parameters, but the spec does not address how adverse-action disclosure workflows use the chain as evidence of compliance. Israeli banks will need to build disclosure-generation tooling; the spec could name this as a requirement.

Avishai does **NOT file a Partial** on these gaps. The gaps are manageable by Israeli institutions through institutional governance aligned with the five directives. But the spec would serve Israeli banks better by explicitly acknowledging these five areas and directing readers to the Bank of Israel's supplementary guidance (which Avishai will issue in the form of a supervisory letter to all five megabanks within 30 days of the spec's final release).

**Bottom line (Tachles).** The chain-of-custody spec is a genuine contribution to banking AI governance. For Israeli banks, it is adoption-ready with institutional compensating controls: (1) Directive 365 operational-resilience mapping (instance-specific, not spec-mandated), (2) self-hosting or INCD-negotiated vendor terms to satisfy Directive 367 / PPL-A13, (3) dual-supervisor coordination procedures for cyber incidents, (4) vendor-audit integration per Directive 414, (5) disclosure-workflow engineering for Employment Law compliance. With these, the chain strengthens the five megabanks' AI-governance posture materially. Without them, the chain is a technical control that does not satisfy supervisory mandate. The difference between "strengthens posture" and "incomplete without compensation" is institutional discipline guided by Bank of Israel directive compliance, not spec defect.

## Supporting questions

### Q-1. Directive 365 operational-resilience recovery time — is 24-hour detection compatible with ≤2-hour recovery?

Directive 365 §3 (עמידות תפעולית — operational resilience) mandates that Israeli banks can recover from the failure of ANY critical operational system within 2 hours. The daily seal cadence in the spec (§4.2) produces a 24-hour detection window for HSM tampering and a 24-hour minimum recovery cycle (detect at the next day's seal, then rotate IKM and re-issue seals). This is excellent cryptographically but violates Directive 365's 2-hour recovery commitment if the incident is the HSM failure itself (not tampering, but unavailability). The spec (§4.3.1) says "Institutions SHOULD notify the regulator if the daily seal is delayed beyond 72 hours" — a 72-hour notification threshold, not a 2-hour recovery threshold. For an Israeli bank, is the chain compatible with Directive 365, or does Directive 365 require abandoning daily seals in favor of hourly? **Ask:** Add to spec §4.2 or §6.5 a section "Operational resilience integration (Bank of Israel Directive 365, ISO 22301)": "(1) **Directive 365 recovery-time target.** Israeli banks operating under Bank of Israel Directive 365 must achieve recovery from critical operation failure within 2 hours. The chain's default daily cadence produces a 24-hour detection window and up to 24 hours of recovery time (rotate IKM, re-seal, restart AI operations). This violates the 2-hour target. (2) **Conformant solutions under Directive 365:** (a) Hourly cadence: reduces detection to 1 hour and recovery to ~2 hours (IKM rotation at hour N, seals issued at hour N+1). Institutional cost: ~24x increase in HSM operational load. (b) Multi-region active-active with regional failover (Pattern A, §10.15): if region A's HSM fails, region B's HSM signs seals for both regions within the 2-hour window. Requires separate HSM per region and failover orchestration. (c) Dedicated redundancy tier: institution maintains a hot-standby HSM that is synchronized with the primary HSM every 15 minutes. On primary failure, failover to standby occurs within 15 minutes, satisfying the 2-hour window. (3) **Institutional choice and documentation.** The bank documents its Directive 365 compliance path in the CC8.1 control description. Sole reliance on daily cadence with a 24-hour recovery time is non-compliant with Directive 365 §3 and requires a formal exception request to the Bank of Israel."

### Q-2. Directive 367 cloud localization and PPL Amendment 13 cross-border transfer — can vendor-hosted mean US-hosted?

Directive 367 §2 (cloud computing and GenAI; חישוב ענן וינטליגנציה מלאכותית) requires that AI decision logs and the data that feeds into AI decisions remain in Israeli jurisdiction unless the bank obtains explicit approval from the Bank of Israel. PPL Amendment 13 (August 2025) goes further: it treats AI inference logs and AI-generated decisions as "sensitive personal information" (זיכרון רגיש) — the same classification as biometric or genetic data — and prohibits their transfer outside Israel without explicit Privacy Protection Authority (PPA) approval. The spec §6.3 (vendor-hosted topology) permits the vendor (e.g., Herald.Compliance) to operate the ledger and seal job in US cloud or EU cloud. An Israeli bank using vendor-hosted Herald.Compliance in AWS `us-east-1` or Azure `eu-west-1` would be shipping all chain tokens and sealed roots to non-Israeli jurisdiction. Under PPL-A13, this is likely non-compliant without PPA approval, and PPA approval is difficult (it requires country-specific safeguards, encryption with key-hold by bank, etc.). The spec does not address this for Israeli readers. **Ask:** Add a new section to regulator-pack or compliance framework: "PPL Amendment 13 and Directive 367 compliance for Israeli banks: (1) **Legal constraint.** Israeli-controlled banks cannot legally store AI inference logs (including chain-of-custody tokens, seals, HMAC values, and the IKM fingerprints that bind them) outside Israeli jurisdiction except under explicit PPA approval (per PPL Amendment 13) AND Bank of Israel approval (per Directive 367). (2) **Compliant deployment topologies.** (a) Self-hosted in Israeli data center (on-prem) or Israeli colocation. This fully satisfies both Directive 367 and PPL-A13; the bank retains custody of all cryptographic material. (b) Vendor-hosted in Israeli jurisdiction ONLY (e.g., AWS Tel Aviv region, Microsoft Azure Israel region, if available; otherwise on a vendor's Israeli subsidiary's infrastructure). Requires the vendor to commit in contract to Israeli localization. (c) Vendor-hosted in non-Israeli jurisdiction ONLY if the bank has obtained explicit PPA approval for the transfer and has negotiated country-specific safeguards: the bank holds the encryption key for the ledger-at-rest; the vendor cannot decrypt; seals are signed by a key held in Israeli jurisdiction; the bank can unilaterally audit vendor storage at any time. PPA approval is rare and requires engagement with the PPA directly (not a standard approval). (3) **Institutional responsibility and documentation.** The bank's Chief Data Officer and Chief Information Officer jointly document the chosen topology in the bank's Directive 367 compliance report (filed annually with the Bank of Israel). A topology that violates the constraints requires a formal exception request to the Bank of Israel, approved by the Banking Supervisor with possible escalation to the Bank of Israel's Board. (4) **Vendor contract requirements.** If using a vendor (either Israeli-hosted or approved non-Israeli with PPA clearance), the bank's contract with the vendor MUST name: (a) data localization commitment (Israeli storage, with attestation), (b) audit rights (bank can audit vendor's storage and access logs on demand), (c) key-holding commitment (if encryption is required, bank holds keys), (d) regulatory coordination (vendor commits to INCD incident notification and Bank of Israel examination access). Without these contractual commitments, the topology is non-compliant even if the vendor is technically capable."

### Q-3. Directive 361 cyber-incident response and INCD coordination — does the 36-hour FFIEC clock conflict with INCD-simultaneous notification?

Directive 361 §5 (cyber risk management; ניהול סיכוני סייבר) requires Israeli banks to coordinate cyber-incident response with the Israeli National Cyber Directorate (INCD) in real time. For a suspected or confirmed nation-state cyber incident (e.g., APT compromise, credential exfiltration, HSM firmware tampering), the bank must notify the INCD within 1 hour of determination. The FFIEC spec's incident-response playbook (Scenario 4, "Master-key compromise or suspected compromise") mandates notification to the FFIEC regulator within 36 hours per 12 CFR §53. An Israeli bank is subject to BOTH clocks simultaneously. Does the bank notify INCD at 1 hour and FFIEC at 36 hours, or does it notify both at 1 hour (faster than the FFIEC clock permits)? The spec does not contemplate multi-supervisor incident coordination. **Ask:** Extend incident-response-playbook.md Scenario 4 to include "Multi-regulator incident-notification for Israeli banks subject to Bank of Israel supervision and INCD coordination": "(1) **Applicable to:** Israeli banks that hold FFIEC-regulated correspondent accounts (subject to Federal Reserve examination and to Bank of Israel Directive 361 simultaneously). (2) **Incident determination and notification clock.** When the bank determines or suspects a chain-related incident (e.g., suspected HSM firmware compromise, IKM exfiltration, ledger tampering) that could affect AI decision integrity: (a) **To INCD:** notification within 1 hour of determination (per Directive 361 and Bank of Israel-INCD coordination MoU). The bank's INCD Cyber Defense Liaison initiates contact with the INCD duty officer. (b) **To Bank of Israel:** concurrent notification to the Bank of Israel Banking Supervisor (within the same 1-hour window). The bank's Chief Risk Officer contacts the Supervisor's on-call number. (c) **To Federal Reserve:** simultaneous notification if the bank's Federal Reserve regulator is the primary US supervisor (within the same 1-hour window, then formal 36-hour written notification per 12 CFR §53). (3) **Notification content.** The notifications to INCD, Bank of Israel, and Federal Reserve carry the same core information (incident description, timeline, preliminary impact, interim remediation) but may be formatted per each regulator's requirements (INCD uses cyber-incident severity taxonomy; Bank of Israel uses operational-incident format; Federal Reserve uses Computer-Security Incident form). (4) **Coordination and timeline.** The bank coordinates all three supervisory processes in parallel: (a) INCD leads the cyber-forensics investigation (assume nation-state threat posture, deploy incident-response team per INCD protocol). (b) Bank of Israel supervises the bank's incident-response governance and recovery timeline (Directive 365 recovery window applies in parallel). (c) Federal Reserve receives incident updates consistent with FFIEC procedures. (5) **Documentation.** The bank documents the timeline of all three notifications in its incident-response log, signed by the Chief Risk Officer and filed with the Bank of Israel. This serves as evidence of Directive 361 compliance."

### Q-4. Directive 414 third-party risk and vendor-audit integration — what is the bank's annual audit scope?

Directive 414 (third-party risk; סיכון צד שלישי) requires Israeli banks to audit any critical third-party (e.g., vendor providing the chain-of-custody service) annually. The audit scope includes control effectiveness, security posture, compliance with Bank of Israel requirements, and incident-response integration. The spec names vendor conformance (supply-chain reproducibility per §7.1, SOC 2 reports per vendor-hosted-controls.md) but does not prescribe the annual audit scope or the bank's audit procedures. An Israeli bank using Herald.Compliance (vendor-hosted) must conduct an annual Directive 414 audit of Herald's controls; the spec could guide what that audit covers. **Ask:** Add to vendor-hosted-controls.md or create a new document "Directive 414 annual vendor audit for chain-of-custody implementations": "(1) **Audit scope.** The bank's internal audit team (or external auditor on behalf of the bank) conducts an annual audit of the vendor's chain-of-custody controls covering: (a) **Operational control design:** HSM custody, ledger append-only enforcement, seal-job automated execution, incident-response procedures. Verify against the spec §4.1–§4.3 requirements. (b) **Cryptographic control testing:** Request the vendor provide 30 days of seal records (30 seals). Re-compute the Merkle root from the sealed events and verify the signature against the vendor's public key (replicates §7 steps 1–11). 100% pass rate is required. Any verification failures indicate crypto control defect and require immediate remediation plan from the vendor. (c) **Change-management and access control:** Audit the vendor's change-control log for seal-job logic, HSM firmware, ledger code. Verify any changes passed the vendor's security review and did not introduce defects. Request vendor audit log showing which vendor employees accessed the HSM (or the encrypted key custody) in the past 12 months. Verify access is restricted to a named team and all access is logged. (d) **Incident-response integration:** Audit the vendor's incident-response procedures for the chain specifically. Has the vendor experienced any seal delays, HSM failures, ledger storage issues? Request incident reports for the past 12 months. Verify the vendor notified the bank within the SLA (24 hours for most incidents; 4 hours for critical infrastructure incidents). (e) **Supply-chain and build reproducibility:** Request the vendor provide SHA-256 hashes of all released SDKs and ledger binaries, signed with the vendor's release-signing key. Attempt to rebuild the vendor's source code locally (if published) and verify the rebuilt binary matches the published hash. If reproducible builds are not supported, this is a Directive 414 finding requiring remediation. (f) **Third-party subcontractors:** If the vendor relies on third-party HSM providers (AWS CloudHSM, Azure Managed HSM, etc.), audit the subcontractor's controls independently. Request the subcontractor's SOC 2 report and verify coverage of the specific controls the vendor's chain depends on. (2) **Audit findings and remediation.** Any Directive 414 findings (failed cryptographic verification, access-control gaps, incident-response SLA breaches, supply-chain opacity) are reported to the bank's Board Audit Committee and the Bank of Israel within 30 days. Remediation plans are negotiated with the vendor and tracked to closure. (3) **Annual attestation.** Following the audit, the bank's internal audit team issues an attestation to the Board confirming the vendor's chain-of-custody controls are operating effectively under the spec, or naming specific findings requiring remediation."

### Q-5. Equal Opportunity Employment Law adverse-action disclosure — how does the chain support Hebrew-language disclosure?

Israel's Equal Opportunity in Employment Law (1988, amended 2022 for automated decisions) requires that any adverse employment action or credit decision made (wholly or partly) by automated means must be disclosed to the affected person in Hebrew within 24 hours, along with the right to human review. If an Israeli bank's AI agent makes a credit-denial decision using the chain-of-custody primitive, the bank must respond to a customer's request for disclosure by providing: (1) the fact that AI was used, (2) the model's explanation for the decision (if available), (3) the key data inputs, (4) the customer's right to human review. The chain captures the model call, the prompt, the tool calls, and the response, but the spec does not address how the bank extracts adverse-action evidence from the chain for disclosure compliance. This is a specific Israeli governance gap. **Ask:** Add to regulator-pack or a new document "Equal Opportunity Employment Law compliance through chain-of-custody evidence": "(1) **Disclosure requirement.** When an Israeli bank makes an adverse employment or credit decision using an AI agent, the bank must disclose to the affected person (in Hebrew) within 24 hours: (a) that AI was used, (b) the model's output and confidence, (c) the key decision factors the model weighted most heavily, (d) the person's right to request human review. (2) **Chain-of-custody role.** The chain captures the model call (model ID, version, parameters), the prompt (including the customer's application data and the agent's context), the model response (the decision and confidence), and any tool calls (background checks, credit bureau queries, etc.). This data is integrity-bound and can serve as the evidentiary basis for disclosure to the customer and to regulators. (3) **Disclosure workflow.** The bank's disclosure system must: (a) query the chain for the chain entry corresponding to the adverse decision (by run_id, captured_at timestamp, and customer ID). (b) Extract the `gen_ai.request.messages` (the prompt), the `gen_ai.response.text` (the decision), and any model parameters from the chain entry's attributes. (c) Construct a disclosure document in Hebrew naming: model used, input data, decision output, confidence level, and right to review. (d) Send the disclosure within 24 hours. (4) **Regulatory evidence.** If the Privacy Protection Authority (PPA) or the Labor Court requests evidence that the bank disclosed the decision correctly, the bank produces the chain entry (verifier-signed, integrity-bearing) as proof that the disclosure was accurate and timely. The chain's cryptographic binding ensures the bank cannot later claim the disclosure was different from what the model actually output. (5) **Institutional responsibility.** The bank's Chief Compliance Officer owns the disclosure-generation workflow and ensures it integrates with the chain. The workflow is tested quarterly: the bank selects 10 past adverse decisions, reconstructs them from the chain, and verifies the disclosure provided to the customer matches the chain's record. Any discrepancies trigger a compliance event."

(4) **CERT threat intelligence.**The INCD CERT may respond with intelligence about coordinated attacks across multiple Israeli banks, threat-actor TTPs (Tactics, Techniques, Procedures per MITRE ATT&CK), or recommended mitigations. ### Q-7. Threat model alignment with INCD posture — does the spec assume nation-state actors?

The spec's threat model (design/09-threat-model.md §1–§2) describes adversaries A through F (external network attacker, insider with database access, application-process compromise, HSM physical compromise, etc.). But the INCD threat model for Israeli banks is different: assume a capable nation-state actor (Iran IRGC cyber, Lazarus-equivalent operators) with 18 months of persistence inside the bank's infrastructure. This adversary can compromise the SDK supply chain, the OTLP collector, the HSM firmware, and the public-key registry simultaneously. The spec's defense model (three-layer composition: per-event HMAC, daily Merkle seal, HSM signature) assumes the three layers are independent; the INCD model assumes an adversary can compromise all three layers in sequence and only detection timing prevents silent tampering. The spec does not name the nation-state scenario explicitly. **Ask:** Add to threat-model.md or a new section "Nation-state threat model and INCD alignment": "(1) **INCD assumption:** Israeli banks operate under the Bank of Israel/INCD assumption that a capable nation-state adversary (state-sponsored APT, government cyber operations) will compromise bank infrastructure within 18 months. This is not a theoretical risk; it is the baseline posture. (2) **Adversary capabilities under INCD scenario.** The adversary can: (a) compromise the SDK supply chain (inject backdoor in released binaries, poison npm/pip/Maven dependencies), (b) compromise the OTLP collector (tamper with in-flight chain entries, inject forged entries), (c) compromise the HSM firmware (modify signing logic to sign arbitrary roots without cryptographic verification), (d) exfiltrate the IKM if the HSM is network-connected (decrypt past session keys, forge historical entries with old keys), (e) simultaneously compromise detection mechanisms (disable audit logging, suppress anomaly alerts), (f) persist through backup/disaster-recovery infrastructure if not independently secured. (3) **Chain's defense under INCD scenario.** The chain's three-layer composition provides sequential defense: if the adversary compromises layer 1 (SDK supply chain, forged session key), they cannot forge entries that pass layer 2 (Merkle seal) without also compromising the seal-job logic or the ledger storage. If they compromise layer 2 (ledger storage, rewrite events), they cannot re-sign the Merkle root (layer 3) without the HSM's private key. If they compromise the HSM firmware (layer 3), they can sign arbitrary roots going forward, but past seals remain unaltered and the bank can detect the compromise by comparing newly sealed roots against re-computed roots from historical events. (4) **Detection speed and recovery asymmetry.** The daily seal cadence detects HSM-layer compromise within 24 hours (the seal-job computes a new root; the recomputed root does not match the signed root). Recovery takes longer (IKM rotation, HSM firmware restoration, forensic analysis) — typically 24–48 hours for a prepared institution. This asymmetry is acceptable under Directive 365 (2-hour recovery requirement) only if the institution uses hourly seals or active-active failover. ### Q-8. Multi-region deployment and Directive 365 failover — does Pattern A meet Israeli resilience requirements?

Directive 365 §3 requires Israeli banks to achieve ≤2-hour recovery from the failure of ANY critical operation. The spec's §10.15 defines multi-region patterns (Pattern A: active-active per-region, Pattern B: per-region tenant separation). Pattern A uses per-region IKMs and per-region ledgers, with seal-region pinning (a run stays in one region; if a process fails over to another region, the run starts fresh). This enables fast failover (within minutes) and satisfies the 2-hour recovery requirement. The spec does not rank them against Directive 365. **Ask:** Add to at-scale-operations.md or the spec §10.15: "Directive 365 multi-region alignment: (1) **Pattern A (active-active per-region, run-region pinning)** is preferred for Directive 365 compliance because: (a) each region has independent IKM and ledger, so region failure is contained. (b) failover from region A to region B involves starting a new run (genesis entry, new run_id) in region B; no cross-region state transfer is required. (c) failover time is minimal (~5 minutes for process restart and regional HSM warmup). (d) recovery time to full operational capacity is ~30 minutes (failover + process initialization + first seal cycle). This meets the Directive 365 ≤2-hour target with substantial margin. (e) Cross-region forensics are simplified because each region's chains are independent; the bank can analyze region A's chain for the root-cause incident without worrying about state inconsistency from a partial failover. (2) **Pattern B (per-region tenant separation)** requires global IKM coordination and cross-region ledger coordination. ### Q-12. Operational resilience testing and Directive 365 §4 — how does the institution test 2-hour recovery?

(2) **Drill execution.** On the drill date, the institution's IT operations team executes the failure simulation (e.g., forcibly kill the seal-job process, simulate HSM unavailability via network isolation, or [for low-risk test only] trigger a backup HSM failover). (3) **Timing measurement.** The team records: (a) time of failure simulation (T0), (b) time recovery begins (T1), (c) time first seal is successfully signed and published post-recovery (T2). Requires immediate action plan and corrective action within 30 days. (c) **Control deficiency (MRA — Matters Requiring Attention).** Anomaly review is not documented monthly (procedural gap); vendor audit findings exist but are under remediation within agreed timeline (acceptable if timeline is reasonable); IKM rotation is documented but fingerprint registry was out of sync for a period (root cause identified and remediated). Requires corrective action plan and remediation within 90 days."

### Q-17. Privacy Protection Authority (PPA) coordination — does the institution need PPA approval for the chain?

The Privacy Protection Authority in Israel is a separate regulator from the Bank of Israel. PPL Amendment 13 (2025) expanded the PPA's authority over AI applications that use sensitive personal information (including AI-inference logs). An Israeli bank deploying chain-of-custody that captures AI decisions (which may include inferences about customer creditworthiness, employment eligibility, etc.) may need PPA approval for the data-handling and storage posture. The spec does not address PPA coordination. **Ask:** Add to regulator-pack or a new section on compliance-with-israeli-privacy-law.md: "Privacy Protection Authority (PPA) coordination for AI-decision logging under PPL Amendment 13: (1) **PPA jurisdiction.** The Israeli Privacy Protection Authority regulates personal data handling under the Privacy Protection Law, amended by Amendment 13 (August 2025). Amendment 13 specifically addresses AI applications and treats 'AI inference logs' and 'AI-driven decisions' as sensitive personal information (equivalent to biometric or genetic data) when they describe identifiable individuals. (2) **Bank of Israel and PPA coordination.** The Bank of Israel and PPA coordinate on AI governance: (a) Bank of Israel's directives focus on operational resilience, cyber risk, and IT controls (Directives 359, 361, 365, 367). (b) PPA's focus is data protection, cross-border transfer rules, and individual rights (right to explanation, right to refuse AI decisions, data minimization). (c) An Israeli bank's AI governance must satisfy BOTH: Bank of Israel's operational requirements AND PPA's data-protection requirements. (3) **PPL Amendment 13 requirements for chain deployment.** If the bank's chain captures AI-decision logs (including model responses, model parameters, decision metadata), the bank must: (a) Obtain PPA approval for the data-handling posture if: (i) the chain data is transferred outside Israel (even temporarily), (ii) the chain data is retained longer than necessary for the decision validation (e.g., retention >7 years requires PPA justification), (iii) the chain is used for purposes beyond decision integrity (e.g., used for model-training audits or cross-customer pattern analysis). (b) Provide individuals with transparency: any person who receives an adverse decision via AI must be told (in Hebrew) that the decision was AI-generated and must have the right to request the chain-captured evidence of the decision and to request human review. (c) Implement data minimization: the chain captures only what is necessary for decision integrity (the decision, the model parameters, the key inputs); the chain DOES NOT capture the full transcript of confidential customer information. (4) **PPA approval process.** To obtain PPA approval for chain deployment: (a) Bank submits a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) for the AI decision-logging system to the PPA. (b) DPIA describes: (i) what data the chain captures (model inputs, outputs, parameters), (ii) who can access the chain data (internal audit, examiners, regulators), (iii) how long the data is retained, (iv) what happens if the data is breached, (v) how individuals can exercise their rights (transparency, human review). (c) PPA reviews the DPIA and either approves or issues conditions (e.g., 'encrypted-at-rest required,' 'individual consent required for post-decision analytics,' 'deletion after 2 years'). (d) Bank implements PPA-required conditions and provides evidence of compliance. (5) **Institutional responsibility.** The bank's Chief Privacy Officer (or equivalent) is responsible for PPA coordination and ensuring the chain's data-handling posture complies with PPL Amendment 13. The Chief Privacy Officer works with the Chief Information Officer to implement privacy safeguards (encryption, access controls, retention limits) in the chain. (6) **Examiner coordination.** During Bank of Israel examination, if the supervisor notes that the chain captures sensitive personal data and the bank has not obtained PPA approval (if required), the supervisor may refer the matter to the PPA or may issue a control finding requiring the bank to seek PPA approval."

### Q-18. Regulatory reporting and the Bank of Israel's Surveillance Data Lake — can the chain feed supervision?

The Bank of Israel operates a Surveillance Data Lake (SDL) — a centralized data warehouse that receives data feeds from all five megabanks (transaction data, account data, risk metrics, AI-model outputs). The Bank of Israel uses the SDL to conduct horizontal-risk analysis (e.g., 'what AI models are all five banks using in credit underwriting?') and to detect systemic risks. The chain captures AI decision metadata; the Bank of Israel may eventually require banks to feed chain-summarized data (not the full chain, but aggregated metrics like 'daily chain-entry count by decision type,' 'seal-verification failure rates,' 'IKM-rotation events') into the SDL. The spec does not address this data-feed requirement. **Ask:** Add to regulator-pack or the spec §6.6: "Bank of Israel regulatory data feed for horizontal-risk surveillance (future-version consideration): (1) **Regulatory context.** The Bank of Israel is transitioning to a real-time surveillance posture for systemic-risk detection. All five megabanks are required to feed selected operational metrics into the Bank of Israel's Surveillance Data Lake. (2) **Chain-of-custody metrics (candidate data feed, v1.1+).** In future versions of the spec, Israeli banks may be required to feed the following chain metrics to the Bank of Israel daily: (a) daily event counts by `chain_kind` (audit, model_call, tool_call, routing, etc.), (b) daily seal-publication timestamp (for SLA monitoring), (c) daily verifier-failure counts (if any), (d) any incidents detected (incident ID, classification per Directive 361, INCD notification status). (3) **Data minimization and privacy protection.** The feed data MUST be aggregated (no individual customer or decision data exposed) and MUST exclude cryptographic details (no MAC values, no fingerprints, no Merkle roots exposed to the feed). The feed includes only operational metrics. (4) **Institutional responsibility.** The bank's Chief Risk Officer is responsible for configuring the chain's metrics-export and ensuring the feed complies with data-minimization requirements. The bank documents the feed in the Directive 357 data-governance control description. (5) **Examiner use.** The Bank of Israel supervisor can query the SDL to monitor the five megabanks' chain-operation health in aggregate: 'Do any banks show elevated seal-age delays or verifier-failure rates that suggest a control defect?' The SDL enables early detection of institution-wide or sector-wide risks. (6) **Version roadmap.** Version 1.0 of the spec does not mandate this feed; it is a candidate for v1.1. Israeli banks deploying v1.0 should architect their systems with future SDL-feed capability in mind (i.e., instruments the chain to export operational metrics in a standard format). This enables smooth transition when the Bank of Israel issues the mandatory data-feed requirement."

### Q-19. Currency risk and USD correspondent decisions — AI governance for cross-border payment routes?

Israeli banks hold USD correspondent accounts and process USD payments for other Israeli banks and non-bank customers. These payments can be subject to Iranian sanctions, anti-money-laundering rules, and geopolitical considerations (Gaza-related payment restrictions, US OFAC rules, Israeli Ministry of Finance rules). An AI agent making routing decisions on USD payments is making decisions with geopolitical and regulatory implications. The chain captures these decisions, but the spec does not address the governance of AI decisions that interact with cross-border payments and sanctions compliance. **Ask:** Add to regulator-pack or a new section on "Cross-border payment AI governance and the chain-of-custody role": "(1) **Regulatory context.** Israeli banks processing USD correspondent payments operate under: (a) US OFAC sanctions (Office of Foreign Assets Control), (b) Israeli Ministry of Finance financing restrictions, (c) Bank of Israel guidance on Iran-related transactions, (d) US CFIUS review (Committee on Foreign Investment in the US) for USD correspondent relationships with Israeli banks. (2) **AI in cross-border payments.** Israeli banks increasingly use AI to: (a) route USD payments (select correspondent bank, select payment path, optimize settlement time), (b) detect sanctions violations (screen payment recipients against OFAC lists, identify beneficial owners), (c) anticipate payment rejections (predict based on regulatory risk if a payment will be declined). These decisions are high-consequence: a misdirected payment affects customer service; a missed sanctions violation affects regulatory compliance. (3) **Chain-of-custody role.** The chain-of-custody primitive captures: (a) the AI agent's routing decision (which correspondent bank was selected, why), (b) the sanctions-screen output (OFAC match score, decision: accept/block/escalate), (c) the model's confidence (e.g., '94% confidence this payment is high-risk'). The chain proves the decision was not tampered with after the fact. (4) **Institutional governance requirement.** An Israeli bank operating AI agents for USD cross-border payments must document: (a) the AI model's decision framework (what inputs feed the decision, what outputs it produces), (b) the human escalation path (if the model detects high-risk, does it escalate to a human reviewer?), (c) the audit trail (the chain captures the decision; audit procedures verify the decision was compliant). (d) coordination with the Ministry of Finance (if the decision involves Iran-related transactions, does the bank notify the Ministry?). (5) **Directive 365 operational resilience and cross-border payment AI.** If a cross-border payment AI agent fails (e.g., model inference latency exceeds the FedWire settlement window, forcing rejection of time-sensitive payments), the bank must recover within its operational-resilience target (typically ≤30 minutes for critical payment decisions). The chain proves the failure occurred and at what timestamp, supporting incident investigation. (6) **Examiner expectations.** Federal Reserve examiners reviewing the cross-border payment AI will ask for the chain-captured evidence of: (a) each payment routed by the AI, (b) the model's decision parameters, (c) any human overrides or escalations. The chain serves as the auditable record for correspondent-banking oversight. (7) **Institutional documentation.** The bank documents its cross-border payment AI governance in the Directive 359 (cyber defense) and Directive 365 (operational resilience) control descriptions, and in the USD correspondent account management procedure. The Chief Risk Officer and the Chief International Officer jointly own this governance."

### Q-20. Merger and acquisition hand-off — how does the chain transfer between institutions?

Israeli banks occasionally merge (e.g., Mizrahi Tefahot + Leumi acquisition discussions in prior years, various smaller-bank consolidations). When two banks merge, the acquiring bank may inherit the target bank's chain-of-custody infrastructure. The spec (§10.10 M&A handoff section) addresses key rotation and tenant migration, but does not address the cross-institution governance questions: How long does the target bank's chain remain separately auditable? How are cross-bank decisions (before the systems are merged) audited? **Ask:** Add to the spec §10.10 or to incident-response-playbook.md an "M&A and institution-transition" section: "(1) **Chain custody during M&A.** When an acquiring bank inherits a target bank's chain-of-custody system (either by system migration or by taking over the target's operations), the chain governance transfers: (a) the acquiring bank becomes the chain's data controller (responsible for data custody, retention, incident response, regulatory reporting). (b) the acquiring bank assigns an IKM custodian (typically the acquiring bank's HSM team) to take custody of the target bank's IKM, which may require key-holder coordination (both the target's and the acquiring bank's crypto officers must participate in key-takeoff and delivery). (c) a multi-tenant transition period may exist during which the target and acquiring banks operate separate AI agents and separate chains (pre-integration); the two chains merge into a unified acquiring-bank chain post-integration. (2) **Pre-integration (separate chains).** Before system integration: (a) the target bank continues to operate its chain independently, with its own IKM, ledger, seal job, and HSM. (b) the acquiring bank operates its own separate chain. (c) the two chains use different `tenant_id` values (e.g., target tenant ID = `target_bank_legacy`, acquiring tenant ID = `acquirer_bank`). (d) verifier output from each chain is independently valid; they do not cross-verify. (3) **Integration date and chain migration.** On a planned integration date: (a) the acquiring bank's IT team takes offline the target bank's chain infrastructure (disables the seal job, freezes the ledger). (b) the final seal is published (covering all events up to the freeze moment). (c) the target bank's IKM is migrated to the acquiring bank's HSM custody (may involve key re-wrapping or re-derivation). (d) the target bank's historical chain (all seals and events) is archived and transferred to the acquiring bank's chain custody. (e) the acquiring bank integrates the target bank's AI agents into the acquiring bank's infrastructure, re-mapping them to the acquiring bank's chain and tenant ID. (4) **Post-integration (unified chain).** After integration: (a) the acquiring bank operates a unified chain with a single tenant ID and unified ledger. (b) new AI decisions from the integrated agents use the unified chain. (c) historical decisions from the target bank (pre-integration) remain in the archived target chain, separately auditable. (d) a verifier can walk the historical target chain (using the target's public key and historical IKM) and confirm pre-integration decisions are integrity-bound. A verifier can walk the acquiring bank's chain and confirm post-integration decisions are integrity-bound. (5) **Audit trail for regulators.** During a regulatory examination post-M&A: (a) the examiner may request evidence of pre-integration decisions from the target chain (to verify the target's compliance with prior oversight). (b) the examiner verifies the target chain is independently valid (verifier passes, seals verify against the target bank's public key). (c) the examiner verifies the integration boundary is documented (the final seal date of the target chain, the first unified seal date of the acquiring chain, the historical archive location). (d) the examiner samples post-integration decisions from the unified chain and confirms they verify successfully. (6) **Institutional responsibility.** The acquiring bank's Chief Information Officer is responsible for the M&A chain-migration procedure. Pre-integration, the target bank's Chief Information Officer owns the target chain; post-integration, the acquiring bank's CIO owns the unified chain and the historical archive. The acquiring bank documents the integration procedure in the Directive 357 data-governance control description. (7) **Regulatory notification.** The acquiring bank notifies the Bank of Israel and the Federal Reserve (if applicable) of the chain-migration completion. The notification confirms: (a) historical target chain is archived and remains auditable, (b) unified chain is operational and continues the integrity-binding for post-integration decisions, (c) public keys for both historical and unified chains are registered with the regulator."

### Q-22. Transition and sunset planning — what happens if the chain is deprecated?

The spec's scope is v1.0. Future versions (v1.1, v2.0) may introduce changes (new algorithms, new wire format, new Merkle construction). An Israeli bank deploying v1.0 today will operate the chain for 7+ years (spec retention period). If the Bank of Israel issues a directive requiring migration to v2.0 (e.g., due to cryptographic algorithm retirement), the bank must have a transition plan. The spec names version numbers and format versions but does not address deprecation timelines or migration procedures. **Ask:** Add to governance-and-roadmap.md or a new section of the spec "Version deprecation and migration roadmap": "(1) **Lifecycle timelines.** (a) **Version 1.0 (current).** Expected deployment through 2027–2028. If no algorithm breaks are detected (SHA-256, Ed25519, HMAC-SHA-256 remain secure), v1.0 will remain conformant through 2035 (assuming 7-year asset retention windows). (b) **Version 1.1 (candidate, 2027).** Potential enhancements: post-quantum signature algorithm co-signing (Dilithium alongside Ed25519), optional per-entry algorithm negotiation, enhanced audit-data capture. v1.1 is backward-compatible with v1.0; existing v1.0 chains remain verifiable under v1.1 verifiers. (c) **Version 2.0 (candidate, 2030+).** Potential breaking changes: migrating to SHA-3 (if SHA-256 weakens), migrating to a different Merkle construction, adding new wire-format fields. v2.0 chains are not backward-compatible with v1.0 verifiers; institutions transitioning to v2.0 must operate both v1.0 and v2.0 verifiers during a transition window. (2) **Deprecation timeline.** (a) When a spec version is deprecated (e.g., v1.0 is deprecated in 2033), the Bank of Israel issues a directive requiring migration to the new version within a defined period (typically 2–3 years, e.g., 'v1.0 chains must be migrated to v2.0 by end of 2035'). (b) The deprecation directive includes: (i) reasons (e.g., 'SHA-256 has been weakened; we are migrating to SHA-3'), (ii) migration procedure (how to convert v1.0 chains to v2.0), (iii) support timeline (how long the Bank of Israel will accept v1.0 verifier output for examination purposes), (iv) timeline for removing v1.0 support from the Bank of Israel's examination standards. (3) **Institutional migration responsibility.** An Israeli bank operating v1.0 chains must: (a) plan for version migration 2–3 years before deprecation is announced (i.e., by 2030–2031, prepare for v1.0 deprecation scheduled for 2035), (b) maintain both v1.0 and v2.0 verifiers during the transition (v1.0 to verify historical chains, v2.0 to verify new chains), (c) re-seal historical v1.0 chains under v2.0 (if required by the migration procedure), or (d) accept that historical v1.0 chains are read-only and archive them (continuing to verify against v1.0 public keys for regulatory evidence). (4) **Verifier distribution.** The Bank of Israel and the FFIEC will publish both v1.0 and v2.0 verifiers for an extended period (at least 5 years post-deprecation) to support institutions verifying historical chains. After 5 years, the Bank of Israel may discontinue v1.0 verifier support. (5) **Examiner expectations.** During an examination, the examiner expects: (a) institutions deploying v1.0 to have a documented version-upgrade plan and timeline, (b) institutions in the transition window to be running both v1.0 and v2.0 verifiers, (c) institutions post-transition to have migrated all chains to v2.0 (or have archived v1.0 chains with clear justification and a plan for eventual migration). If a bank has no documented plan for v1.0 deprecation, the examiner may cite it as a control gap requiring a plan within 90 days."

## Closing assessment

**Partial? No.** The spec is operationally sound and does not violate any Bank of Israel regulatory requirements. The five identified gaps (Directive 365 recovery-time integration, Directive 367 cloud-localization compliance, Directive 361 INCD coordination, Directive 414 vendor-audit integration, Hebrew-language disclosure workflow) are manageable through institutional compensating controls. These gaps are not defects in the spec's design; they are governance questions that each Israeli institution must answer per its own risk profile and regulatory mandate. The spec's silence on these topics is appropriate for a US-born specification; the spec should not dictate Bank of Israel supervisory procedure. Instead, the gaps are the responsibility of the Bank of Israel to address through supplementary guidance to the five megabanks.

**Confidence under Bank of Israel supervision.** Israeli banks can deploy chain-of-custody systems conformant with this spec and can simultaneously achieve Bank of Israel compliance. The chain is cryptographically and operationally sound. The integration with Israeli directives requires institutional discipline and supplementary governance, not spec amendments. Banks deploying v1.0 should plan for version evolution (v1.1, v2.0) and should engage with the Bank of Israel's supervisory team early in the deployment cycle to confirm their chosen topology and operational procedures align with the five directives. With institutional governance in place, the chain strengthens the Israeli banking system's AI-governance and cyber-resilience posture materially.

**Recommended next steps.** (1) **Bank of Israel guidance document (6 months, internally).** Avishai recommends the Bank of Israel issue a supervisory letter to all five megabanks (by December 2026) addressing the five gaps and providing supplementary guidance on Directive 365 integration (recovery-time targets), Directive 367 compliance (cloud-localization rules), Directive 361 (INCD coordination), Directive 414 (vendor audits), and Hebrew-language disclosure workflows. The letter cites this spec as the technical foundation and the Bank of Israel directives as the regulatory framework. (2) **FFIEC engagement (3 months, concurrently).** Avishai recommends the Bank of Israel request observer status on the FFIEC chain-of-custody working group and propose amendments or a future-version roadmap addressing international-regulator needs. Engagement with the FFIEC is most productive early (within 6 months of spec publication). (3) **Institutional guidance for deployers (ongoing).** The Bank of Israel should develop a brief deployment guide for Israeli banks: "Chain-of-Custody v1.0 Deployment for Israeli Banks: Alignment with Directives 357, 359, 361, 365, 367, 411, 414." This guide walks through topology choices (self-hosted vs vendor-hosted, INCD approval), operational-resilience planning, incident-response procedures, and regulatory reporting. (4) **Examiner training (6 months).** The Bank of Israel's examination team should be trained on the spec and the five directives' integration points.
