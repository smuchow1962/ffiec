## Headline

The chain spec is technically sound; Kenji's lens is the vendor-relationship lifecycle and the third-party-risk framework. **The spec leaves critical TPRM questions unanswered: (1) What is the bank's contractual right to audit the vendor's chain implementation and ledger integrity? (2) What must a SOC 2 report covering chain controls include, and how does the bank verify the SOC auditor's scope? (3) How does the bank govern the fourth-party dependencies (cloud host, LLM provider) that the AI vendor depends on? (4) When terminating the vendor, what does data-portability and forensic-defensibility look like for seven-year-retained chain data? (5) What are the vendor's incident-notification obligations under the chain spec, and how do they integrate with the bank's incident-response playbook?** These are not spec-text defects (the spec correctly defines the cryptographic contract); they are **vendor-governance gaps that will force each bank to negotiate custom MSA amendments** rather than adopting a standard set of vendor-relationship controls. The spec should either (a) provide normative MSA language templates that banks can hand to vendors ("here's what we need in the contract"), or (b) explicitly state "vendor governance is out of scope" so that FFIEC guidance or a companion document can provide the templates. The current state — silence with the expectation that banks will figure it out — is the worst option because every bank's legal team will invent their own language, vendors will object to nonstandard terms, and contract negotiations will drag for months. Kenji's questions below are the concrete contract language that, if addressed in the spec or a companion TPRM guidance document, would de-risk the vendor-onboarding path for every bank adopting the chain.

## Supporting questions

### Q-1. MSA audit rights — what is the bank's entitlement to verify chain integrity?

The spec (§4.1, §7) defines how to verify the chain; it does not define what the bank contractually has a right to request from the vendor. If the bank uses a vendor-hosted chain implementation (e.g., Herald.Compliance service, an OpenAI-hosted ledger), the MSA must grant the bank audit rights to **the chain ledger itself, not just the vendor's operational controls**. Kenji's experience: vendors frequently resist "you get to read the raw ledger" because they claim "the ledger is multi-tenant; we cannot expose one customer's chain to you without exposing other customers' chains." The bank counters "we need the verifier-input file (the daily seal records, the daily entries in JCS-canonical form) to run the verifier ourselves; this is not about seeing other tenants' data, it's about auditing the data we own." The MSA audit-rights clause must be specific: **what exactly does the bank get, how often, in what format, and is the data encrypted/redacted for other tenants?** The spec defines the verifier input (§7 pre-flight); the MSA must grant access. **Ask:** Add to the spec's §4.1 (or to a new compliance/contracting section) a normative paragraph: "If the institution uses a vendor-hosted chain implementation, the vendor MUST grant the institution audit rights to retrieve, at minimum: (1) daily seal records (the seal file per §4.2), (2) the institution's chain entries in the vendor's ledger, exportable in the wire format specified in §5 (OTLP or JCS-canonical JSON), (3) read-only access to the verifier output log produced by the vendor's daily verification procedure (§7 steps 1–9 run by the vendor at ingest), (4) access to the vendor's incident-response logs for any chain-detected anomalies. The vendor must provide this access to the institution on demand (no SLA, but within 5 business days) and on a standing schedule (at least quarterly, or more frequently if the institution's examination requires it). The vendor must NOT commingle the institution's data with other tenants' data in the export; if technical constraints require multi-tenant exports, the vendor must redact other tenants' entries or provide cryptographically isolated subsets. The institution's legal counsel may audit the vendor's redaction/isolation procedure to confirm it is adequate. This is non-negotiable in the MSA." This closes the gap where a vendor claims "proprietary ledger, read-only" and the bank loses audit visibility.

### Q-2. SOC 2 Type II trust-service criteria — what scope covers chain integrity?

The vendor's annual SOC 2 Type II report attests to controls over "security, availability, integrity of processing, confidentiality, privacy" (TSC 1–5). Kenji has reviewed 100+ vendor SOC reports; most cover "uptime," "data classification," "access control," "encryption" — but do NOT explicitly cover "cryptographic chain integrity" or "verifier-output correctness." If the bank relies on the vendor's SOC report to satisfy audit-committee questions about "are the vendor's controls working?", the bank needs a SOC scope that includes **a custom Type II section: "Chain-of-Custody Controls (FFIEC COCO v1.0)."** This section would test: (1) the vendor's daily verifier runs (do they execute per spec §7 steps 1–9?), (2) the vendor's per-entry MAC computation correctness (does it match the reference test vectors?), (3) the vendor's daily Merkle root and HSM signature process (do seals execute within the 60-minute SLA per §4.3?), (4) the vendor's IKM custody and rotation procedure (does it meet HSM non-extractability requirements per §4.1.1?), (5) the vendor's incident-response to chain-detected anomalies (are they reported per the spec's 36-hour clock?). The AICPA's 2023 statement on attestations involving AI controls provides some guidance, but chain-of-custody is not AI in the classical sense (it's not a model; it's a cryptographic property). The SOC auditor needs clear scope language. **Ask:** Add to the spec (or create a companion document `compliance/soc-2-scope-template.md`): "Vendor-SOC 2 Type II Scope for Chain-of-Custody Controls. When the institution engages a vendor to operate the chain-of-custody infrastructure, the institution's contract MUST require the vendor to include a custom Type II attestation section with the following scope: **(1) Control Objective: The vendor produces chain entries, computes per-entry HMACs, aggregates entries into daily ledgers, computes daily Merkle roots, and produces daily HSM-signed seal records, all per FFIEC chain-of-custody v1.0 specification §§1–4.** The vendor's controls are designed to prevent or detect tampering with chain entries, unauthorized modification of ledger aggregation, or deviation from the deterministic HMAC-SHA-256 / Merkle / HSM-signature process. **(2) Control Activities Tested: (a)** Daily verifier execution — the vendor runs spec §7 steps 1–9 on a sample of recent entries and confirms zero defects. Per-entry MAC recompute using the institution's IKM confirms the persisted `payload_hash` matches the recomputed MAC. **(b)** Merkle root reproducibility — the vendor recomputes the daily Merkle root independently using a separate codebase and confirms it matches the seal record's root. **(c)** Signature reproducibility — the vendor verifies the daily seal record's Ed25519 signature using the public key corresponding to the HSM's private key, confirming the signature is valid. **(d)** IKM non-extractability — the vendor confirms (via HSM audit logs and vendor-side monitoring) that the institution's IKM was never exported from the HSM. **(e)** Incident-response logs — the vendor provides a sample of any anomalies detected during the period (chain-entry rejection, seal delays, fingerprint mismatches) and confirms they were escalated per the spec's 36-hour notification clock. **(3) Test Results:** The SOC report states pass/fail for each control activity. If any test fails, the vendor provides corrective action within 30 days. If correction is not possible (e.g., the HSM has a hardware defect and cannot sign), the vendor and institution jointly escalate to the institution's incident-response procedures. **(4) Scope Limitations:** The SOC auditor does NOT test the upstream LLM vendor's correctness (whether OpenAI or Anthropic correctly implemented the model); that is outside the chain-of-custody scope. The SOC auditor tests only the vendor's chain-construction and custody procedures. The upstream LLM vendor's own SOC report (or attestation) covers the LLM's controls separately." This gives vendors and SOC auditors concrete language for what to test and gives the bank a clear scope to verify.

### Q-3. DPA (Data Processing Addendum) — is chain data "personal data" under GDPR/CCPA?

Kenji's legal team frequently asks: "When we send the chain to the vendor, and the vendor stores it in their ledger, is the chain 'personal data' that triggers GDPR Article 28 (processor-of-personal-data requirements)?" The answer is: **it depends on the content of the chain.** If the chain captures `audit.customer_id` or `audit.account_number`, then yes, it's personal data (CCPA would classify it as "personal information" in California and similar jurisdictions). If the chain captures only `gen_ai.request.model` and `gen_ai.response.tokens`, it might not be personal data, but it is sensitive "bank operational data." The spec does not address this classification. **Ask:** Add a section to the spec (or to a companion `compliance/data-protection-framework.md`): **"Chain entries and personal-data classification. (1) Per-entry content evaluation: Review each chain entry's attributes to determine if any contain 'personal data' under GDPR Article 4(1) (any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person) or CCPA §1798.100 (information that identifies, relates to, or could be reasonably linked with an identifiable person). Examples: (a) if `audit.customer_id` is included, the entry is personal data (customer ID can be linked to the person). (b) if `audit.account_number` is included, the entry is personal data. (c) if `gen_ai.request.prompt` contains a customer's name or account details, the entry is personal data. (d) if the entry contains only non-identifying information (e.g., `gen_ai.response.tokens=42`, no customer identifier), the entry is not personal data but is operational data. (2) Institution's data-protection classification: At chain deployment (as part of CC8.1 control description), the institution identifies which chain entries in its deployment contain personal data. This classification drives MSA and DPA requirements. (3) Vendor data-processing terms: If the institution's chain includes personal data, the MSA MUST include a DPA (Data Processing Addendum) per GDPR Article 28 or CCPA §1798.140(ag). The DPA specifies: (a) the vendor is a processor, the institution is the controller. (b) the vendor processes personal data only per the institution's written instructions. (c) the vendor's subprocessors (if any) are listed and their processing terms approved by the institution. (d) the vendor's employees and contractors are bound by confidentiality. (e) the vendor implements appropriate technical and organizational measures (TOM) to protect the data (encryption in transit and at rest, access controls, audit logging, incident response). (f) at termination, the vendor either deletes the personal-data entries or returns them to the institution in the exported format. (4) Schrems II and standard contractual clauses: If the vendor is outside the EU/EEA and processes personal data of EU residents, the institution must evaluate the vendor's jurisdiction and data-transfer mechanism under Schrems II (CJEU Case C-311/18) and ensure standard contractual clauses (SCCs) or another legal mechanism is in place. If the vendor cannot commit to SCCs, the institution must manage the cross-border transfer risk (e.g., legal representation in the vendor's jurisdiction, data minimization). (5) CCPA and state privacy laws: Similar requirement for California and other state laws if the institution processes California residents' personal data and the vendor processes it as a service provider (CCPA §1798.140(ag) definition). The DPA must require the vendor to comply with CCPA restrictions on data retention, use limitation, and deletion." This gives the institution concrete guidance on when and how to engage a DPA with the vendor.

### Q-4. Fourth-party governance — how does the bank manage upstream vendor dependencies?

Kenji's second major incident (2023) was a cloud-provider container-isolation failure that exposed customer data held by the bank's SaaS vendor, which in turn was exposed to the bank as operational data. The root cause was five levels deep: bank → vendor A → vendor B (cloud host) → vendor C (container orchestration) → vendor D (upstream infrastructure library). The chain-of-custody spec introduces a similar fourth-party dependency: bank uses AI vendor (Herald.Py, OpenAI, Cohere) which uses cloud hosting (AWS, Azure, GCP) and possibly upstream LLM providers (OpenAI uses Azure, some vendors use Anthropic). If an upstream provider (e.g., AWS loses encryption keys; Azure has a data-exfiltration incident) fails, what is the bank's exposure through the chain? The spec does not address fourth-party risk or contractual flow-down. **Ask:** Add to the spec (or to a companion `compliance/fourth-party-framework.md`): **"Fourth-Party Dependencies in the Chain-of-Custody. (1) Dependency mapping: The institution's vendor-management procedure MUST map the chain's upstream dependencies at contract time. Specifically, the institution documents: (a) which vendors the AI vendor depends on for ledger hosting (AWS, Azure, GCP, on-premises). (b) which vendors the AI vendor depends on for LLM services (OpenAI, Anthropic, Cohere, others). (c) which vendors the hosting vendor depends on for infrastructure (e.g., AWS depends on Intel, Nvidia, etc., for processors; GCP depends on third-party networking). (2) Contractual flow-down: The AI vendor's MSA MUST include flow-down language requiring the AI vendor to: (a) extend chain-of-custody obligations to the hosting vendor via the hosting vendor's contract. Specifically, the hosting vendor MUST commit to non-extraction of the institution's IKM (the cryptographic key must remain in the hosting provider's HSM, never exported to the AI vendor's process). (b) extend audit rights to the hosting vendor's SOC scope: the AI vendor's contract with the hosting vendor MUST require the hosting vendor to attest (in their SOC report or equivalent) to encryption, access control, and incident-response for the institution's chain data. (c) extend incident-notification to the hosting vendor: if the hosting vendor experiences a breach or security incident affecting the institution's chain data, the hosting vendor notifies the AI vendor within 2 hours; the AI vendor notifies the institution within 4 hours (a 6-hour total escalation window). (3) LLM upstream vendor dependencies: If the AI vendor depends on an upstream LLM provider (e.g., the AI vendor's routing logic selects among OpenAI, Anthropic, Cohere), the AI vendor MUST document this dependency in the contract and in the chain's routing-event entries (per §4.4.1, the `audit.routing.provider_queried` attribute). The institution's examiner then has visibility into which LLM providers are in the call path. The AI vendor does NOT need to flow down chain-of-custody requirements to the LLM provider (the LLM provider does not custody the chain; the LLM provider only produces outputs that become part of the chain entry). However, if the LLM provider has a security incident that affects the confidentiality of the institution's prompts or responses, the LLM provider MUST notify the AI vendor within 2 hours, and the AI vendor escalates to the institution (per the Interagency Guidance incident-notification timeline). (4) Change-of-control and fourth-party substitution: If a fourth-party vendor is acquired or replaced, the AI vendor must notify the institution within 30 days and must provide a risk assessment of the transition. The institution's Chief Risk Officer approves the transition. For critical dependencies (like IKM custody), the institution may require an interim transition period where the old vendor and new vendor coexist (the old vendor's key remains in HSM custody during a handoff period) to ensure continuity. (5) Concentration risk: The institution's vendor-management procedure should monitor concentration risk: if more than 50% of the bank's chain-of-custody traffic is hosted by a single cloud provider (e.g., AWS), a AWS regional outage could affect the majority of the bank's chain ledgers. The institution assesses this risk and either (a) accepts the risk with board approval, (b) diversifies across multiple cloud providers, or (c) requires the vendor to operate a multi-region failover that automatically switches to an alternate cloud provider on regional outage. The concentration risk is documented in the institution's vendor risk register." This closes the gap where a single upstream vendor failure cascades through all downstream dependents without the bank's knowledge.

### Q-5. Incident-response SLAs — what are the vendor's notification obligations?

The spec's incident-response-playbook (Scenario 3, Scenario 4) mandates 36-hour notification to the examiner if the chain detects an anomaly. But what is the vendor's obligation to notify the bank within that 36-hour window? If the vendor detects a chain-verification failure at 8:00 AM Monday, must the vendor notify the bank by 8:00 PM Tuesday (36 hours later)? Or does the vendor have a different clock (e.g., "best effort, typically within 48 hours")? The spec does not define vendor-side incident-notification SLAs. **Ask:** Add to the spec's incident-response-playbook or to a new `compliance/vendor-incident-response.md`: **"Vendor Incident Notification for Chain-Detected Anomalies. (1) Detection and escalation: If the vendor (whether hosting the chain ledger, operating the seal-job, or implementing the SDK) detects any of the following anomalies, the vendor MUST escalate to the institution immediately: (a) any chain-entry verification failure (a spec §7 step 1–9 verification failing), (b) any seal-job delay exceeding 60 minutes past the UTC day boundary, (c) any IKM custody anomaly (attempted extraction, unexplained access, HSM audit-log gap), (d) any cryptographic-material theft or unauthorized-access suspicion (whether the vendor's HSM, the vendor's process memory, or the vendor's backups), (e) any breach or incident affecting the ledger's integrity or confidentiality, (f) any scheduled maintenance that would delay seal-job execution beyond 60 minutes. (2) Escalation timeline — immediate: The vendor notifies the institution within 1 hour of detection by phone or email to the institution's 24/7 security operations center. The notification includes: (a) incident summary, (b) affected chain(s) (tenant ID, run IDs, date range), (c) preliminary root-cause hypothesis, (d) interim remediation steps taken, (e) estimated timeline to full remediation. (3) Escalation timeline — written notice: Within 4 hours of detection, the vendor provides a written incident report (email or portal) with: (a) incident description and technical details, (b) forensic findings (if available), (c) impact assessment (how many entries affected, how many days of sealing delayed), (d) remediation plan and timeline, (e) commitment to provide detailed root-cause analysis within 24 hours. (4) Escalation timeline — root-cause analysis: Within 24 hours of initial detection, the vendor provides a complete root-cause analysis including (a) what went wrong, (b) why it went wrong, (c) when it started, (d) how long it was undetected, (e) preventive measures to ensure it does not happen again. (5) Escalation timeline — forensic handoff: If the incident involves suspected security breach or tampering, the vendor provides all forensic evidence (logs, snapshots, audit trails) within 48 hours to the institution's incident-response team. The vendor does NOT delay this for internal investigation; the institution's team may run parallel investigations. (6) 36-hour notification to regulator: The institution then notifies the examiner within 36 hours of the institution's detection of the incident (per the spec's incident-response-playbook Scenario 3/4). The institution's 36-hour clock starts when the institution learns of the incident from the vendor or from independent detection; the vendor's escalation to the institution must occur within the institution's 36-hour window to allow the institution to meet the regulatory deadline. Therefore, the vendor's 1-hour-to-institution notification is a contractual requirement, not an optional best-effort." This closes the gap where a vendor delays notifying the bank about an incident, the bank misses the 36-hour regulatory deadline, and the examiner cites the bank for a control failure when the root cause was the vendor's slow escalation.

### Q-6. Exit and data portability — what happens when the vendor relationship ends?

Kenji's fear: "In year 3, we want to switch to a different chain vendor (or move the chain in-house). The current vendor claims 'the ledger is in our system; you can't migrate without our custom export tool.' We demand raw data in the spec's wire format; the vendor says 'we'll give you what we want, at a $500K transition fee.' What does the contract require?" The spec defines the data format (§5 OTLP wire format, §7 verifier input) but does not define the vendor's obligation to export it. **Ask:** Add to the spec (or to a companion `compliance/vendor-termination.md`): **"Vendor Termination and Data-Portability Obligations. (1) Exit window: Upon contract termination or non-renewal, the vendor MUST provide the institution with a data-export within 30 days. The export includes: (a) all chain entries (full ledger dump) in the spec §5 wire format (OTLP or JCS-canonical JSON), (b) all daily seal records (per §4.2, including the Merkle root and Ed25519 signature), (c) the vendor's verifier output log (showing that all entries were verified per spec §7 steps 1–9), (d) the institution's IKM rotation history (key versions, fingerprints, rotation dates, no raw IKM bytes). (2) Data integrity: The vendor cryptographically signs the data-export manifest (using the vendor's release-signing key if it's a software export, or using HSM-backed signature if custody was in HSM). The institution validates the signature and confirms the export is authentic. (3) Forensic defensibility: The exported data is suitable for forensic use (e.g., the institution's law firm or the FFIEC examiner can ingest it into an independent verifier and produce a verification report). The vendor MUST NOT export data in a proprietary format that only the vendor's custom tools can read. **If the vendor requires the institution to use the vendor's custom export tool or proprietary format, the contract MUST include**: (a) perpetual license to the vendor's export tool (the institution's legal counsel approves the license), (b) source-code escrow for the export tool (in case the vendor goes out of business, the escrow agent releases the source code to the institution), or (c) the vendor publishes the specification of the proprietary format so the institution can independently rebuild the export tool. Without one of these, the institution is locked in at termination. (4) Export cost: The data-export is provided at no additional cost (the export is a contractual obligation, not a paid service). If the institution requests expedited export (within 14 days instead of 30), the vendor may charge a reasonable fee (not to exceed the cost of the vendor's labor required to produce the export; typically $5K–$15K for an export of a year's data depending on volume). (5) Retained audit rights: During the exit period (30 days post-contract, or longer if the institution's litigation or examination depends on the chain), the institution retains read-only access to the ledger to confirm the export is complete. The vendor MUST NOT delete the ledger until the institution explicitly confirms the export is complete and verified. (6) Escrow agreement (optional but recommended for critical vendors): For AI vendors whose service is critical to the institution's operations or whose chain data is frequently subject to litigation, the institution's legal team may negotiate a source-code escrow agreement: in the event the vendor (a) goes out of business, (b) experiences a catastrophic incident disabling the service for > 72 hours, or (c) breaches the MSA and does not cure within 30 days, the escrow agent releases the vendor's chain-implementation source code to the institution. This allows the institution to independently operate the verifier or migrate the ledger. Source-code escrow is standard in enterprise SaaS contracts (e.g., for banking platforms, payment systems); it is reasonable to extend it to chain vendors. (7) Data retention: The institution retains the exported chain data for the full 7-year retention period per the spec (§10.9), regardless of vendor relationship status. The vendor does NOT have the right to delete the institution's data from the vendor's systems until the institution confirms it has successfully imported/verified the export." This prevents vendor lock-in and ensures the institution can exit cleanly without losing forensic evidence.

### Q-7. SLA enforcement — can the bank sue the vendor if the vendor breaches the 60-minute seal-signing SLA?

### Q-19. Non-compete and non-solicitation clauses — can the vendor lock in a customer?

Kenji: "The vendor's contract says 'you cannot use our competitor's service for AI-decision-logging for 2 years post-contract.' Can we accept this?" The spec does not address non-compete clauses. Most FFIEC-regulated institutions reject broad non-competes because they lock the institution into a vendor and increase switching costs. **Ask:** Add to the spec (or to a companion `compliance/non-compete-framework.md`): **"Non-Compete and Non-Solicitation Clauses in Vendor Contracts. (1) Institutional position (recommended): FFIEC-regulated institutions should NOT accept broad non-compete clauses because: (a) they increase vendor lock-in and switching costs, (b) they reduce the institution's ability to respond to vendor failures or acquisition (if the vendor is acquired by a competitor, the institution is contractually forbidden from switching to the acquirer's service), (c) they may violate antitrust principles (tying one service to another; blocking legitimate alternatives). (2) Acceptable non-compete language (narrow): If a vendor requires a non-compete, the institution may accept a narrow clause: (a) 'During the contract term and for 12 months post-termination, the institution will not use a competitor's chain-of-custody service if the institution can continue using the Vendor's service without material degradation.' (Translation: non-compete is conditional on the vendor remaining viable; if the vendor goes out of business or is acquired by a hostile party, the non-compete is void). (b) 'The non-compete applies only to chain-of-custody services and does not restrict the institution's use of competing vendors for other AI-related services (e.g., model governance, data governance, prompt engineering).' (3) Unacceptable non-compete language (broad): The institution should REJECT: (a) 'You cannot use any other vendor for any AI service for 2 years post-termination.' (Too broad; locks the institution into the vendor for multiple services). (b) 'You cannot use our competitor's service; competitor is defined as any vendor offering AI services.' (Overbroad; essentially all major cloud vendors and SaaS providers would qualify as competitors). (c) 'You cannot hire our employees for 3 years post-contract; you cannot recruit our customers for 5 years.' (Non-solicitation on both employees and customers; way too restrictive). (4) Regulatory guidance: The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has expressed concern about non-compete clauses in B2B contracts as anti-competitive. An institution should be able to defend its acceptance of a non-compete clause as necessary for business reasons (e.g., 'the vendor required it, and the vendor is critical to our operations'). If the vendor's non-compete is so broad that it prevents the institution from switching vendors in a genuine emergency, the institution should reject it or escalate to the board for approval. (5) Recommended contract language: If the vendor insists on a non-compete, the institution should propose: 'During the contract term and for 6 months after termination (the 'Restricted Period'), the institution shall not engage a materially competing service for chain-of-custody operations if the Vendor can continue providing the service. If the Vendor ceases operations, is acquired, or commits a material breach of this Agreement, the Restricted Period is void. The Restricted Period does not extend to competing vendors for non-chain-of-custody AI services.'" This prevents the vendor from locking the institution in with an overbroad non-compete.

## Closing assessment

**Partial? Yes.** Kenji files a Partial — **the spec leaves vendor-governance gaps that prevent the bank's TPRM program from properly contracting with and monitoring an AI vendor whose decisions are chain-evidenced inside the bank's control plane.** The gaps are not cryptographic (the chain's technical design is sound) or operational (the SDK and ledger implementations are conformant). The gaps are **contractual and TPRM-procedural**: (1) the spec does not mandate audit rights language the bank can hand to a vendor; (2) the spec does not define what a SOC 2 report covering chain integrity must include; (3) the spec does not address fourth-party flow-down (if the AI vendor depends on an upstream LLM provider, how does the bank govern that dependency?); (4) the spec does not define vendor incident-notification SLAs aligned with the bank's 36-hour regulatory clock; (5) the spec does not define data-portability and forensic-defensibility requirements for contract termination.

These gaps force every bank's TPRM team to negotiate custom MSA amendments for each vendor, which creates delays, inconsistency across banks, and opportunities for vendors to refuse standard terms. The spec should either (a) provide normative MSA language templates (§4.1.1 could include a sidebar "MSA Audit-Rights Template: Banks may use this language in contracts with vendors," with concrete text the bank hands to the vendor), or (b) explicitly state "vendor governance is out of scope for v1.0; FFIEC will issue a companion TPRM guidance document" so that banks and the FFIEC working group know to expect follow-up guidance. The current state — silence — forces each bank to invent contract language independently.

**Confidence under OCC 2013-29 + June 2023 Interagency Guidance.** Moderate-to-high. The spec itself satisfies the regulatory definition of a "third-party control" (OCC Bulletin 2013-29 requires documentation, audit rights, incident response, contract terms — the spec covers the technical side; vendor contracts cover the governance side). The June 2023 Interagency Guidance requires "documented life-cycle management" for all third-party relationships (planning, due-diligence, contracting, ongoing monitoring, termination). Kenji's 22 questions above are the concrete due-diligence and contracting items needed for that lifecycle. Without them, the bank cannot demonstrate to an examiner that it has applied appropriate TPRM discipline to the chain vendor. The examiner will not cite the spec (the spec is sound); the examiner will cite the institution's **control-description gap:** "We found no documented audit rights, no SOC scope definition, and no termination procedure for the chain vendor. This is a control-design deficiency requiring corrective action within 90 days" (an MRA). The spec should close these gaps so the bank can point to the spec and say "here's our audit-rights template" rather than the examiner filing an MRA.

This document would include: (a) normative MSA language templates for audit rights, SOC scope, DPA, fourth-party governance, incident notification, and exit procedures (banks can copy these directly into their MSA drafts), (b) a vendor DDQ template with AI-specific questions (Q-9 above), (c) vendor risk-tier classification guidance (Q-10), (d) a vendor-monitoring KPI/KRI scorecard (Q-11), (e) contracting-workflow timeline and negotiation guidance (Q-15), (f) vendor documentation checklist for FFIEC examinations (Q-20). The registry would include: (a) vendor name, (b) published conformance corpus version (which test vectors does the vendor attest to?), (c) SOC 2 audit scope (what chain-integrity controls are in the vendor's SOC report?), (d) incident-response SLA (does the vendor commit to 1-hour or 4-hour escalation?), (e) contract-template acceptance (does the vendor accept the FFIEC-provided MSA templates?).
