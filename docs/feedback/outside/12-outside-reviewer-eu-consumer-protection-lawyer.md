## Headline

The technical primitives are sound. I take that as established by the cryptographic-security analyst's drop and by the spec's own §1.3 security definitions and §1.4 compositional analysis. The chain produces artifacts that, in the institution's hands and verified under the institution's IKM, prove what the AI said and that the recorded bytes were not altered after capture. That is a legitimate evidentiary claim under EU national procedural law and would support an Advanced Electronic Signature analysis under eIDAS Article 26.

**My read pass found ten places where the spec creates an asymmetric evidentiary posture that disadvantages the consumer in EU adversarial proceedings — and the spec does not name the consumer-side mechanisms that close the asymmetry.** The institution holds the IKM, the HSM signing key, the ledger storage, the verifier binary, and the operational events stream. The data subject holds none of these. Even when EU law (GDPR Article 15, AI Act Article 86, Charter Article 47) entitles the consumer to receive evidence of the decision, the spec does not specify the form in which the chain artifacts are surfaced to the consumer, the verification posture the consumer's expert can adopt without IKM access, or the obligations the institution carries when the data subject's lawyer demands the §7 verifier output as part of a subject access request or a court-ordered disclosure. The spec's §10.13 evidentiary-artifact list is institution-side; there is no parallel consumer-side artifact list. The §7 witness-verifier mode (`PASS-STRUCTURALLY, key-bound verification skipped`) is the closest the spec comes to giving the consumer's expert an independent verification path, but the spec does not say the institution must make this path mechanically available to data subjects, nor does it name the form of disclosure the consumer's expert receives.

**What the spec does well for consumer-side proceedings.** The §7 verifier procedure is byte-deterministic and reproducible from the spec text plus the test-vector corpus, which means a consumer-side expert can run the same procedure against the same artifacts and produce a comparable result. The §1.2 epistemic-scope discipline ("the chain proves what the AI said and that the record was not tampered with; the chain does NOT prove the statement is factually accurate, policy-compliant, or unbiased") is exactly the framing my client needs the institution to adopt — it forecloses the institution from claiming the chain proves the decision was correct, only that the decision was as recorded. The §10.11 ECOA translation schema and the customer-dispute-procedures.md GDPR Article 22 section commit the institution to chaining the human-review record; this preserves the meaningful-review evidence in integrity-bound form and is structurally helpful to the consumer's challenge. The §10.10.2 Pattern B (per-region tenant_id) maps cleanly onto the Brussels I bis cross-border consumer-jurisdiction architecture. These are real strengths and I name them without qualification.

**What the spec needs for consumer-side EU adversarial proceedings.** A normative description of the form in which chain artifacts are surfaced to a data subject under GDPR Article 15(3) — what does the institution produce, on what timeline, accompanied by what verification artifacts. A normative path for an independent consumer-side expert to verify the chain entries the institution produces, without institution-side veto. An AI Act Article 86 mapping naming which chain attributes deliver the "main elements of the decision taken" the consumer is entitled to receive. A Charter Article 47 effective-remedy posture covering the case where the consumer cannot afford a cryptographic expert. A SCHUFA C-634/21 acknowledgment recognising that automated credit scoring is itself an Article 22 decision and the chain's evidentiary scope follows. An Unfair Contract Terms analysis for institution-side cross-jurisdictional disclosure clauses that route disputes to a forum the consumer cannot afford. A Representative Actions Directive (2020/1828) per-consumer extraction procedure for collective-redress proceedings. A counterfactual-evidence acknowledgment naming what the chain does NOT capture (the decision a non-AI process would have produced) and where that evidence lives. A subject-access-request cost framework grounded in Article 15(3)'s "first copy is free" rule. A regulator-pack document the consumer's national consumer-protection authority can read alongside the institution's CC8.1.

Each finding below is a one-paragraph gap. The closing language proposal is a spec text addition, a regulator-pack document the consumer-protection authority can rely on, or a procedural commitment the institution makes that closes the asymmetry.

## Supporting questions

### CQ-1. Subject-access form and verification accompaniment under GDPR Article 15(1)–(3)

**Question.** GDPR Article 15(1) gives the data subject the right to obtain "confirmation as to whether or not personal data concerning him or her are being processed, and where that is the case, access to the personal data and [specified] information," including under Article 15(1)(h) "the existence of automated decision-making, including profiling … and, at least in those cases, meaningful information about the logic involved." Article 15(3) adds that the controller shall provide a "copy of the personal data undergoing processing." In SCHUFA Holding (Case C-634/21, 7 December 2023), the CJEU held that automated credit scoring constitutes an Article 22(1) decision and that the corresponding Article 15(1)(h) obligation requires the controller to provide meaningful information about the logic — not merely a generic description of the algorithm but information sufficient for the data subject to "understand the procedure and principles concretely applied." When the institution running the chain receives an Article 15 request from a data subject whose AI-driven decision is captured in the chain, what does the institution produce? The spec is silent. The spec's §10.13 evidentiary artifacts list is institution-internal (for the IT witness laying foundation in the institution's litigation defense); there is no parallel data-subject artifact list. The institution will pull the chain entries by `(tenant_id, run_id, seq)` via its customer-correlation index per `customer-dispute-procedures.md`, but: in what form does the institution disclose the chain entries themselves to the data subject? The captured JSON? The canonical bytes? The verifier output? The seal record? The HSM public-key? Without normative guidance, each institution chooses, and consumer-side experts arriving at the disclosed material discover they received different shapes from different banks, none of which let them mechanically verify the chain.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** Add a new §10.16 (or a parallel section in `customer-dispute-procedures.md` raised to normative status) titled "Data-subject disclosure form (informative for institutions; load-bearing for the data subject's right of access)" naming: (a) the institution's GDPR Article 15(3) "copy of personal data undergoing processing" disclosure for chain entries SHOULD include, at minimum, the captured JSON form of every chain entry concerning the data subject (per spec §5.2 best-evidence posture, the captured JSON is the content-bearing form); (b) the institution SHOULD additionally provide the canonical bytes of those entries (the integrity-bearing form) so the data subject's expert can independently reproduce the per-event MAC compute when the institution makes its IKM verification accessible; (c) the institution SHOULD provide the seal record (`merkle_root`, `algorithm`, `public_key_id`, `signature`, `signed_at`) for each tenant-day the disclosed entries belong to and a pointer to the institution's published tenant-key registry resolving `public_key_id` to the Ed25519 public key; (d) the institution SHOULD provide its verifier-run output for the disclosed period showing `Status: PASS` (or the institution's documented anomaly disposition); (e) the disclosure timeline aligns with Article 12(3) — within one month, extendable by two months for complex requests with notification. Per Article 15(3) third sentence the first copy is free of charge; subsequent copies may carry a reasonable fee based on administrative cost, NOT a fee that effectively makes verification unaffordable to the data subject. The institution's CC8.1 control description names the disclosure form the institution adopts and the procedure that surfaces the artifacts within the regulatory window.

### CQ-2. Independent consumer-side verification — making §7 witness mode mechanically usable

**Question.** Spec §7 defines a witness-verifier mode (no `--master-key`, executes steps 1, 2, 3, 3a, 4, 5, 6, 10, 11, 12, and 12a; skips 7, 8, 9 — the IKM-bound steps). The output is `Status: PASS-STRUCTURALLY, key-bound verification skipped`. This is the closest the spec gets to giving a consumer-side expert an independent verification path. The structural mode confirms there is no insertion, deletion, reordering, format-version mismatch, header-tampering, cross-chain lift, or seal-signature forgery, AND it confirms the daily Merkle root recomputation matches the HSM-signed seal. Those are real properties; a competent consumer-side expert can run them and produce an independent integrity opinion that is admissible as expert testimony in EU national courts under each country's procedural code. But the spec does not say the institution MUST make this path mechanically available to data subjects who request it under Article 15. The witness-verifier requires (a) the chain entries produced in their on-disk form per §6, (b) the seal records, (c) the institution's public key. The institution holds (a), (b), and (c) and chooses what to disclose. Without a normative obligation to disclose the artifacts in a witness-runnable form, the institution can effectively veto independent consumer-side verification by disclosing only a printed PDF or a summary.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** Add to §7 (under "Witness-verifier mode") a paragraph titled "Disclosure for consumer-side verification" naming: institutions disclosing chain entries to a data subject under GDPR Article 15 SHOULD include the artifacts required to run §7 witness mode against the disclosed entries — specifically, the on-disk NDJSON form of the entries (with their canonical bytes preserved), the seal record(s) for the tenant-day(s) the entries belong to, and the resolution of `public_key_id` to the Ed25519 public key bytes (or a pointer to the institution's published tenant-key registry). The institution SHOULD additionally publish a "consumer-side verification guide" naming the §7 procedure, the witness-mode invocation, the expected `Status: PASS-STRUCTURALLY, key-bound verification skipped` output, and the meaning of any failure-reason string the consumer's expert may encounter. The reference verifier ships under Apache 2.0 and the consumer's expert obtains it from the project repository; the institution does not gate access to the verifier binary. This closes the institution-side veto on independent verification without compromising the IKM's confidentiality.

### CQ-3. AI Act Article 86 right to explanation — chain attribute mapping for the consumer's lawyer

**Question.** EU AI Act Article 86(1) gives any affected person subject to a decision taken by a deployer on the basis of the output of a high-risk AI system listed in Annex III (which includes credit-scoring under Annex III point 5(b) and most banking-AI-credit-decision systems) the right to "obtain from the deployer clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system in the decision-making procedure and the main elements of the decision taken." Article 86(3) clarifies that the right applies to decisions producing legal effects or similarly significantly affecting health, safety, or fundamental rights. The chain captures, per §4.4 and §4.4.1, attributes that are substantively the building blocks of a meaningful explanation: `gen_ai.request.model`, `gen_ai.response.model`, `gen_ai_parameters` (decoding parameters, system prompt, retrieval context, intra-run dependencies), `audit.routing.*` (provider chosen, failover reasons, circuit state), `audit.deployment.*` (A/B test, canary, multi-region drift, vendor reroute), and the institution's `audit.*` decision-class payload. But the spec doesn't speak Article 86 vocabulary. When the institution's customer-dispute desk receives an Article 86 request, what does it produce? Henrik's drop #08 EQ-1 already raised AI Act Article 12 logging-conformance — that's the institution-side compliance question. My question is the data-subject's mirror: which chain attributes deliver the "main elements of the decision taken" the consumer is entitled to receive, in a form a non-cryptographer can read?

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** Add a new informative annex (parallel to Henrik EQ-1's Annex C "AI Act Article 12 mapping") titled "Annex D: AI Act Article 86 right-to-explanation mapping (informative)" naming the chain attributes the institution surfaces to satisfy Article 86's "main elements of the decision taken" obligation. Suggested mapping: "role of the AI system in the decision-making procedure" → `audit.routing.provider_chosen`, `audit.routing.policy_version`, `audit.deployment.intent`, `audit.deployment.policy_version`, plus the institution's documented description of the role under its CC8.1 (or EU-equivalent) control description; "main elements of the decision taken" → the entry's `audit.*` decision-class payload, `gen_ai.response.model`, `gen_ai_parameters` (per the §4.4 RECOMMENDED contents), the human-reviewer entry chained as a child of the disputed decision per `customer-dispute-procedures.md` GDPR Article 22 section, and any `audit.fact_verification.*` cross-check entry per the same document. The annex is informative; the institution's customer-dispute desk uses it as the baseline content of its Article 86 response and adapts to the regulatory framework that applies.

### CQ-4. SCHUFA C-634/21 acknowledgment — automated credit scoring is itself an Article 22 decision

**Question.** SCHUFA Holding (CJEU C-634/21, 7 December 2023) held that the establishment of an automated probability value concerning a person's ability to meet payment obligations in the future is itself an Article 22(1) "decision based solely on automated processing" when third parties (banks, lenders) draw heavily on that value to take consequential decisions. The judgment fundamentally changed what counts as an Article 22 decision in EU credit-decision systems: it is not only the bank's final credit-grant or credit-deny that is an Article 22 decision, but the upstream score the bank consumed. The chain captures both — the score-producing AI call AND the bank's decision based on it — but the spec does not acknowledge the SCHUFA framing. An institution's litigation defense in a SCHUFA-aligned proceeding will rely on the chain entries; the institution's CC8.1 control description should align the chain's recorded `audit.*` decision-class payload with the SCHUFA-pertinent attribute set, and the institution's customer-dispute desk should be prepared to surface the score-producing entries (not only the bank's final-decision entry) when the data subject invokes Article 22.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** Add to `customer-dispute-procedures.md` GDPR Article 22 section a paragraph titled "SCHUFA Holding (CJEU C-634/21) — score-producing decisions in the chain": "Per SCHUFA Holding (CJEU C-634/21, 7 December 2023), an automated probability value concerning a person's ability to meet payment obligations is itself an Article 22(1) automated decision when third parties draw on the value for consequential downstream decisions. Institutions whose AI pipelines produce such scores capture the score-producing event in the chain as a chain entry of `chain_kind = "model_call"` (per spec §3); the downstream bank-side decision is a separate chain entry whose `parent_run_id` / `parent_seq` may link to the score-producing entry when the institution operates a multi-process pipeline (per spec §4.4 cross-run linkage). When a data subject invokes Article 22 on a credit denial, the institution's dispute desk surfaces both the score-producing entry AND the bank-side decision entry; the human-reviewer record per the §"GDPR Article 22 — automated decision-making and human review" section above is required for both. The institution's CC8.1 control description names the score-producing AI pipeline, the bank-side decision pipeline, and the chain attributes that record each. A score-producing AI pipeline that is operated by an upstream vendor (e.g., a credit bureau) is the upstream vendor's controller responsibility under SCHUFA Holding paragraph 49; the bank's chain captures the bank's consumption of the score, and the bank's data subject's Article 15 request to the bank produces the bank-side chain entries — the upstream score-producing entries are obtained from the upstream vendor under a separate Article 15 request to the upstream controller."

### CQ-5. Charter Article 47 effective-remedy posture — the consumer who cannot afford a cryptographic expert

**Question.** Charter of Fundamental Rights Article 47 gives every person whose rights guaranteed by EU law are violated the right to an effective remedy and to a fair hearing. The CJEU has consistently read Article 47 as imposing substantive procedural-fairness obligations on the EU and Member States, particularly in proceedings between consumers and traders (Profi Credit Polska, C-176/17; EOS KSI Slovensko, C-448/17; Banco Santander, C-105/17). Effective remedy in this context means the consumer can in fact challenge the institution's decision; an evidentiary regime that requires every consumer to retain a cryptographic expert to verify the chain artifacts the institution produces effectively forecloses the remedy for low-value disputes (a typical contested credit denial, mortgage rate, or fraud flag is well below the cost of a cryptographic expert engagement). The spec's §1.1 Daubert grounding speaks to US courtroom posture; §10.13 names the institution's evidentiary artifacts. There is no parallel Charter Article 47 / consumer-side analysis. Under EU consumer law, the institution producing technically sophisticated evidence the consumer cannot challenge is the very pattern Profi Credit Polska held the national court must guard against — the court must take steps of its own motion to ensure the evidence is intelligible to the consumer-side party, including by appointing a court-side expert at the institution's cost when warranted.

**Status:** Gap.

**Closing language proposal.** Add to the regulator-pack a new document `docs/regulator-pack/charter-art-47-effective-remedy.md` (or fold into a Camille-derived companion to Diego's litigation-support.md) covering: (a) the institution's commitment, recorded in its CC8.1 (or EU-equivalent) control description, to produce chain artifacts in a form independently verifiable by an expert without IKM access (the §7 witness-verifier path per CQ-2 above); (b) the institution's commitment to bear the cost of a court-appointed expert under each Member State's procedural code when the national court orders one in a consumer dispute, with the expert running the witness-verifier and producing an independent integrity report; (c) the institution's commitment to translate verifier output and the spec §7 step explanations into the consumer's procedural language (BCP 47 codes per spec §10.11) when the proceeding is in a non-English EU forum; (d) the institution's posture against the unfair-terms analysis under Directive 93/13/EEC of any clause in its terms of service that purports to require the consumer to bear cryptographic-expert costs in a chain dispute — such a clause is presumptively unfair under Article 3(1) and the indicative list at point 1(q) of the Annex (excluding or hindering the consumer's right to take legal action); (e) the institution's commitment to provide the verifier binary and the §7 procedure documentation to consumer-protection authorities (DGCCRF, Verbraucherzentrale, Test-Achats, BEUC, equivalents) under cooperation procedures so those bodies can run the verifier on aggregated complaints data within their own infrastructure.

### CQ-6. Unfair contract terms — institution-side disclosure clauses and Directive 93/13/EEC

**Question.** Directive 93/13/EEC on unfair terms in consumer contracts prohibits non-individually-negotiated terms that, contrary to the requirement of good faith, cause a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations to the detriment of the consumer (Article 3(1)). The Annex's indicative list includes terms excluding or hindering the consumer's right to take legal action (point 1(q)), terms unilaterally limiting the trader's commitments (point 1(b)), and terms requiring the consumer to fulfil obligations the trader does not (point 1(o)). When the institution presents the consumer with a chain-of-custody-backed evidentiary regime in its terms of service — typically as boilerplate "the institution's records of AI decisions are conclusive evidence of the decision content" or "the customer waives the right to challenge the integrity of the institution's audit logs" — does the chain spec inadvertently encourage such clauses? The spec is technically agnostic, but the institution's litigation-support document (cross-referenced from §10.13) frames the chain as authentication evidence under FRE 901(b)(9). The risk is that institution counsel translates "the chain authenticates the process" into terms-of-service language that effectively forecloses challenge: "by accepting this agreement, you acknowledge that the institution's chain records are accurate and unalterable evidence of the institution's automated decisions and waive any challenge to their integrity." Such a clause would be presumptively unfair under Article 3(1) Directive 93/13/EEC, and CJEU Aziz (C-415/11), Banco Santander (C-105/17), and Banco Bilbao Vizcaya Argentaria (C-655/20) require the national court to assess unfairness of its own motion. The spec does not warn institutions against drafting such clauses or document the consumer-side counter-position.

**Status:** Gap.

**Closing language proposal.** Add to `docs/regulator-pack/eu-unfair-terms-and-the-chain.md` (new document) a paragraph: "The chain produces tamper-evident records of automated decisions; under EU law, these records are evidence whose probative weight is for the national court to assess. A term of service that purports to render the chain's records 'conclusive evidence' or that purports to waive the consumer's right to challenge the records' integrity, accuracy, or completeness is a candidate unfair term under Directive 93/13/EEC Article 3(1) and the Annex point 1(q). Institutions deploying the chain SHOULD NOT include such clauses in consumer-facing terms; doing so is a credibility hazard before national consumer courts and exposes the institution to a finding of unfairness that voids the clause and may attract a national consumer-protection authority's enforcement attention. The chain's evidentiary value is established by the institution's lawful production of the artifacts and the §7 verifier procedure, not by contractual fiat. The institution's terms-of-service review under the institution's product-counsel governance MUST screen out such clauses before deployment in any EU consumer market." The companion regulator-pack document (`docs/regulator-pack/charter-art-47-effective-remedy.md` per CQ-5) cross-references this position.

### CQ-7. Brussels I bis cross-border consumer jurisdiction — Pattern A vs Pattern B for consumer-suit forum

**Question.** Brussels I bis Regulation (Reg 1215/2012) Article 18(1) gives the consumer the right to sue the trader either in the courts of the Member State in which the trader is domiciled or in the courts of the place where the consumer is domiciled. Article 19 limits the parties' freedom to derogate from Articles 17–18 — a forum clause in a consumer contract is essentially unenforceable against the consumer. This creates a structural posture: a French consumer suing a German bank gets jurisdiction in France; a Dutch consumer suing a Belgian fintech gets jurisdiction in the Netherlands. The chain artifacts must be admissible in any EU national court, and the institution's litigation-defense team will produce them in the consumer's forum, not the institution's home forum. Spec §10.15 Pattern B (per-region `tenant_id`) maps cleanly onto this — Henrik's drop #08 EQ-5 already noted Pattern B is the recommended posture for EU multi-jurisdiction supervision. From the consumer-side, Pattern B has an additional benefit: the consumer's local court receives chain artifacts that pertain to the consumer's local-jurisdiction tenant, and the institution's evidentiary production is naturally bounded by the relevant tenant rather than encompassing irrelevant cross-border material that triggers Schrems II / Article 44 transfer concerns. But spec §10.15 doesn't acknowledge the Brussels I bis consumer-jurisdiction angle, and the spec's `customer-dispute-procedures.md` doesn't name the institution's commitment to produce chain artifacts in the consumer's forum on the consumer's procedural timeline.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** Add to §10.15 Pattern B (extending Henrik EQ-5's EU-jurisdiction posture paragraph) a paragraph titled "Brussels I bis consumer-jurisdiction posture (informative for EU institutions)": "Brussels I bis Regulation Article 18(1) gives EU consumers the right to sue the institution in the consumer's home Member State court. Pattern B (per-region `tenant_id`) supports this jurisdiction posture by aligning each EU consumer's chain entries with a tenant_id whose seal region, IKM custody, and ledger storage are within the consumer's home Member State — the institution can produce chain artifacts in the consumer's forum without triggering Article 44 GDPR cross-border transfer questions on the chain disclosure itself. The institution's CC8.1 (or EU-equivalent) control description names the per-Member-State tenant_id allocation, the seal-region pinning per Member State, and the institution's commitment to produce chain artifacts within the procedural timeline of the consumer's home forum (typically 14–60 days under each Member State's civil procedure rules). Pattern A (single seal region) is conformant for EU institutions whose risk posture admits cross-Member-State seal aggregation, but the institution's litigation-support team must address cross-border production timeliness and Schrems-II-style transfer analysis when the consumer's home forum is in a Member State whose data did not reach the seal region in compliant form."

### CQ-8. Representative Actions Directive (2020/1828) — per-consumer extraction at scale

**Status:** Gap.

**Closing language proposal.** Add to `docs/regulator-pack/eu-representative-actions.md` (new document) covering: (a) the qualified consumer association's procedural standing under Dir 2020/1828 Article 4 and the Member-State-implementation laws; (b) the institution's chain-artifact production posture for representative actions — typically ordered by the national court under the qualified entity's representative claim, the institution produces a representative sample of chain entries (sample size and selection methodology agreed with the court), the witness-verifier output for the sample, and aggregated statistics on the pattern at issue; (c) the qualified-entity expert verification procedure (run the §7 witness-verifier across the sampled corpus, produce a cumulative integrity report, identify any negative-vector-style anomalies); (d) the cross-jurisdictional procedural harmonisation when the representative action is brought across Member States (Article 6 of the Directive on cross-border representative actions); (e) the institution's commitment that representative-action production does NOT require the consumer association to bear the cost of the verifier or the cryptographic-expert engagement — the institution provides the artifacts in witness-runnable form per CQ-2 above, and the qualified entity runs the verifier within its own infrastructure. The document is consumer-side and is co-authored with the consumer associations; the FFIEC working group's outreach (per Henrik EQ outreach) extends to the EU consumer-association network.

### CQ-9. Counterfactual evidence — what the chain does NOT capture

**Question.** Spec §1.2 epistemic scope (informative) is clear that the chain proves what the AI said and that the record was not tampered with; it does not prove the AI's statement is factually accurate, policy-compliant, or unbiased. This framing is structurally helpful to the consumer's challenge — it forecloses the institution from claiming the chain proves the decision was correct. But §1.2 leaves a gap the consumer's lawyer needs filled: the chain does not capture the counterfactual decision (what would a non-AI process, or a different AI, have decided?). In consumer-protection proceedings, counterfactual evidence is often the foundation of damages quantification: the consumer claims the AI's decision caused harm; the institution claims the same outcome would have occurred under any process. The chain does not adjudicate this. The spec doesn't say so explicitly, and an institution-side expert testifying from the chain may overreach into "the chain proves the decision was reasonable" — which it does not. The consumer-side expert needs a clean §1.2-style framing of the counterfactual non-claim.

**Status:** Nit.

**Closing language proposal.** Extend spec §1.2 (epistemic scope) by adding a fourth non-claim line: "(f) That the AI's statement reflects the decision a non-AI process or an alternative AI process would have produced. The chain captures the decision the institution's AI in fact produced; it does not capture or compare against any counterfactual decision. Counterfactual evidence — what an alternative process would have decided — is outside the chain's scope and lives in the institution's separate model-validation, fairness-audit, and customer-dispute-investigation evidence regimes. For consumer-protection proceedings where damages quantification turns on the counterfactual (e.g., a representative action alleging disparate impact), the counterfactual evidence is produced from those separate regimes, not from the chain. The chain composes alongside such evidence regimes without subsuming them."

### CQ-10. Data-subject access cost framework — Article 15(3) "first copy is free" and verifier-output cost

**Question.** GDPR Article 15(3) provides that the controller "shall provide a copy of the personal data undergoing processing. For any further copies requested by the data subject, the controller may charge a reasonable fee based on administrative costs." Recital 63 elaborates that the right of access should not adversely affect the rights or freedoms of others, including business secrets or intellectual property. CJEU FT (C-307/22, 26 October 2023) further clarified the "free first copy" rule and the reasonableness limit on subsequent copies. When a data subject requests chain entries under Article 15, what is the institution's cost framework? The spec is silent. CQ-1 above proposes that the institution disclose the captured JSON, the canonical bytes, the seal record, the public-key resolution, and the verifier-run output. The marginal cost to the institution of providing these artifacts is small (an existing ledger query plus a verifier invocation), but the institution may attempt to charge for the verifier output as an additional "service." Under FT (C-307/22), this is unlikely to be a "reasonable fee based on administrative cost" — the verifier run is automated and the marginal cost is near zero; the institution's first-copy disclosure of the verifier output is part of the Article 15(3) free-of-charge entitlement. Without spec guidance, institutions will inconsistently charge for verifier output or for canonical-bytes form, effectively gating the consumer's verification path.

**Status:** Partial.

**Closing language proposal.** Add to the §10.16 / `customer-dispute-procedures.md` data-subject-disclosure section proposed in CQ-1 a paragraph on cost: "Under GDPR Article 15(3) and CJEU FT (C-307/22), the data subject's first copy of the chain artifacts associated with their personal data — including the captured JSON form, the canonical bytes, the seal record(s), the resolved public key, and the institution's verifier-run output for the disclosed period — is provided free of charge. The institution does not charge a marginal fee for the verifier-run output as a separate service; the verifier run is automated and the marginal administrative cost is near zero. Subsequent copies (e.g., a re-disclosure six months later) may attract a reasonable fee based on the institution's actual administrative cost, NOT a fee that effectively makes verification unaffordable. The institution's CC8.1 (or EU-equivalent) control description names the cost framework, including the institution's posture on subsequent-copy fees and the threshold above which a fee is reasonable. A national consumer-protection authority (DGCCRF, Verbraucherzentrale, Test-Achats, equivalents) reviewing the institution's framework consults the institution's CC8.1 and the institution's actual subsequent-copy fee history for proportionality."

### CQ-11. The dual-algorithm transitional period — consumer-side risk during co-signing

**Question.** Spec §4.3.2 and §7 step 11 cases (a)–(e) define the dual-algorithm transitional posture in which a seal carries both an Ed25519 signature and a post-quantum signature (Dilithium per FIPS 204 or SLH-DSA per FIPS 205). The AND-security analysis (the seal is valid only if both signatures verify) is sound and consumer-friendly — a dual-algorithm seal is at-least-as-strong as the stronger constituent. But case (e) in §7 step 11 — both signatures present, one valid + one invalid — defaults under non-strict to PASS-WITH-ANOMALY. The §7 step 11 case (e) text correctly notes this is a Severe finding regardless of bracket and that examiners cite `regulator-pack/finding-language.md` row "11 (dual-algo) co-signed seal failure" (Severe MRA). From the consumer-side, the concern is that the institution's witness-verifier disclosure under CQ-2 / CQ-1 may surface a case (e) result, the consumer's expert reports it as PASS-WITH-ANOMALY (per the verifier output), and the institution's litigation defense translates that into "the chain verified" — eliding the Severe finding. The consumer-side disclosure should make case (e) unambiguous to a non-cryptographer reading the verifier output.

**Status:** Nit.

**Closing language proposal.** Extend spec §7 step 11 case (e) with a sentence: "When case (e) is reported in a verifier-output disclosure to a data subject under GDPR Article 15 or to a qualified consumer-protection entity under Directive 2020/1828 Article 7 representative-action production, the disclosure MUST include the case (e) "Severe MRA" finding-language reference verbatim (`regulator-pack/finding-language.md` row 11) so a non-cryptographer reading the disclosure understands the seal is in a Severe-finding state regardless of the PASS-WITH-ANOMALY bracket the verifier produced under non-strict. The institution's customer-dispute desk explanation accompanying the disclosure SHOULD use plain-language summary: 'Two signature algorithms cover this seal. One verifies; the other does not. This is a Severe finding our regulator treats as material; we are investigating and will report results to the relevant supervisory authority and to you under the regulatory window.' The non-strict PASS-WITH-ANOMALY bracket reflects the spec's posture that the un-broken algorithm still provides integrity assurance for downstream consumers; it does NOT diminish the finding's severity for examination, regulatory, or consumer-protection purposes."
